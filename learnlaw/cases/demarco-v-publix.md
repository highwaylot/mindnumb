# DeMarco v. Publix Super Markets, Inc., 384 So. 2d 1253 (Fla. 1980)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · employment · at-will employment · wrongful discharge · public policy
**Used in:** [`05-agency-employment/03-at-will-employment.md`](../05-agency-employment/03-at-will-employment.md), [`05-agency-employment/07-discrimination-and-retaliation.md`](../05-agency-employment/07-discrimination-and-retaliation.md), [`05-agency-employment/08-hiring-and-firing-compliance.md`](../05-agency-employment/08-hiring-and-firing-compliance.md)

## One-line takeaway

Florida does not recognize a common-law claim for wrongful discharge in violation of public policy; an at-will employee may be fired for any reason not prohibited by statute, and any change is for the Legislature.

## Facts

Louis DeMarco worked for Publix as an at-will employee. His minor son was injured in a Publix store. DeMarco, as the child's father, brought a personal injury claim against Publix on the son's behalf. Publix fired DeMarco. He alleged the firing was in retaliation for pursuing the lawsuit.

## Procedural posture

DeMarco sued Publix for wrongful discharge, arguing that firing an employee for exercising the constitutional right of access to the courts violates public policy and should be actionable even for an at-will employee. The trial court dismissed the complaint for failure to state a cause of action. The Third District Court of Appeal affirmed, *DeMarco v. Publix Super Markets, Inc.*, 360 So. 2d 134 (Fla. 3d DCA 1978), and certified the question to the Florida Supreme Court as one of great public interest.

## Issue

Does Florida recognize a cause of action for an at-will employee who is discharged for a reason that violates public policy, here retaliation for pursuing a lawsuit against the employer?

## Holding

No. The Florida Supreme Court approved the district court's decision and adhered to the rule that where the term of employment is indefinite, either party may end it at any time for any reason, and no action lies for the termination. The court said that if the rule is to change, the Legislature should change it.

## Reasoning

The Supreme Court's opinion was short. It restated the at-will rule, which Florida had followed for decades, and declined to carve a public policy exception. It noted that other states had begun to recognize such an exception but concluded that the choice involved policy judgments about employer and employee relations that belong to the Legislature. The court observed that the Legislature had already acted in specific areas, for example by prohibiting discharge in retaliation for a workers' compensation claim, § 440.205, Fla. Stat., and that this showed the Legislature knew how to create exceptions when it wished.

The Third District's opinion, which the Supreme Court adopted, had been more expansive. It acknowledged the harshness of the result, discussed the trend elsewhere, and still concluded that the courts should not create a new tort with uncertain boundaries. It also observed that the constitutional right of access to courts runs against the state, not against private employers.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent on the result. The strongest argument for DeMarco was the one most other states accepted in the 1970s and 1980s: an employer should not be able to use its economic power to punish an employee for doing something the law protects or encourages, such as filing a lawsuit, serving on a jury, refusing to commit a crime, or reporting illegal conduct. Allowing it undermines the policy behind the protected conduct. Florida's answer has been statutory rather than judicial.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Florida remains one of the most employer-friendly states on termination. An at-will employee can be fired for a bad reason or no reason, and the firing is not a tort. The limits are statutes, and there are many. The practical rule for a business is that every termination should be checked against the statutory list, not against a general sense of fairness. The common-law claim does not exist, but a termination that looks retaliatory invites a statutory claim and a charge with an agency, and juries do not like retaliation.

## Florida connection

*DeMarco* is still the law and Florida courts continue to cite it. The Legislature has responded with specific statutes. The Private Whistleblower Act, §§ 448.101 to 448.105, Fla. Stat., enacted in 1991, prohibits an employer with ten or more employees from retaliating against an employee who discloses or objects to a violation of a law, rule, or regulation, or who refuses to participate in one, with written notice requirements that courts have read differently (verify current case law on the notice requirement). The Public Whistleblower Act, § 112.3187, Fla. Stat., covers public employees. Section 440.205 bars retaliation for workers' compensation claims and supports a private claim, *Smith v. Piezo Technology & Professional Administrators*, 427 So. 2d 182 (Fla. 1983). The Florida Civil Rights Act, ch. 760, Fla. Stat., and federal laws such as Title VII, the ADA, the ADEA, and the FLSA prohibit discrimination and retaliation on specified grounds. Section 448.095 (E-Verify) and § 448.102 add further protected activity. Jury service is protected by § 40.271, Fla. Stat. (verify). Each of these has its own procedure and deadline, and the FCRA requires an administrative charge within 365 days before suit.
