# Consideration and promissory estoppel

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 2 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

"I promise" is not a contract. The law enforces bargains, not favors, and the line is consideration. This decides whether a verbal bonus promise is binding, whether a mid-project price increase can be enforced, and whether the supplier who told you to go ahead and build out a store can walk away. The backup doctrine, promissory estoppel, catches some promises that fail for lack of consideration. Florida applies it narrowly.

## The rule

**Consideration** is a bargained-for exchange. Each side gives something of legal value in exchange for the other's promise. Legal value means a promise, an act, or a forbearance that the person was not already obligated to give. Courts do not weigh adequacy. A dollar can support a million-dollar promise if it was actually bargained for and not a sham.

The usual failures:

1. **Past consideration.** A promise to pay for something already done is a gift. "Thanks for last year, I will give you a bonus" is unenforceable at common law.
2. **Pre-existing duty.** Doing what you were already legally bound to do is not consideration. A contractor who demands more money to finish the job it already agreed to finish gets no enforceable promise, unless something new is exchanged or the circumstances changed in a way the parties did not anticipate.
3. **Illusory promise.** A promise that leaves the promisor free to do nothing, such as "I will buy whatever I decide to order," is not consideration. Courts try hard to read an implied obligation into such promises. See *Wood v. Lucy* in lesson 5.
4. **Gift promises and moral obligation.** A promise motivated by affection or gratitude is not bargained for.

Modifications at common law need new consideration. The UCC drops this for goods: § 672.209(1), Fla. Stat., makes a good-faith modification binding without consideration.

**Promissory estoppel** enforces a promise that lacks consideration when: (1) the promisor made a promise that it should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance; (2) the promisee actually relied on it; (3) the reliance was reasonable and detrimental; and (4) injustice can be avoided only by enforcement. Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90. Remedies are often limited to reliance losses rather than the full expectation.

The party claiming the contract bears the burden of proving consideration. The party asserting estoppel bears the burden on each element, and in Florida the promise must be definite.

## How courts reason about it

*Hamer v. Sidway* sets the baseline. An uncle promised his nephew money if the nephew refrained from drinking, smoking, and gambling until age twenty-one. The nephew did. The estate argued the nephew benefited from clean living, so he gave nothing. The court said that was the wrong question. The nephew gave up a legal right. Forbearance is consideration whether or not it hurt him. Courts do not ask whether the deal was wise, only whether something of legal value was exchanged.

The pre-existing duty rule is where courts hesitate. It exists to stop holdups: the roofer who, with the rafters exposed and rain coming, demands double. But courts also see honest renegotiations when costs change. Restatement § 89 and many courts enforce a modification made before full performance if it is fair in light of circumstances not anticipated at contracting. The tell is whether the party seeking more had a legitimate reason or just leverage. Courts also find consideration in small additions: a new delivery date, a waiver of a claim, a longer term.

Promissory estoppel is the pressure valve. In *Hoffman v. Red Owl Stores*, Red Owl strung Hoffman along for two years, telling him to sell his bakery, buy a lot, and move his family, each time raising the capital he needed. No contract formed because essential terms were never settled. The court let him recover his reliance losses anyway. The policy is that a business should not induce costly reliance and then say "no deal." The counter-policy is that negotiations must be free. Courts resolve this by requiring a real promise, not optimism, and by asking whether reliance was reasonable given how far along the talks were.

Forbearance to sue is consideration if the claim was asserted in good faith, even if it would have lost.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on consideration, past consideration, the pre-existing duty rule, and illusory promises. Florida courts speak of "mutuality of obligation," which is the same idea: both sides must be bound or neither is.

Florida departs from some states on promissory estoppel in two ways.

- **It is recognized, but the promise must be definite.** In *W.R. Grace & Co. v. Geodata Services, Inc.*, 547 So. 2d 919 (Fla. 1989), the Florida Supreme Court adopted Restatement § 90 but held that a promise to keep a contractor busy "for years" was too indefinite to enforce. The court also required that the reliance be reasonable and that the promise be one the promisor should have expected to induce it. Vague assurances do not qualify.
- **It cannot defeat the statute of frauds.** *Tanenbaum v. Biscayne Osteopathic Hospital, Inc.*, 190 So. 2d 777 (Fla. 1966), refused to use promissory estoppel to enforce an oral multi-year employment promise that § 725.01 required to be in writing. This is a minority position. Many states let estoppel override the statute. In Florida, a promise that needs a writing is unenforceable without one, reliance or not.

Other Florida points:

- Option contracts need consideration or must fit the UCC firm-offer rule in § 672.205. A recital of "$10 and other good and valuable consideration" is usually honored if the money was actually paid or the recital is not challenged, but a sham recital can be attacked.
- Florida courts enforce modifications supported by new consideration and read a party's continued performance after a requested change as evidence of assent. Written contracts often say they can be modified only in writing. Florida courts have held such clauses can be waived by conduct (verify).

## Angles checklist

- What did each side give or promise? Was it something they were already bound to do?
- Is the promise for something already done? Then it is a gift unless restated as part of a new exchange.
- Is either promise illusory? Is there a termination right so broad it swallows the obligation?
- For a modification: was anything new exchanged, or was it a sale of goods under § 672.209?
- If consideration fails, is there a definite promise, reasonable reliance, and real detriment for an estoppel claim?
- Does the promise fall within the statute of frauds? In Florida, estoppel will not rescue it.
- Flip it: can the promisor show the "promise" was a statement of intent, a prediction, or a negotiation posture?
- Remedy: expectation under a contract, or reliance losses under estoppel?

## Drill

A Jacksonville restaurant owner tells a chef during an interview: "Come work for us. In two years, if things go well, I will make you a partner with a 20% stake." The chef quits a higher-paying job and moves from Atlanta. After eighteen months the owner sells the restaurant and the chef is let go. Does the chef have a claim?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Contract.** Is there consideration? The chef's move and work are consideration for employment, but the partnership promise is conditioned on "if things go well," which is close to illusory because it leaves the owner the judge. Even if treated as a promise, "a 20% stake" without any terms on capital, management, or what "things go well" means may be too indefinite to enforce.
- **Statute of frauds.** A promise to be performed in two years cannot be performed within one year, so § 725.01 requires a writing. There is none. Under *Tanenbaum*, promissory estoppel cannot cure this.
- **Promissory estoppel.** Even setting the statute aside, *W.R. Grace* requires a definite promise. "If things go well, I will make you a partner" is the kind of vague assurance the court rejected. The chef's reliance was real, but reliance on an indefinite, conditional promise is hard to call reasonable.
- **Likely outcome.** Weak claim. The chef is an at-will employee who was paid for work performed. The partnership promise likely fails for indefiniteness, the statute of frauds, and Florida's narrow estoppel doctrine.
- **Facts that would change it.** A signed offer letter stating the partnership terms would satisfy the statute of frauds and supply definiteness. A promise performable within a year, with specific terms, would open an estoppel claim for moving costs and lost wages. If the owner never intended to keep the promise when made, fraud in the inducement is a separate tort claim, which is not barred by the statute of frauds. See module 04, lesson 3.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A customer owes $10,000 on an invoice. The vendor agrees to accept $8,000 "in full satisfaction" if paid by Friday. The customer pays Friday. Can the vendor sue for the other $2,000?
2. A landlord tells a tenant, "Go ahead and build out the space, we will sign a five-year lease next month." The tenant spends $60,000 on buildout. The landlord then refuses to sign. Florida result?
3. A supplier agrees to raise its price mid-contract because its raw material costs doubled after a hurricane. Is the buyer's promise to pay the higher price enforceable?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Probably yes at common law, because paying less than an undisputed liquidated debt is a pre-existing duty, unless something else was given, such as early payment before the due date, which can be consideration. If the debt was disputed in good faith, the compromise is binding as an accord and satisfaction. Check § 673.3111, Fla. Stat., for checks marked "payment in full."
2. The tenant has a problem. A five-year lease requires a writing under § 725.01 and § 689.01. Florida does not let promissory estoppel override the statute of frauds under *Tanenbaum*. The tenant's best claims are unjust enrichment for the value of the improvements that benefit the landlord, and fraud if the landlord never intended to sign.
3. For goods, yes under § 672.209 if made in good faith, and a doubled cost from an unanticipated event is strong evidence of good faith. For a services contract, the common law pre-existing duty rule applies, but Restatement § 89 and many courts enforce a fair modification prompted by unanticipated circumstances.

</details>
