# Commercial leases

**Module:** 06 · **Lesson:** 1 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[FL]` `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

For most small businesses the lease is the largest contract they will ever sign and the one with the least negotiation. A five-year lease at $4,000 a month is a $240,000 obligation, usually backed by your guaranty. Florida's nonresidential statute gives the landlord strong remedies and the tenant almost nothing by default, so the lease document is the whole deal.

## The rule

Nonresidential tenancies are governed by Part I of chapter 83, §§ 83.001 to 83.251, Fla. Stat., and by the common law of contracts and property. The residential statute does not apply.

**What the statute supplies:**

- **Term by default.** A tenancy without a written lease for a definite term is a tenancy at will, terminable on notice tied to the rent period (§§ 83.01 to 83.03).
- **Double rent for holdover.** A tenant who holds over after written demand to vacate owes double the monthly rent for the holdover period (§ 83.06).
- **Landlord's lien.** A lien for rent on the tenant's property usually kept on the premises, with priority over most later liens (§ 83.08) (verify priority rules).
- **Distress for rent.** A court process to seize the tenant's property on the premises for unpaid rent (§§ 83.11 to 83.19). Rarely used, but a lever.
- **Removal of tenant.** Summary eviction when the tenant fails to pay rent after three days' written notice, fails to cure another breach after fifteen days' notice, or holds over (§§ 83.20, 83.21) (verify notice periods).
- **Rent into the registry.** A tenant who contests an eviction must pay the rent alleged due, and rent accruing during the case, into the court registry, or waive all defenses other than payment (§ 83.232). This is the most powerful tool in the chapter.
- **Re-entry on abandonment.** The landlord may retake abandoned premises; abandonment is presumed after 30 days' absence without paying rent (§ 83.05) (verify).

**What the statute does not supply.** There is no implied warranty of habitability or fitness in a commercial lease. The tenant takes the premises as is unless the lease says otherwise. There is no security deposit rule, no cap on late fees, no required disclosure beyond the radon notice of § 404.056(5), and no landlord duty to repair unless the lease imposes one. There is no duty to mitigate damages unless the landlord elects to retake possession for the tenant's account; see the Florida specifics.

**The lease.** Because the statute is thin, each of these clauses is doing real work:

1. **Premises and term.** Square footage, commencement date, renewal options and how they are exercised.
2. **Rent and CAM.** Base rent, escalations, and in a "triple net" lease the tenant's share of taxes, insurance, and common area maintenance. Look for CAM caps and an audit right.
3. **Use clause.** Narrow use clauses limit assignment and pivots.
4. **Assignment and subletting.** "Not to be unreasonably withheld" is the tenant's goal; "sole discretion" is the landlord's.
5. **Buildout and improvements.** Who builds, who pays, and whether improvements become the landlord's at the end.
6. **Repairs.** Who maintains roof, structure, and HVAC.
7. **Default and remedies.** Cure periods, acceleration of remaining rent, election of remedies.
8. **Holdover.** Often 150% or 200% of rent.
9. **Exclusivity.** The landlord's promise not to lease to a competitor in the center.
10. **SNDA and estoppel.** The tenant subordinates to the lender's mortgage; the lender agrees not to disturb a tenant in good standing after foreclosure. An estoppel certificate confirms lease status for a buyer or lender.
11. **Personal guaranty.** The owner's individual promise to pay if the LLC does not. Variants cap the amount, limit the term, or release the guarantor after a "good guy" surrender.

## How courts reason about it

Florida courts treat commercial leases as contracts between sophisticated parties and enforce them as written.

**Election of remedies on default.** When a tenant defaults and leaves, a Florida landlord may: treat the lease as terminated and retake for its own account, ending the tenant's future liability; retake for the tenant's account and relet, holding the tenant for the shortfall; or do nothing and sue for rent as it accrues. Conduct, not labels, decides which election was made. Reletting on new terms without reserving rights can be found to be a termination.

**Acceleration.** An acceleration clause is enforced if the lease contains it and the landlord has not also retaken the premises for its own account. Courts will not let a landlord both accelerate all future rent and keep the premises free to relet for its own benefit; that is a double recovery. Accelerated rent is reduced to present value and credited with rents from a replacement tenant.

**Guaranties.** Courts read guaranties strictly as to scope but enforce them firmly within it. A guaranty covers renewals and amendments only if it says so. A guaranty signed as "Manager" of the LLC can be read as the LLC's own signature, which guarantees nothing; see module 05, lesson 1.

**Consent to assignment.** Where consent "shall not be unreasonably withheld," courts ask whether the refusal rested on the assignee's finances, use, or experience, or on a desire to renegotiate rent. The latter is unreasonable. Sole-discretion clauses are generally enforced.

**Constructive eviction.** The tenant's one common-law shield. If the landlord makes the premises substantially unusable and the tenant leaves within a reasonable time, the tenant is released. Staying and withholding rent is a breach.

**Damages.** *Hadley* foreseeability governs consequential claims in both directions, which is why leases waive the landlord's liability for the tenant's lost business.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule that commercial tenants get no implied warranty of habitability. It departs from the majority on mitigation: in most states a landlord must make reasonable efforts to relet after the tenant abandons. In Florida, a landlord that does nothing and sues for rent as it accrues has no duty to mitigate; the duty arises only if the landlord retakes possession for the tenant's account. *Coast Federal Savings & Loan Ass'n v. DeLoach*, 362 So. 2d 982 (Fla. 2d DCA 1978) (verify citation). Tenants should ask for an express mitigation clause.

Other Florida points:

- **Sales tax on commercial rent** was repealed effective October 1, 2025 (verify). Leases signed before then often still contain tax language; the obligation is gone.
- **Construction liens.** Under § 713.10, the landlord's interest is not subject to liens for tenant improvements if the lease prohibits it and a notice is recorded. Contractors can still lien the leasehold.
- **Statute of frauds.** A lease for more than one year must be in a signed writing (§ 725.01). The witness requirement for leases was removed in 2020 (verify).
- **Radon disclosure** is mandatory in every lease (§ 404.056(5)).
- **Attorney fees.** Lease fee clauses are reciprocal under § 57.105(7).

## Angles checklist

- Who is the tenant: the LLC or you? Who signed the guaranty, and in what capacity?
- What does the lease say about repairs, HVAC, CAM caps, and audit rights?
- Is the use clause broad enough for the next pivot? Can you assign on a sale of the business?
- What are the default cure periods, and is there an acceleration clause?
- Flip it: constructive eviction; landlord's breach of exclusivity or repair covenant; set-off rights.
- Procedure: three-day notice for rent, fifteen-day for other breaches, registry deposit, county court, reciprocal fees, five-year limitation period on a written lease (verify).

## Drill

Marisol's bakery LLC signs a five-year lease for a shopping center space at $5,000 a month triple net, with an exclusivity clause for "bakery goods," a sole-discretion assignment clause, acceleration on default, and a personal guaranty Marisol signs as "Marisol Vega, Manager." In year two the landlord leases the next unit to a coffee shop that sells pastries. In year three the HVAC fails; the lease makes the tenant responsible for "maintenance and repair of HVAC." Marisol stops paying rent, and the landlord serves a three-day notice. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Exclusivity.** A coffee shop selling pastries likely breaches "bakery goods" exclusivity, though the landlord will argue pastries are incidental. Marisol has a contract claim for provable, foreseeable lost profits.
- **HVAC.** "Maintenance and repair" versus replacement is the classic ambiguity. Courts often hold that a maintenance clause does not shift replacement of a system that failed from age. Facts needed: the system's age and whether the lease addresses capital items.
- **Withholding rent.** No commercial tenant may withhold rent for landlord breach absent a lease provision. Stopping payment is a default. After the three-day notice, Marisol must deposit rent into the registry or lose every defense except payment (§ 83.232).
- **Acceleration.** If the landlord retakes the space and relets for its own account, it cannot also collect accelerated rent. If it sues for accelerated rent, present value and credits for reletting apply.
- **The guaranty.** Signed as "Manager," it may be read as the LLC's signature, guaranteeing nothing. The landlord will argue the title "Personal Guaranty" controls. Contested; the body of the guaranty decides.
- **Procedure.** County court eviction; registry deposit; reciprocal fees; compulsory counterclaim for the exclusivity breach.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. The lease is silent on mitigation. The tenant abandons in year two of five. The landlord locks the door and sues for the remaining rent monthly. Must it try to relet?
2. A tenant believes the landlord breached the repair clause and withholds rent. The landlord files for eviction. What must the tenant do within the answer period?
3. Does the landlord's lien under § 83.08 attach to equipment the tenant leased from a third party?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. In Florida, no, as long as it has not retaken possession for the tenant's account. The landlord may sue for rent as it accrues without mitigating. Most other states would require reasonable efforts to relet.
2. Deposit the rent alleged due into the court registry, and keep depositing rent as it comes due, or file a motion to determine the amount. Otherwise the tenant waives all defenses other than payment and the landlord gets possession (§ 83.232).
3. Generally no as to property the tenant does not own (verify § 83.08's priority language). The equipment lessor should file a UCC-1 and ask the landlord for a lien waiver.

</details>
