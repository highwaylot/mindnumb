# MacPherson v. Buick Motor Co., 217 N.Y. 382, 111 N.E. 1050 (N.Y. 1916)

**Tags:** `[US]` · torts · negligence · products liability · privity · duty
**Used in:** [`04-torts/01-negligence.md`](../04-torts/01-negligence.md), [`04-torts/06-economic-loss-rule.md`](../04-torts/06-economic-loss-rule.md), [`03-contracts/08-ucc-sales-of-goods.md`](../03-contracts/08-ucc-sales-of-goods.md)

## One-line takeaway

A manufacturer owes a duty of care to the foreseeable users of its product, not just to the party it sold to, when the product is reasonably certain to endanger life if negligently made.

## Facts

Buick Motor Company built an automobile and sold it to a retail dealer. The dealer sold it to Donald MacPherson. While he was driving at about eight miles an hour, one of the wooden wheels collapsed because its spokes were made of defective wood. MacPherson was thrown out and injured. Buick had not made the wheel. It bought wheels from another manufacturer. The evidence showed that a reasonable inspection would have revealed the defect and that Buick did not inspect.

## Procedural posture

MacPherson sued Buick for negligence. Buick argued it owed him no duty because it had no contract with him; its only contract was with the dealer. A jury found for MacPherson. The Appellate Division affirmed. Buick appealed to the New York Court of Appeals, which affirmed.

## Issue

Does a manufacturer owe a duty of care to a remote purchaser with whom it has no contract, when the product is of a kind that is dangerous if negligently made?

## Holding

Yes. Judge Cardozo, for the majority, held that if the nature of a thing is such that it is reasonably certain to place life and limb in peril when negligently made, it is a thing of danger. If the manufacturer knows that it will be used by persons other than the purchaser, and used without new tests, then regardless of contract the manufacturer is under a duty to make it carefully. Knowledge of the probable danger and knowledge that the product will be used by others, not just the immediate buyer, are the two conditions. Both were met.

## Reasoning

The old rule came from *Winterbottom v. Wright*, 152 Eng. Rep. 402 (Ex. 1842): no duty without privity of contract. New York had carved an exception in *Thomas v. Winchester*, 6 N.Y. 397 (1852), for things "imminently dangerous" in themselves, like mislabeled poison. Later cases extended the exception to a defective scaffold and an exploding coffee urn. Cardozo read that line of cases as having already abandoned the privity rule for a large class of products. He then stated the principle directly.

The test is not whether the product is inherently dangerous, as poison or explosives are. It is whether the product is dangerous if defective, and whether the defendant should expect it to reach users without further inspection. An automobile is not dangerous when sound. It is dangerous at speed with a bad wheel. Buick knew the car would be sold to a driver, that the dealer would not test the wheels, and that a driver and passengers would trust the car.

Cardozo dealt with the fact that Buick did not make the wheel. A manufacturer that assembles components into a finished product is responsible for inspecting them.

The opinion also closed the door on the argument that the duty came from contract. "We have put aside the notion that the duty to safeguard life and limb, when the consequences of negligence may be foreseen, grows out of contract and nothing else." The duty comes from the law and from the foreseeability of harm.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Chief Judge Willard Bartlett dissented. He read *Thomas v. Winchester* as limited to articles inherently dangerous, such as poison, and would not extend it to a car, which is dangerous only if it fails. He warned that the majority's rule had no stopping point and would expose manufacturers to liability to anyone who touched their goods. He also noted that the English courts had kept the privity requirement.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

If you make, assemble, import, or sell products, the people who can sue you for a defect are the people who use the product, not just your customer. A disclaimer in your contract with a distributor does not reach the end user. The case also explains why the economic loss rule matters: *MacPherson* opened the door to tort claims by non-parties for personal injury and property damage, and courts later had to decide whether that door also opened for pure financial loss. In Florida, after *Tiara Condominium*, it does not, in the products context.

## Florida connection

Florida follows this rule and has gone further. Florida recognizes negligence claims against manufacturers without privity and adopted strict liability in tort for defective products in *West v. Caterpillar Tractor Co.*, 336 So. 2d 80 (Fla. 1976), following Restatement (Second) of Torts § 402A. A plaintiff injured by a defective product in Florida can sue the manufacturer, and often the seller, in negligence, strict liability, and warranty. Products liability claims are subject to a statute of repose, generally 12 years from delivery to the first purchaser, with exceptions. § 95.031(2)(b), Fla. Stat. (verify current text). The economic loss rule still bars tort recovery for a product that damages only itself. *Tiara Condominium Ass'n v. Marsh & McLennan Cos.*, 110 So. 3d 399 (Fla. 2013).

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Does the outcome change? Suppose the dealer had inspected every wheel and missed the defect. Does Buick's duty to MacPherson change? Does its liability?
2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What facts about the wheel supplier or the inspection process would have helped Buick?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? You assemble custom e-bikes from parts you buy online and sell them to a local shop. A buyer from the shop is injured when a frame cracks. Who can sue whom, and on what theories?
