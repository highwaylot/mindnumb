# White v. Mederi Caretenders Visiting Services of Southeast Florida, LLC, 226 So. 3d 774 (Fla. 2017)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · employment · non-competes · restrictive covenants · legitimate business interest · § 542.335
**Used in:** [`05-agency-employment/06-non-competes-and-restrictive-covenants.md`](../05-agency-employment/06-non-competes-and-restrictive-covenants.md), [`06-property-ip/04-trade-secrets.md`](../06-property-ip/04-trade-secrets.md)

## One-line takeaway

The list of legitimate business interests in § 542.335(1)(b) is not exhaustive; relationships with referral sources can be a legitimate business interest that supports enforcing a non-compete, depending on the facts.

## Facts

The decision resolved two cases. In the first, Carla White worked as a marketing representative for Mederi Caretenders, a home health agency. Her job was to build relationships with physicians, hospitals, and other medical providers who refer patients to home health agencies. She signed an agreement not to compete within a defined area for a year after leaving. She left and went to work for a competing agency in the same role. In the second case, Americare Home Therapy sued Caryn Hiles, a marketing representative who had done the same kind of work and left for a competitor under a similar covenant.

Home health agencies cannot solicit patients directly in any practical way. Patients come almost entirely through referrals from physicians and facilities. The marketing representatives' entire job was cultivating those sources.

## Procedural posture

In *White*, the trial court refused to enforce the covenant, finding that referral sources are not a legitimate business interest under § 542.335 because the statute does not list them and the referral sources are not the agency's customers. The Fourth District reversed. In *Hiles*, the trial court enforced the covenant and the Fifth District reversed, holding referral sources are not a protectable interest. The Florida Supreme Court took the cases to resolve the conflict.

## Issue

Can a home health agency's relationships with its referral sources be a "legitimate business interest" under § 542.335(1)(b), Fla. Stat., even though the statute's list does not mention them?

## Holding

Yes. The court held that the statutory list is non-exhaustive, that referral source relationships can qualify as a legitimate business interest, and that whether they do in a given case is a fact question for the trial court. The court approved the Fourth District's decision and quashed the Fifth District's.

## Reasoning

Justice Lewis began with the text. Section 542.335(1)(b) says the term "legitimate business interest" "includes, but is not limited to" five categories: trade secrets; valuable confidential business information; substantial relationships with specific prospective or existing customers, patients, or clients; customer goodwill associated with a trade name, geographic location, or trade area; and extraordinary or specialized training. "Includes, but is not limited to" means the list is illustrative. A court may recognize an unlisted interest.

The court then explained what makes an interest legitimate. The statute protects interests that have value apart from the employee's own skill and effort, interests the employer invested in and that an employee could unfairly appropriate by walking across the street. It does not protect an employer from ordinary competition. The court drew on the statute's instruction that it be construed in favor of providing reasonable protection to all legitimate business interests, § 542.335(1)(h), and on the common-law history of protecting customer relationships and goodwill.

Applied to home health, referral sources are the practical equivalent of customers. The agency invested in those relationships. The marketing representative's value to a competitor lay in carrying them over. The court cautioned that the result is not automatic. The employer must prove, with evidence, that the relationships are substantial, that it invested in them, and that the employee could exploit them.

## Dissent or counterpoints

The opinion was unanimous on the result (verify). The strongest argument for the employees was the Fifth District's: the statute protects relationships with "customers, patients, or clients," referral sources are none of those, and reading the catch-all broadly lets employers restrain any employee who has met anyone useful. The court answered that the statute's own words make the list open, but it accepted the concern by making the inquiry fact-specific and putting the burden on the employer.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

If your business depends on relationships that are not strictly customers, such as referral partners, brokers, suppliers with limited capacity, or platform contacts, a well-drafted covenant can protect them. The drafting lesson is to name the interest in the agreement and to keep proof of the investment: who built the relationship, with what resources, over what time. The litigation lesson is that § 542.335 requires the employer to plead and prove a legitimate business interest first; a covenant that cannot point to one is unenforceable no matter how reasonable its length.

## Florida connection

This is Florida law. Section 542.335 governs every restrictive covenant signed after July 1, 1996. The employer must prove a legitimate business interest and that the restraint is reasonably necessary to protect it. A six-month or shorter post-employment restraint is presumed reasonable and more than two years is presumed unreasonable (verify). The statute bars courts from considering the individual hardship to the employee and presumes irreparable injury from violation of an enforceable covenant. The 2025 CHOICE Act, §§ 542.41 to 542.45, Fla. Stat. (verify numbering), adds a separate regime for covered employees earning more than twice the county's annual mean wage, with covenants up to four years and mandatory preliminary injunctions. *White* continues to govern the threshold question under § 542.335.

2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What evidence would defeat the agency's claim that its referral relationships were a protectable interest?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? Your sales manager's main value is relationships with three distributors who control access to your market. Draft, in a sentence, the interest you would name in the covenant and the evidence you would keep.
