# Federal reporting and miscellaneous compliance

**Module:** 07 · **Lesson:** 7 · **Time:** ~40 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

A handful of federal obligations attach to almost every business: a tax ID, reporting payments to contractors, verifying work authorization, a safe workplace, and accessibility. None is hard. All are enforced by agencies that do not care whether you knew. This lesson puts them on one checklist and notes the one that changed most often, beneficial ownership reporting.

## The rule

**Employer Identification Number.** An LLC with employees, more than one member, or an election to be taxed as a corporation needs an EIN (Form SS-4, free from the IRS). Banks require one to open an entity account.

**Corporate Transparency Act and beneficial ownership (BOI).** The 2021 statute required most small entities to report their beneficial owners to FinCEN starting January 1, 2024. After litigation, FinCEN issued an interim final rule in March 2025 (verify) exempting all entities formed in the United States and all U.S. persons from reporting. Only foreign entities registered to do business in the United States still report (verify). The statute remains on the books. Check FinCEN's current position before concluding nothing is due.

**Information returns.** A business must file Form 1099-NEC for payments to a non-employee for services in a trade or business at or above the threshold: $600 (verify) through 2025, rising to $2,000 (verify) for payments made after December 31, 2025, with inflation indexing after. Collect a Form W-9 before paying, and withhold 24% (verify) backup withholding if the payee refuses to give a taxpayer ID. Payment card and third-party network payments are reported by the processor on Form 1099-K, with the $20,000 and 200-transaction (verify) threshold restored by the 2025 federal tax act.

**New hires and work authorization.** Form I-9 for every employee within three business days (verify) of the start date; retain for three years after hire or one year after termination, whichever is later. Florida requires private employers with 25 (verify) or more employees to use E-Verify (§ 448.095). Report new hires, including independent contractors paid $600 (verify) or more, to the Florida New Hire Reporting Center within 20 days (verify) (§ 409.2576).

**OSHA.** The general duty clause, 29 U.S.C. § 654(a)(1), requires a workplace free from recognized hazards likely to cause death or serious harm, plus compliance with specific standards. Florida has no state plan; federal OSHA covers private employers. Employers with more than 10 (verify) employees keep injury logs (Form 300) unless in a low-hazard industry. Report a fatality within 8 hours (verify) and an inpatient hospitalization, amputation, or eye loss within 24 hours (verify). Required posters: OSHA, FLSA, EEOC, Florida minimum wage, reemployment assistance, workers' compensation.

**ADA Title III (42 U.S.C. § 12182).** A place of public accommodation may not discriminate on the basis of disability and must remove architectural barriers where readily achievable. Remedies for private plaintiffs are injunctive relief and attorney fees, not damages; the Department of Justice can seek civil penalties.

**Websites.** The DOJ's position is that websites of public accommodations must be accessible, and WCAG 2.1 AA (verify version) is the de facto standard. The Eleventh Circuit in *Gil v. Winn-Dixie Stores, Inc.*, 993 F.3d 1266 (11th Cir. 2021), held a website is not itself a place of public accommodation, but the opinion was vacated as moot, 21 F.4th 775 (11th Cir. 2021) (verify), leaving no binding circuit rule. Suits continue.

**Export controls and sanctions.** Shipping software, technology, or goods abroad, or dealing with foreign parties, can implicate the Export Administration Regulations and OFAC sanctions; screen counterparties against the restricted party lists. One line here; a specialist if it applies.

**Burden.** The agency proves the violation. The business proves exemptions and good-faith compliance.

## How courts reason about it

- **Strict compliance for paperwork.** I-9, 1099, and new hire reporting are penalized per form, and courts and agencies rarely accept ignorance. The scale is small per item and large in aggregate.
- **Who is the employer.** OSHA, the ADA, and payroll obligations attach to the "employer" or the "operator." Courts use control tests. A business that controls a job site can be cited for a subcontractor's hazard.
- **"Readily achievable."** ADA barrier removal is judged by cost against the business's resources. Courts look at the nature of the barrier, the cost, and the size and finances of the entity and its parent. Old buildings need not be rebuilt, but the cheap things are required: ramps, signage, door hardware.
- **Website standing.** Courts in the Eleventh Circuit have both allowed and dismissed website suits depending on whether the site has a nexus to a physical location and whether the plaintiff intends to return. The variance is why these cases settle.
- **BOI.** The CTA litigation turned on Congress's commerce power; district courts split and the Supreme Court stayed one injunction before FinCEN mooted the issue by rule. Compliance regimes change by agency rule as fast as by statute; the source to check is the agency.
- **OSHA citations.** Contested citations go to the Occupational Safety and Health Review Commission. The Secretary must prove the standard applied, was violated, employees were exposed, and the employer knew or could have known with reasonable diligence. Unforeseeable employee misconduct is a defense if the employer had and enforced a rule.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows federal law on all of these; it has no state OSHA plan and no state accessibility statute beyond the building code, which incorporates accessibility requirements through the Florida Accessibility Code (ch. 553, part II).
- Florida's E-Verify mandate for employers of 25 or more under § 448.095 goes beyond federal law, which requires E-Verify only for certain federal contractors.
- Florida's new hire reporting covers independent contractors, which many states do not (verify).
- Florida entities are exempt from BOI reporting under the March 2025 rule as domestic companies, like entities in every other state.

## Angles checklist

- EIN obtained and bank account in the entity's name?
- W-9 on file for every vendor before the first payment; 1099-NEC issued by January 31?
- I-9 for every employee within three days, stored separately, E-Verify if 25 or more?
- New hire reports filed within 20 days, including contractors?
- OSHA: hazard assessment done, logs kept if required, posters up, reporting procedure for serious injuries?
- ADA: physical barriers surveyed, readily achievable fixes done, website audited against WCAG, accessibility statement posted?
- BOI: confirmed current FinCEN rule this year?
- Export: any foreign customers or shipments screened?
- Flip it: what would an inspector find tomorrow?

## Drill

Palmetto Print Co., an LLC with 28 employees, runs a print shop open to walk-in customers in a 1962 building with two steps at the entrance. It pays three freelance designers $15,000 a year each, never collected W-9s, and did not issue 1099s. It hired six people this year and completed I-9s for four. A press operator lost a fingertip last month; the owner drove him to urgent care and did not report it. The website's order form cannot be used with a screen reader. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **1099s.** Three designers at $15,000 exceed any threshold. Missing W-9s and 1099-NECs mean per-form penalties and potential backup withholding liability for amounts that should have been withheld.
- **I-9 and E-Verify.** Two employees with no I-9 is a per-form civil penalty. With 28 employees, Florida's § 448.095 requires E-Verify for all new hires, with escalating state penalties and possible license suspension for repeat violations (verify).
- **OSHA.** An amputation, including a fingertip, must be reported within 24 hours. Failure is a citation. The underlying machine guarding is likely a separate standard violation. With 28 employees in manufacturing, Form 300 logs are required.
- **ADA, building.** Two steps at the public entrance is an architectural barrier. A ramp or lift is often readily achievable for a business of this size. Serial plaintiffs target exactly this.
- **ADA, website.** An order form unusable by screen reader is the standard website claim. In the Eleventh Circuit the nexus to the physical shop supports the claim.
- **New hire reporting.** Six hires and three contractors should have been reported within 20 days.
- **Red herring.** BOI reporting. As a domestic LLC, Palmetto is exempt under the March 2025 rule, assuming it still stands.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the designers were corporations, no 1099 would be due. If the building entrance change would require structural work costing more than the business can bear, "readily achievable" shifts to alternative service methods.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A Florida LLC formed in 2023 filed a BOI report in 2024. Does it need to update it when a member changes in 2026?
2. Who is responsible for an OSHA violation created by a subcontractor's crew on a job site the LLC controls?
3. Why do ADA Title III cases against small businesses settle so often?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Under the March 2025 interim final rule, domestic companies are exempt from reporting and updating. Verify FinCEN's current rule, because the position has changed more than once and the statute still exists.
2. Potentially the LLC, as the controlling employer on a multi-employer worksite, along with the subcontractor as the exposing employer. Control over the site and ability to correct the hazard drive the citation.
3. Because the plaintiff's remedy is an injunction plus attorney fees, the barrier is usually real, and the fee exposure grows with every month of litigation. Fixing the barrier and paying a modest fee is cheaper than defending.

</details>
