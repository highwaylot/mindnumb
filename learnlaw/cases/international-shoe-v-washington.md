# International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (U.S. Supreme Court 1945)

**Tags:** `[US]` · personal jurisdiction · due process · minimum contacts · long-arm
**Used in:** [01-foundations/02-jurisdiction-and-venue.md](../01-foundations/02-jurisdiction-and-venue.md), [08-disputes-procedure/08-when-you-are-sued.md](../08-disputes-procedure/08-when-you-are-sued.md)

## One-line takeaway

A state court may exercise jurisdiction over an out-of-state defendant that has minimum contacts with the state such that the suit does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.

## Facts

International Shoe was a Delaware corporation headquartered in St. Louis. It had no office, inventory, or property in Washington. It did employ eleven to thirteen salesmen who lived in Washington, solicited orders there under the direction of St. Louis managers, and showed samples in rented hotel rooms and temporary display spaces. Orders went to St. Louis for acceptance and goods were shipped from outside the state. Commissions on Washington sales exceeded $31,000 a year.

Washington's unemployment compensation statute required employers to contribute to a state fund. The state served notice of assessment on one of the salesmen in Washington and mailed a copy to the St. Louis office.

## Procedural posture

The company appeared specially to contest jurisdiction and lost before the state agency and in the Washington courts. The Washington Supreme Court held the company was doing business in the state and that service was proper. The company appealed to the U.S. Supreme Court, arguing that subjecting it to suit in Washington violated due process and that the salesmen's activities were not "doing business" within the state.

## Issue

Does due process permit Washington to subject a foreign corporation to suit for contributions when the corporation's only activities in the state are regular solicitation of orders by resident salesmen?

## Holding

Yes. Due process requires only that the defendant "have certain minimum contacts with [the forum] such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend 'traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice.'" The company's activities in Washington were systematic and continuous, not isolated, and the obligation sued on arose out of those very activities. Service on a salesman plus mail to the home office gave reasonable notice. The judgment was affirmed.

## Reasoning

Chief Justice Stone abandoned the older fiction that a corporation was "present" in a state and could be sued only where it was physically found. A corporation acts only through agents. The question is whether the quality and nature of its activities in the state make it reasonable to require it to defend there.

The opinion sketched a sliding scale that later cases turned into doctrine. Continuous and systematic activities that give rise to the claim support jurisdiction, as here. A single or occasional act may support jurisdiction over claims arising from that act if its nature makes that fair. Casual or isolated acts unrelated to the claim do not. The Court also noted that a corporation that enjoys the benefits and protection of a state's laws by doing business there takes on a corresponding obligation to answer in that state's courts.

Applied to the facts, the salesmen's activity was regular, ongoing, and produced a large volume of interstate business. The tax was assessed on the very activity that constituted the contacts. Jurisdiction was fair.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Justice Black concurred in the result but objected to the method. He thought the state's power to tax and regulate activity within its borders was clear and did not depend on a vague judicial test of "fair play." He worried that the new formula handed federal courts an open-ended power to override state authority. Later history proved him partly right. "Minimum contacts" has generated decades of litigation over exactly where the line falls.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Every time your business sells into another state, hires a representative there, or signs a contract with a company based there, you add a contact. Enough contacts and you can be sued there. This brief is the starting point for any question of "can they really sue me in Texas." The answer runs in two steps: does the forum state's long-arm statute reach the conduct, and does the Constitution permit it. *Burger King v. Rudzewicz*, also in this course, applies the second step to a contract.

Forum selection clauses exist in part to take this uncertainty off the table. A clause consenting to one forum is the cheapest way to control where you will fight.

## Florida connection

Florida follows this rule because it must; it is federal constitutional law binding on every state court. Florida's long-arm statute is § 48.193, Fla. Stat. Florida courts apply a two-step test set out in *Venetian Salami Co. v. Parthenais*, 554 So. 2d 499 (Fla. 1989): first, whether the complaint alleges facts bringing the defendant within § 48.193; second, whether the defendant has sufficient minimum contacts to satisfy due process. Florida also has a general jurisdiction provision in § 48.193(2) for defendants engaged in "substantial and not isolated activity" in Florida. Since the U.S. Supreme Court's *Daimler* decision in 2014, general jurisdiction over a company is normally limited to where it is incorporated or has its principal place of business, so the specific jurisdiction analysis under § 48.193(1) does most of the work.
