# Non-competes and restrictive covenants

**Module:** 05 · **Lesson:** 6 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Florida is the most employer-friendly non-compete state in the country, and in 2025 it became more so. That cuts both ways. The covenant your salesperson signed is probably enforceable if drafted to the statute. The covenant your new hire signed with a competitor is probably enforceable too, and reaches you if you induce the breach. In Florida the statute is nearly the whole law, so this lesson reads it closely.

## The rule

Restrictive covenants in Florida are governed by § 542.335, Fla. Stat. "Restrictive covenant" includes non-competes, non-solicitation of customers or employees, and confidentiality agreements. The statute sets the elements, the presumptions, and the rules of construction.

**Elements the party seeking enforcement must plead and prove:**

1. **A writing** signed by the person against whom enforcement is sought (§ 542.335(1)(a)).
2. **One or more legitimate business interests** justifying the restriction (§ 542.335(1)(b)). The statute lists: trade secrets; valuable confidential business or professional information that does not qualify as a trade secret; substantial relationships with specific prospective or existing customers, patients, or clients; customer, patient, or client goodwill associated with an ongoing business, a trade name or mark, a specific geographic location, or a specific marketing or trade area; and extraordinary or specialized training. The list is not exhaustive. A covenant not supported by a legitimate business interest is unlawful and void.
3. **The restraint is reasonably necessary** to protect that interest (§ 542.335(1)(c)). Once the enforcing party shows this on its face, the burden shifts to the other side to prove the restraint is overbroad, overlong, or otherwise not reasonably necessary.

**Presumptions of reasonableness in time** (§ 542.335(1)(d), (e)) (verify):

| Covenant against | Presumed reasonable | Presumed unreasonable |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Former employee, agent, or independent contractor | 6 months or less | More than 2 years |
| Former distributor, dealer, franchisee, or licensee | 1 year or less | More than 3 years |
| Seller of a business or its goodwill | 3 years or less | More than 7 years |
| Protecting trade secrets | 5 years or less | More than 10 years |

**Rules of construction that favor enforcement:**

- A court **shall not** consider individualized economic or other hardship to the person against whom enforcement is sought (§ 542.335(1)(g)1).
- A court **shall** construe the covenant in favor of reasonable protection of all legitimate business interests, and **shall not** apply any rule of construction against the drafter (§ 542.335(1)(h)).
- A court **shall not** refuse enforcement on public policy grounds unless the policy is specifically articulated and outweighs the need for enforcement (§ 542.335(1)(i)).
- If a restraint is overbroad, the court **shall modify** it and grant only the relief reasonably necessary. Florida requires blue-penciling rather than striking the covenant (§ 542.335(1)(c)).
- Violation of an enforceable covenant creates a **presumption of irreparable injury**, the key element for an injunction (§ 542.335(1)(j)).
- The court **may** award attorney fees to the prevailing party (§ 542.335(1)(k)).
- A covenant is enforceable by an assignee or successor only if the agreement expressly authorizes it (§ 542.335(1)(f)).

**The 2025 CHOICE Act** (Contracts Honoring Opportunity, Investment, Confidentiality, and Economic Growth), effective July 1, 2025, codified at §§ 542.41 to 542.45 (verify numbering), adds a stronger regime for "covered employees": employees or contractors who earn, or are reasonably expected to earn, more than twice the annual mean wage of the Florida county where the employer's principal place of business is located, or where the employee resides if the employer is out of state (verify). Health care practitioners are excluded. For covered employees:

- A **covered non-compete** may run up to 4 years after employment ends, if the employee had at least 7 days to review it, received written notice of the right to consult counsel, and acknowledged in writing receipt of confidential information or customer relationships.
- A **covered garden leave agreement** may require up to 4 years' notice, during which the employee stays employed and paid, and after the first 90 days need not work.
- A court **must** preliminarily enjoin a breach, and may dissolve or modify the injunction only on clear and convincing evidence that the employee will not use confidential information or serve the former employer's customers, or that the employer failed to pay.

Agreements outside the CHOICE Act stay under § 542.335.

## How courts reason about it

**Legitimate business interest is the real fight.** Because the statute removes hardship, public policy, and construction against the drafter, the defense concentrates on element two. The question is whether the employer is protecting something it built, or just suppressing competition. In *White v. Mederi Caretenders*, the Florida Supreme Court held that referral sources for a home health agency can be a legitimate business interest even though they are not on the statutory list, reading "includes" as illustrative. The question since is whether the interest is an asset the business built and would lose to a departing worker, rather than general know-how the worker brought.

**Specific customers, substantial relationships.** The most litigated phrase. A relationship is substantial if the employee had meaningful contact and the business can identify the customer. Customers the employee brought from a prior job cut the other way.

**Reasonably necessary, and the blue pencil.** Courts rarely void a covenant for overbreadth. They shorten it, shrink the territory, or narrow the activity. A statewide restriction on a salesperson who covered three counties becomes a three-county restriction. The cost of overreaching is low, which is why Florida covenants are broad.

**Injunctions.** *Proudfoot Consulting Co. v. Gordon* shows a federal court enforcing a six-month covenant with an injunction and damages, while refusing speculative lost profits. Because irreparable injury is presumed, the preliminary injunction fight is about the legitimate business interest, not harm.

**Tortious interference by the new employer.** A competitor who hires the employee knowing of the covenant can be sued for inducing the breach and added to the injunction. See *Ethan Allen* for the limits of that tort.

**The federal picture.** The FTC's 2024 rule banning most non-competes was set aside by a federal district court in Texas in August 2024, and in 2025 the FTC abandoned its appeal and moved to case-by-case enforcement (verify). There is no federal ban. Florida law governs.

## Florida specifics

Florida departs from the majority on nearly every point above. Most states weigh employee hardship, construe against the drafter, and blue-pencil sparingly if at all. Several ban non-competes for low-wage workers or entirely. Florida does none of that.

- **Physicians.** § 542.336 voids restrictive covenants with physicians in a specialty where one entity employs or contracts with all the specialists in a county (verify).
- **Lighter alternatives.** A customer non-solicitation covenant, an employee non-solicitation covenant, and a confidentiality agreement are each restrictive covenants under the statute, each need a legitimate business interest, and each is easier to justify than a full non-compete. Trade secrets are protected by statute without any contract (module 06, lesson 4).

## Angles checklist

- Is there a signed writing? Signed by the person to be bound?
- What interest is being protected, specifically? Which customers, what information, what training?
- Duration, territory, and scope of activity. Where do they fall on the presumption table?
- Is the person a covered employee under the CHOICE Act? Was the 7-day review and counsel notice given?
- Did the employee bring the customers, or did the employer?
- Was the employer in breach first, for example unpaid commissions? Prior breach is a defense the statute preserves.
- Flip it: tortious interference against the new employer; declaratory judgment by the employee; fee exposure both ways.
- Remedy: preliminary injunction, damages, fees. Forum and choice-of-law clauses.

## Drill

Gulf Marine Supply, LLC hired Priya as an outside sales rep in Pinellas County in 2023 at $70,000 plus commission. She signed a covenant barring her for two years from working for any marine supplier anywhere in Florida and from soliciting any Gulf Marine customer. In 2026 she leaves for a competitor in Jacksonville and emails her 40 former accounts, 15 of which she had brought with her from a previous job. Gulf Marine owes her $9,000 in commissions. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Legitimate business interest.** Substantial relationships with the 25 accounts Gulf Marine developed through Priya, and any confidential pricing. The 15 accounts she brought are weak: relationships that were hers before, which courts often exclude.
- **Reasonableness and blue pencil.** Two years is at the edge of the presumption table for an employee. Statewide scope for a Pinellas rep is overbroad. A court would likely narrow to the Tampa Bay area or to the specific accounts and perhaps shorten the term, rather than void the covenant. The non-solicitation of the 25 accounts is the strongest piece.
- **CHOICE Act.** At $70,000 plus commission she is probably below twice the county mean wage, so the Act does not apply and the 4-year regime is unavailable (verify county figure).
- **Prior breach.** The $9,000 in unpaid commissions is a defense: a party in material breach cannot enforce. Materiality is the fact question.
- **New employer.** The Jacksonville competitor faces a tortious interference claim if it knew of the covenant and the solicitation.
- **Remedies.** Injunction against soliciting the 25 accounts; lost profits need proof, per *Proudfoot*; fees to the prevailing party.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A Florida court finds a two-year statewide non-compete overbroad. What does it do?
2. Can an employee argue that enforcing the covenant will leave her unable to support her family?
3. Does a confidentiality agreement need a legitimate business interest?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. It modifies the covenant to what is reasonably necessary and enforces the narrowed version. § 542.335(1)(c) requires blue-penciling, not striking.
2. Not in Florida. § 542.335(1)(g)1 forbids the court from considering individualized economic hardship. Most other states allow the argument.
3. Yes. A confidentiality agreement is a restrictive covenant under § 542.335 and must be supported by a legitimate business interest, usually trade secrets or valuable confidential information. The bar is low but the information must actually be confidential.

</details>
