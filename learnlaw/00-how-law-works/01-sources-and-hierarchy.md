# Sources of law and the hierarchy of authority

**Module:** 00 · **Lesson:** 1 · **Time:** ~40 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Every legal question starts with "which law, and whose word is final." A Florida LLC dispute is governed by a Florida statute, interpreted by Florida courts, and a federal court hearing it must follow them. A wage claim might be federal, state, or both. Knowing the map tells you where to look, which source wins when two conflict, and why a case from another state is interesting but not binding.

## The rule

There are four kinds of law, and they stack.

1. **Constitutions.** The U.S. Constitution sits above everything. The Florida Constitution sits above all Florida law. A statute that conflicts with the applicable constitution is void.
2. **Statutes.** Laws passed by a legislature. Congress makes the United States Code. The Florida Legislature makes the Florida Statutes. Counties and cities make ordinances, which sit below state statutes.
3. **Regulations.** Rules written by agencies under authority a statute gave them. Federal rules live in the Code of Federal Regulations. Florida rules live in the Florida Administrative Code. A regulation that goes beyond its statute is invalid.
4. **Case law.** Decisions of courts. Case law does two jobs. It interprets constitutions, statutes, and regulations. And in areas the legislature has not covered, it *is* the law. This second kind is called **common law**. Most of contracts, torts, and agency started as common law and much of it still is.

Two systems run side by side.

- **Federal law** applies everywhere but only on subjects the Constitution gives Congress. Business examples: bankruptcy, patents and copyright, federal taxes, securities, interstate commerce, federal employment law like the Fair Labor Standards Act and Title VII.
- **State law** covers everything else, which for a business is most things: entity formation, contracts, torts, property, most employment rules, consumer protection, licensing.

When they genuinely conflict, federal law wins under the Supremacy Clause. This is called **preemption**. But most of the time they do not conflict; they both apply, and the stricter one controls. Florida's minimum wage is higher than the federal one, so a Florida employer pays Florida's rate.

### Binding versus persuasive authority

A court must follow **binding** (mandatory) authority: the constitution and statutes that apply, and the decisions of courts above it in its own system. Everything else is **persuasive**. A court may find it convincing but need not follow it.

For a Florida trial court:

- Florida Supreme Court decisions: binding.
- Decisions of the District Court of Appeal for its district: binding.
- Decisions of other DCAs: binding too, unless its own DCA has decided the question differently. This is a Florida rule from *Pardo v. State*, 596 So. 2d 665 (Fla. 1992). In the absence of inter-district conflict, a DCA decision binds all Florida trial courts.
- U.S. Supreme Court decisions on federal law: binding.
- Eleventh Circuit decisions on *federal* law: persuasive to a Florida state court, not binding. Florida courts follow the U.S. Supreme Court on federal questions, not the federal circuit courts.
- Other states' decisions, treatises, law review articles, the Restatements: persuasive.

For a federal court in Florida deciding a state-law claim:

- Florida Supreme Court decisions: binding on what Florida law is.
- Florida DCA decisions: binding unless there is persuasive indication the Florida Supreme Court would decide differently.
- Eleventh Circuit decisions: binding.

This last point comes from *Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins*, 304 U.S. 64 (1938). A federal court sitting in diversity applies state substantive law. It does not invent a "federal common law" of contracts.

### Published versus unpublished

In the federal system, an unpublished opinion is not binding precedent. In Florida, DCA opinions are published, but a one-word affirmance without opinion ("per curiam affirmed" or PCA) has no precedential value because nobody can tell what it decided.

## How courts reason about it

Courts spend real effort deciding which law applies before they get to what it says.

- **Is there a statute on point?** If yes, the statute controls and the common law yields, but courts read statutes narrowly when they change long-standing common law. The presumption is that the legislature did not abolish a common-law rule unless it said so clearly.
- **Is there a federal statute that occupies the field?** Preemption comes in three flavors: express (the statute says it preempts), field (the federal scheme is so complete there is no room for state law, as with much of immigration), and conflict (it is impossible to comply with both, or state law obstructs the federal purpose). Courts presume against preemption in areas states have traditionally regulated.
- **Which state's law?** When parties or events touch more than one state, "choice of law" rules decide. Contracts often pick their own governing law and Florida courts generally honor the choice. For torts, Florida uses the "most significant relationship" test from the Restatement (Second) of Conflict of Laws, adopted in *Bishop v. Florida Specialty Paint Co.*, 389 So. 2d 999 (Fla. 1980).
- **Has the law changed since the events?** Statutes usually apply going forward. Florida's 2023 tort reform, for example, cut the negligence limitation period from four years to two, but only for claims that accrued on or after March 24, 2023.

## Florida specifics

- Florida Statutes are organized by **title, chapter, and section**. "§ 605.0105" is chapter 605, section 0105. The Legislature publishes them at Online Sunshine and republishes the whole set each year after session.
- The Florida Administrative Code is at `flrules.org`.
- The **Florida Constitution** matters more to a business than you might expect. It sets the minimum wage (art. X, § 24), the homestead exemption (art. X, § 4), the right to access the courts (art. I, § 21), and bans a personal income tax (art. VII, § 5).
- Florida courts cite the **Restatements** often in contract and tort cases, and have adopted several sections as Florida law. A Restatement section is still only persuasive until a Florida court adopts it.
- Florida's **Rules of Civil Procedure**, **Small Claims Rules**, and **Rules of Appellate Procedure** are written by the Florida Supreme Court, not the Legislature. They govern how cases run. A statute that tries to set procedure can be struck as a separation-of-powers problem.

## Angles checklist

When a question lands on your desk:

- Is this federal, state, or both? Which statute or chapter?
- Is there a statute at all, or is this common law?
- Which court would hear it, and whose decisions bind that court?
- Does a contract pick a governing law or forum?
- When did the events happen, and has the law changed since?
- Is the source I am reading current? Statute text has a year. Case law can be overruled or superseded by statute.

## Drill

A Miami LLC sells software subscriptions nationwide. A customer in Oregon sues the LLC in Oregon state court for deceptive advertising under Oregon's consumer protection act. The subscription agreement says Florida law governs and all disputes must be brought in Miami-Dade County. Before reading on, list the "which law, which court" questions a lawyer would ask.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Forum selection.** The agreement picks Miami-Dade. Will an Oregon court enforce that clause and dismiss? Most courts enforce forum clauses in commercial contracts unless unreasonable; consumer contracts get more scrutiny. Issue one is whether the customer is a consumer and whether the clause was fairly presented (see module 03 on online contracts).
- **Choice of law.** The agreement picks Florida law. But a state's consumer protection statute often cannot be waived by a choice-of-law clause as to that state's residents, and Oregon's court applies Oregon's choice-of-law rules, not Florida's. So the clause might not knock out the Oregon claim even if the case moves to Florida.
- **Personal jurisdiction.** Can an Oregon court reach a Florida LLC at all? Selling subscriptions to Oregon residents is probably enough "minimum contacts" (module 01, *International Shoe*).
- **Federal removal.** If the customer is an Oregon citizen and the LLC's members are all Florida citizens and the amount exceeds $75,000, the LLC could remove to federal court. An LLC's citizenship is that of every member, not where it is formed.
- **Binding authority.** If the case ends up in a Florida court applying Florida law, Florida DCA decisions bind. If it ends up in federal court in Florida applying Oregon law, the federal court must predict what the Oregon Supreme Court would do.
- **Does FDUTPA apply too?** Possibly, since the conduct came from Florida. That is a second claim the customer might add.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A Florida trial judge in Tampa (Second DCA) finds a Third DCA case squarely on point and no Second DCA case. Must the judge follow it?
2. Can a Florida statute override a Florida Supreme Court decision interpreting the common law?
3. An Eleventh Circuit opinion interprets Florida's non-compete statute. Is a Florida circuit judge bound by it?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Yes. Under *Pardo*, a DCA decision binds every Florida trial court unless the trial court's own DCA has ruled otherwise.
2. Yes, within constitutional limits. The Legislature can change common law by statute. Courts will read the statute narrowly if it is unclear whether it meant to abolish the common-law rule. The Legislature cannot override a decision interpreting the Florida Constitution without a constitutional amendment.
3. No. On a question of Florida law, federal circuit decisions are persuasive only. The Florida judge is bound by Florida appellate courts. In practice, Florida judges read Eleventh Circuit opinions carefully and often agree.

</details>
