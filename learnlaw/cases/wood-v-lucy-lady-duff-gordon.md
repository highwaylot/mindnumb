# Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon, 222 N.Y. 88, 118 N.E. 214 (N.Y. 1917)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · consideration · implied terms · good faith · exclusive dealing
**Used in:** [`03-contracts/02-consideration-and-promissory-estoppel.md`](../03-contracts/02-consideration-and-promissory-estoppel.md), [`03-contracts/04-interpretation-and-parol-evidence.md`](../03-contracts/04-interpretation-and-parol-evidence.md), [`03-contracts/05-performance-conditions-breach.md`](../03-contracts/05-performance-conditions-breach.md)

## One-line takeaway

An exclusive dealing agreement that does not spell out the agent's duties carries an implied promise to use reasonable efforts, and that implied promise is consideration enough to make the contract binding.

## Facts

Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon, was a famous fashion designer. Her name sold clothes. In 1915 she signed a written agreement giving Otis Wood the exclusive right, for one year and renewable, to place her endorsements on other makers' designs, to sell her own designs, and to license others to sell them. She was to receive half of all profits and revenues. Wood was to keep accounts and pay her monthly. The writing said nothing about what Wood had to do. It did not say he would try to sell anything.

Lucy then endorsed fabrics and garments for Sears, Roebuck on her own, kept the money, and told Wood nothing. He sued for his share of the profits.

## Procedural posture

Wood sued in New York for breach of contract. Lucy moved to dismiss, arguing the agreement lacked mutuality: she had bound herself, but Wood had promised nothing, so there was no consideration and no contract. The trial court denied the motion. The Appellate Division reversed and dismissed the complaint. Wood appealed to the New York Court of Appeals.

## Issue

Is an exclusive agency agreement that states no express duty on the agent enforceable because the agent impliedly promised to use reasonable efforts?

## Holding

Yes. Judge Cardozo, for a divided court, held that the writing was "instinct with an obligation, imperfectly expressed." Wood impliedly promised to use reasonable efforts to place Lucy's endorsements and market her designs. That implied promise was consideration, so the contract was binding on both sides. The Appellate Division's dismissal was reversed.

## Reasoning

Cardozo's method was to read the whole writing and ask what business sense it made. Lucy gave Wood an exclusive. Without that exclusivity she could have dealt with anyone. The only reason to grant it was that Wood would work. Her sole compensation was half of the profits. Unless Wood tried, she would get nothing. A promise to pay half the profits implies a promise to make some.

The court pointed to the mechanics in the document. Wood was to account monthly and pay what was due. He was to take out patents and copyrights where needed. The agreement ran for a fixed term with notice to end it. These terms describe a working business arrangement, not a gift of an option. "The law has outgrown its primitive stage of formalism when the precise word was the sovereign talisman," Cardozo wrote. A promise may be lacking in words and still be fairly implied from the whole.

The case shifted contract law toward reading agreements as business people would, and it is the origin of the modern rule that exclusive dealing contracts carry a best efforts or reasonable efforts obligation.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Three judges dissented without writing an opinion (verify). The losing argument, which the Appellate Division had accepted, was the traditional one: courts enforce promises people make, not promises courts think they should have made. If Wood wanted to bind himself to anything he could have said so. Reading in a duty of effort lets a party who wrote a one-sided document claim the benefit of terms it never gave. The counterpoint still matters. Courts imply terms to fill gaps, not to rewrite a contract or to contradict what it says.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Exclusive arrangements are everywhere in small business: a sales rep with exclusive territory, a distributor with exclusive rights to a product, a licensee with exclusive rights to a brand. This case means that granting an exclusive without written performance standards does not give the other side a free option. They owe reasonable efforts. It also means you cannot assume silence protects you. If you are the one holding the exclusive, courts will expect you to work it. If you grant it, write the minimum: sales quotas, marketing commitments, reporting, and a termination right if targets are missed. Implied terms are a fallback. Express terms are better.

## Florida connection

Florida follows this rule. For sales of goods, § 672.306(2), Fla. Stat. codifies it: an exclusive dealing agreement imposes an obligation on the seller to use best efforts to supply and on the buyer to use best efforts to promote sale, unless otherwise agreed. For contracts generally, Florida recognizes an implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing in every contract. The limits matter. The implied covenant cannot override an express term and cannot be the basis of a claim by itself; it must attach to the performance of a specific term. *County of Brevard v. Miorelli Engineering, Inc.*, 703 So. 2d 1049 (Fla. 1997); *Burger King Corp. v. Weaver*, 169 F.3d 1310 (11th Cir. 1999) (applying Florida law). Florida courts also apply the doctrine that a contract leaving one side free to perform or not is illusory, but they read agreements as a whole to find a real obligation before reaching that conclusion.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Does the outcome change? Suppose the agreement had given Wood a non-exclusive right to market her designs. Would there still be an implied promise of reasonable efforts?
2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What language in the writing could Lucy have pointed to that made Wood's performance optional?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? You sign an exclusive distributor for your product in the Southeast with no quotas. Sales are flat for a year. What claim do you have and what facts decide it?
