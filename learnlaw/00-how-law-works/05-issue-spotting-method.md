# The issue-spotting method

**Module:** 00 · **Lesson:** 5 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

This is the lesson the whole course is built on. A situation arrives as a story: a customer will not pay, a partner wants out, an employee left with the client list. The lawyer's first skill is turning the story into a list of legal issues, seen from every party's side, with the open questions marked. The second skill is noticing what is *not* an issue. Everything after this lesson is content you plug into this method.

## The rule

There is no rule. There is a procedure. Do it in writing, in this order, every time, until it is automatic.

### Step 1: Parties

List every person and entity in the story. For each, note:

- Legal form. Individual, LLC, corporation, partnership, government, estate. An LLC and its sole member are two different parties.
- Role. Buyer, seller, employer, employee, landlord, tenant, lender, guarantor, agent, member, manager, customer, competitor, bystander.
- Relationships to each other. Contract, employment, agency, ownership, family, none.

Draw it if there are more than three. Most missed issues hide in a relationship you did not write down.

### Step 2: Timeline

Put the events in order with dates. Mark:

- When each agreement was made and in what form (written, oral, texts, conduct).
- When each party did or failed to do something.
- When each party learned something.
- When harm occurred.
- Today's date.

The timeline drives limitation periods, who breached first, what each party knew when, and whether a later law applies.

### Step 3: Walk the doctrines

Go through this list and ask, for each, "is there a possible claim here, by whom, against whom?" Write down every maybe. Do not evaluate yet.

| Area | Ask |
| --- | --- |
| **Contract** | Was there an agreement? What were its terms? Did anyone fail to perform? Is there a writing? Any conditions? Any clause on fees, venue, arbitration, limitation of liability, termination? |
| **Quasi-contract** | If no contract, did someone confer a benefit that would be unjust to keep? (Unjust enrichment, quantum meruit, promissory estoppel.) |
| **Tort** | Did anyone act carelessly and cause harm? Lie to induce a deal? Interfere with someone else's contract or customers? Say something false and damaging? Harm property? |
| **Entity** | Who actually owns the business? Is there an operating agreement? Did a member or manager breach a duty? Is the entity properly formed and in good standing? Is anyone trying to reach an owner personally? |
| **Agency** | Who was acting for whom? Did they have authority? Is the principal bound or liable? |
| **Employment** | Employee or contractor? Wages owed? Any restrictive covenant? Any discrimination or retaliation angle? Was the termination lawful? |
| **Property and IP** | Who owns the physical property, the name, the logo, the code, the customer list, the content? Was anything taken or copied? |
| **Statutory** | Does a specific statute apply: FDUTPA, wage statute, consumer protection, licensing, lien law, data breach, telemarketing? Statutes often add fee shifting and multiplied damages. |
| **Regulatory and criminal** | Does anything here need a license, a permit, a tax filing, or a notice to a government? Could any conduct be a crime (theft, fraud, unlicensed contracting)? |

### Step 4: Flip it

For every claim you listed, now take the other side.

- What **defenses** exist? Statute of limitations, statute of frauds, waiver, estoppel, release, prior breach, failure of condition, lack of standing, lack of authority, comparative fault, mitigation, unclean hands, arbitration clause.
- What **counterclaims** could the defendant bring? The customer who will not pay often has a story about defective work.
- Who else could be **brought in**? Third-party claims against the subcontractor, the insurer, the other member.

Every party is both a potential plaintiff and a potential defendant. Write the matrix.

### Step 5: Remedies and procedure

For each live claim:

- **What can be recovered?** Money (compensatory, consequential, liquidated, statutory, punitive), an injunction, specific performance, rescission, declaratory relief, attorney fees, interest, costs.
- **What is it worth?** Rough order of magnitude. This decides the forum and whether it is worth pursuing.
- **Where would it be filed?** Small claims, county, circuit, federal, arbitration. Any forum clause?
- **How long is left?** Limitation period from the timeline.
- **Who pays fees?** American rule by default; contract clause, statute, or proposal for settlement can shift them.
- **Any pre-suit requirement?** Notice, demand, mediation, administrative charge (discrimination claims must go to the EEOC or Florida Commission on Human Relations first).

### Step 6: Mark what you do not know

For each issue, write the facts that would change the answer and that the story does not give. "Was the operating agreement signed?" "What exactly did the text say?" "Did the employee sign anything at hiring?" These are the questions to ask the client or the documents. A good issue list is half questions.

### Step 7: Rank

Now evaluate. Which issues are strong, which weak, which worth money, which are leverage only. Which cut the other way. State the likely outcome for each with the main reason, in one line. Flag the red herrings: things that looked like issues and are not, with the reason.

## How courts reason about it

A court sees the issues the parties plead. Issues not raised are usually waived. That is why the method matters before a lawsuit: the issue you did not spot is the defense you did not plead and the claim you did not bring, and it can be gone for good. Appellate courts in Florida routinely refuse to consider arguments not made in the trial court.

The method also mirrors the structure of an opinion. The court identifies the parties and their relationships, states the facts in order, names the claims, applies the elements, considers defenses, and decides remedies. Writing an issue list is writing the skeleton of the opinion a court would produce.

## Florida specifics

- **Pre-suit requirements** in Florida that catch people: the 21-day § 57.105 safe harbor before a sanctions motion; § 768.72's pre-suit showing for punitive damages; the proposal for settlement rules; notice requirements in construction lien law; the administrative charge requirement under the Florida Civil Rights Act (365 days); notice to a landlord or tenant under chapter 83.
- **Pleading** in Florida state court is fact pleading, not notice pleading. A complaint must allege ultimate facts supporting each element. Fraud must be pleaded with particularity (Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.120(b)).
- **Compulsory counterclaims.** A counterclaim arising from the same transaction must be brought in the same case or it is lost (Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.170(a)).
- **Business entities must have counsel** in Florida courts outside small claims. The owner cannot represent the LLC.

## Angles checklist

The full checklist is the method above. Keep this short version in your head:

1. Who are the parties and how are they related?
2. What happened, in order, and when?
3. Contract, tort, entity, agency, employment, property, statute: which apply?
4. Flip it: defenses, counterclaims, third parties.
5. What is it worth, where would it go, how long is left, who pays fees?
6. What do I not know?
7. Rank, and name the red herrings.

## Check yourself

1. Why list parties before claims?
2. A client describes a dispute with a supplier and mentions, in passing, that the supplier's driver backed into the loading dock last year. Issue or red herring?
3. Why does the method insist on writing the open questions?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Because claims run between parties. Until you know who the parties are and how they are related, you cannot see who could sue whom. Most missed issues are missed relationships.
2. Possibly an issue. Property damage by the supplier's employee in the course of employment is a negligence claim with vicarious liability against the supplier, with a two-year limitation period in Florida for claims accruing after March 24, 2023. It may be leverage in the supplier dispute or a counterclaim. Whether it is worth pursuing is a step-7 question, not a step-3 question.
3. Because the answer to almost every legal question depends on facts, and the facts a client volunteers are never complete. The open questions are the work plan.

</details>
