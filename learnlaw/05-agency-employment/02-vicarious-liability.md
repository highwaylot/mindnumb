# Vicarious liability

**Module:** 05 · **Lesson:** 2 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Your delivery driver rear-ends someone. Your installer drops a tool through a customer's ceiling. Your subcontractor's crew injures a bystander. Whether the business pays for those harms depends on vicarious liability, which is liability for someone else's wrong because of your relationship to them. It is the main reason a small business carries liability insurance, and the main reason a plaintiff names the company rather than the worker.

## The rule

**Respondeat superior.** An employer is liable for a tort committed by an employee acting within the scope of employment. The plaintiff must prove:

1. the tortfeasor was an **employee** (a servant, in older language), not an independent contractor;
2. the employee committed a **tort**; and
3. the tort was committed **within the scope of employment**.

Fault of the employer is not an element. The employer is liable even if it hired carefully and forbade the conduct.

**Scope of employment.** Florida uses a three-part test. Conduct is within scope if it (a) is of the kind the employee was employed to perform, (b) occurred substantially within the time and space limits of the job, and (c) was motivated at least in part by a purpose to serve the employer. See *Sussman v. Florida East Coast Properties, Inc.*, 557 So. 2d 74 (Fla. 3d DCA 1990) (verify citation).

- **Frolic and detour.** A minor deviation for personal reasons (a detour) stays within scope. A major departure on the employee's own business (a frolic) leaves it. Scope resumes when the employee returns to the work.
- **Going and coming.** Commuting is outside scope. Travel between job sites, or a special errand, is inside.
- **Intentional torts.** Usually outside scope, because the employee is serving personal motives. An assault to collect a debt for the employer, or a bouncer using excessive force, can be inside. Sexual assault almost never is.

**Independent contractors.** A hirer is generally not liable for a contractor's torts. The exceptions swallow much of the rule:

- **Non-delegable duties.** Duties the law places on the owner personally, such as a landowner's duty to keep premises reasonably safe for invitees, or duties imposed by statute or license. Hiring a contractor does not shift them.
- **Inherently dangerous activities.** Work that carries a high risk of harm unless special precautions are taken, such as demolition, blasting, or crane work. The hirer stays liable for harm from that risk.
- **Negligent hiring, retention, or supervision.** This is direct liability, not vicarious. The hirer is liable for its own carelessness in choosing or keeping an unfit worker, employee or contractor, when the unfitness caused foreseeable harm.

**Florida's dangerous instrumentality doctrine.** The owner of a motor vehicle who entrusts it to another is vicariously liable for the operator's negligent driving. See the Florida specifics below.

The plaintiff bears the burden on every element, including employee status and scope.

## How courts reason about it

The policy underneath respondeat superior is enterprise liability: the business that profits from an activity and controls it should bear the risks it creates, and it is better placed than the victim to insure and to deter.

The fights are about edges.

**Employee or contractor.** This is the first battleground and is governed by the right-to-control test from *Cantor v. Cochran* (lesson 4). A plaintiff will emphasize control, uniforms, company vehicles, and integration into the business. The defendant will emphasize the written contract, the contractor's own tools and insurance, and freedom over method. Courts look past the label to the reality.

**Motive to serve.** Florida's third element does the most work in intentional tort cases. A security guard who beats a patron can be found within scope because restraining patrons is the job. A clerk who assaults a customer over a personal insult is outside it. Courts ask whether the employment gave the employee the occasion and some purpose connected to the job, or merely the opportunity.

**How far off the path.** Frolic cases turn on time, distance, and purpose. A driver who stops for lunch on the route is on a detour. A driver who takes the truck to a bar across town after hours is on a frolic.

After Florida's 2023 tort reform (HB 837), negligence claims accruing on or after March 24, 2023 carry a two-year limitation period, and a plaintiff more than 50% at fault recovers nothing (§ 768.81(6)) (verify). A vicariously liable employer stands in the employee's shoes for apportionment rather than getting its own share.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rules on respondeat superior, scope, and the contractor exceptions. It departs in two ways.

**Dangerous instrumentality doctrine.** Since *Southern Cotton Oil Co. v. Anderson*, 86 So. 629 (Fla. 1920) (verify citation), Florida treats a motor vehicle as a dangerous instrumentality. The owner who voluntarily entrusts the vehicle to another is liable for that person's negligent operation, with no need to show scope of employment or any fault by the owner. This is a minority rule. It reaches the business that lets an employee take the company truck home, and the owner who lends a car to a friend. The Legislature capped it for some owners. Under § 324.021(9)(b) (verify), an owner who is a natural person and lends a vehicle to a permissive user is liable for bodily injury up to $100,000 per person and $300,000 per incident, and $50,000 for property damage, with additional exposure for economic damages up to $500,000 if the driver is uninsured or underinsured. Rental and lease companies are largely protected by the same section and by the federal Graves Amendment, 49 U.S.C. § 30106. The doctrine does not apply when the vehicle was stolen or taken without consent.

**Negligent hiring presumption.** Under § 768.096, an employer gets a presumption against negligent hiring if, before hiring, it ran a criminal background check, checked references, and took the other listed steps, and nothing turned up that showed unfitness. The presumption is rebuttable.

Workers' compensation (ch. 440) is the exclusive remedy for an employee injured on the job. That immunity does not protect the employer from suits by third parties the employee injures.

## Angles checklist

- Who committed the wrong, and what was their relationship to the business: employee, contractor, volunteer, member?
- Was the conduct the kind they were hired to do, at a work time and place, with some purpose to serve the business?
- Was a vehicle involved, and who owned it? Was the driver permitted to use it?
- If a contractor: is there a non-delegable duty, an inherently dangerous activity, or evidence the business knew the contractor was unfit?
- Is the injured person an employee, which routes the claim to workers' comp?
- Flip it: can the business seek indemnity from the employee or contractor? Does the contractor's insurance name the business as an additional insured?
- Comparative fault, the two-year limitation period, and apportionment among nonparties.

## Drill

Bayfront Pools, LLC hires Carlos as a service technician. He drives a company van home each night. One Saturday he uses the van to help his brother move, and on the way back he stops at a customer's house to drop off a filter the customer had asked about, then hits a cyclist. Separately, Bayfront hired Delta Excavation, a contractor, to dig a pool. Delta's crew struck a gas line that Delta had not located, and the neighbor's garage was damaged. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Respondeat superior for the cyclist.** Carlos is an employee. The move for his brother is a frolic: personal, on a weekend, no purpose to serve Bayfront. But the stop at the customer's house to deliver a filter is work. Courts ask whether he had resumed the employer's business when the accident occurred. Likely contested.
- **Dangerous instrumentality.** This likely moots the scope fight. Bayfront owns the van and entrusted it to Carlos. Under Florida's doctrine, Bayfront is vicariously liable for his negligent driving regardless of scope. The § 324.021(9)(b) caps protect natural-person owners, not an LLC. A policy forbidding weekend use does not defeat the doctrine if the original entrustment was voluntary, though it supports indemnity against Carlos.
- **The gas line.** Delta is a contractor. Bayfront is not liable for Delta's negligence unless an exception applies. Excavation near utilities is a strong candidate for inherently dangerous activity, and Florida's underground facility damage statute (ch. 556) imposes duties on excavators. The neighbor will plead negligent selection if Delta was unlicensed or had a record.
- **Procedure.** Two-year limitation period, comparative fault of the cyclist, apportionment, and indemnity and additional-insured questions between Bayfront and Delta.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. An employer's handbook forbids personal use of company vehicles. Does the prohibition defeat respondeat superior when an employee causes a crash during a work errand?
2. Why does a plaintiff plead negligent hiring as well as respondeat superior?
3. A sole proprietor lends her personal car to a friend who injures someone. Is she liable, and how much?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Scope of employment depends on the nature, time, place, and purpose of the conduct, not on whether the employer approved it. Forbidden conduct can still be within scope. In Florida the dangerous instrumentality doctrine adds owner liability regardless.
2. Because negligent hiring is direct liability that does not depend on scope of employment and can reach conduct outside scope, such as an intentional tort, when the employer should have known the worker was dangerous.
3. Yes, under the dangerous instrumentality doctrine, as a natural-person owner who lent the car. Her exposure is capped by § 324.021(9)(b): $100,000 per person and $300,000 per incident for bodily injury, $50,000 property damage, plus up to $500,000 in economic damages if the friend is uninsured or underinsured (verify).

</details>
