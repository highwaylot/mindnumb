# SEC v. W.J. Howey Co., 328 U.S. 293 (1946)

**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]` · securities · investment contract · raising money · Florida citrus
**Used in:** [`07-regulatory-compliance/06-raising-money-securities-basics.md`](../07-regulatory-compliance/06-raising-money-securities-basics.md), [`02-business-entities/01-choosing-an-entity.md`](../02-business-entities/01-choosing-an-entity.md)

## One-line takeaway

An investment contract, and therefore a security, is any scheme in which a person invests money in a common enterprise and is led to expect profits from the efforts of the promoter or a third party; the label the parties put on it does not matter.

## Facts

This is a Florida case. W.J. Howey Company owned large citrus groves in Lake County, Florida, near the town of Howey-in-the-Hills. It sold narrow strips of its groves to the public, mostly guests at the resort hotel it operated nearby. Buyers were mainly out-of-state professionals and business people with no knowledge of citrus farming and no intention of farming the land.

Each buyer received a deed to the strip and was offered a service contract with Howey-in-the-Hills Service, Inc., an affiliate under the same control. Under the service contract the affiliate took full possession, cultivated, harvested, and marketed the fruit, pooled the produce, and paid the buyer a share of the net profits. The buyer could not enter the land without consent. About 85 percent of the acreage sold was sold with a service contract. Neither the land sales nor the service contracts were registered with the Securities and Exchange Commission.

## Procedural posture

The SEC sued in the Southern District of Florida to enjoin Howey from using the mails and interstate commerce to offer and sell unregistered securities in violation of § 5 of the Securities Act of 1933. The district court denied the injunction, finding the transactions were sales of real estate with a separate service contract, not securities. The Fifth Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed.

## Issue

Is an offering of citrus grove land coupled with a service contract to manage the groves and distribute profits an "investment contract," and therefore a security, under the Securities Act?

## Holding

Yes. Justice Murphy, for the Court, held that an investment contract means a contract, transaction, or scheme whereby a person invests money in a common enterprise and is led to expect profits solely from the efforts of the promoter or a third party. The test disregards form and looks to economic reality. The Howey arrangement met it. The transactions were securities and had to be registered or exempt.

## Reasoning

The Court traced the term "investment contract" to state blue sky laws, where courts had used it to reach schemes that placed capital in the hands of others in the expectation of profit. Congress used the term in the 1933 Act without definition, so it carried that meaning.

Applied to the facts, every element was present. Buyers invested money. The enterprise was common: fruit was pooled and profits allocated. Buyers expected profits. And the profits came from the efforts of Howey's affiliate. The buyers did nothing but pay. The deeds and the separate service contract were form. The substance was an offering of an interest in a profit-making citrus enterprise managed by the promoter.

The Court added that it did not matter that some buyers could have declined the service contract or that the land had some value on its own. The offering as a whole was an investment scheme.

Later cases softened "solely" to "predominantly" or "substantially" the efforts of others, so that minor investor participation does not defeat security status.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Justice Frankfurter dissented. Two lower courts had found as a fact that this was a sale of land plus an ordinary service contract that buyers could take or leave. He would have respected those findings. He also noted that buyers were free to hire other management companies, which cut against the idea that profits depended on Howey. The majority's answer was that the record showed the scheme was marketed and operated as a unit.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Any time a business takes money from people who expect to profit from what the business does, this test decides whether the law of securities applies. Selling LLC interests to passive investors, offering "revenue shares" or "profit participations," crowdfunding, promissory notes with returns tied to the business, and tokens all get run through *Howey*. If the answer is yes, the offering must be registered or fit an exemption, antifraud rules apply to every statement made to investors, and the business and its principals can face rescission claims and personal liability.

## Florida connection

Florida's Securities and Investor Protection Act, ch. 517, Fla. Stat., defines "security" in § 517.021 to include an investment contract, and Florida courts apply the *Howey* test to decide what counts. Sales of securities in Florida must be registered under § 517.07 or exempt under § 517.061, which includes a limited offering exemption for sales to a small number of purchasers without general solicitation (verify current conditions). Section 517.301 prohibits fraud in connection with the sale of securities. Section 517.211 gives a purchaser of an unregistered or fraudulently sold security the right to rescind and recover what was paid with interest, and reaches every director, officer, partner, or agent who personally participated in the sale. The Office of Financial Regulation enforces the act.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Does the outcome change? Suppose buyers had to manage their own groves and the affiliate offered only optional fruit marketing at market rates. Still a security?
2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What facts would have supported the lower courts' view that these were ordinary land sales?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? Your LLC offers friends a 10 percent "profit share" on a new food truck in exchange for $20,000 each, and they will not work in the business. What questions does *Howey* raise, and what statute in Florida governs?
