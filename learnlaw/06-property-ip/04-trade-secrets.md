# Trade secrets

**Module:** 06 · **Lesson:** 4 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[FL]` `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Most of what makes a small business valuable is not patented, trademarked, or registered anywhere. It is the pricing model, the supplier terms, the customer list with notes on who buys what, the process that makes the work profitable. Trade secret law protects that information, but only if you treat it as secret. The law does not reward what you know; it rewards what you took reasonable steps to keep.

## The rule

Florida's Uniform Trade Secrets Act (FUTSA), ch. 688, Fla. Stat., and the federal Defend Trade Secrets Act (DTSA), 18 U.S.C. § 1836 et seq., run in parallel. Their definitions are nearly identical.

**Trade secret** (§ 688.002(4)): information, including a formula, pattern, compilation, program, device, method, technique, or process, that

1. derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known to, and not being readily ascertainable by proper means by, other persons who could obtain economic value from it; and
2. is the subject of efforts that are reasonable under the circumstances to maintain its secrecy.

**Misappropriation** (§ 688.002(2)) is either:

- **acquisition** of a trade secret by a person who knows or has reason to know it was acquired by **improper means** (theft, bribery, misrepresentation, breach or inducement of a breach of a duty to maintain secrecy, or espionage); or
- **disclosure or use** of a trade secret without consent by a person who used improper means to acquire it, or who knew or had reason to know it came from someone who did, or who acquired it under a duty to keep it secret, or who learned it by accident and knew it was a trade secret before changing position.

Reverse engineering and independent development are proper means. Buying a product and taking it apart is not misappropriation.

**Remedies.** Injunction against actual or threatened misappropriation, which may continue for as long as needed to eliminate the commercial advantage gained (§ 688.003). Damages for actual loss plus unjust enrichment not counted in the loss, or a reasonable royalty (§ 688.004). Exemplary damages up to twice the award for willful and malicious misappropriation. Attorney fees for willful and malicious misappropriation, or for a claim made in bad faith (§ 688.005). The DTSA adds a federal forum, the same remedies, and an ex parte seizure order in extraordinary cases (§ 1836(b)(2)).

**Limitation period.** Three years from when the misappropriation was discovered or should have been (§ 688.007; 18 U.S.C. § 1836(d)) (verify section numbering).

**Whistleblower immunity notice.** Under 18 U.S.C. § 1833(b), an individual is immune from liability for disclosing a trade secret in confidence to a government official or attorney solely to report a suspected violation of law, or in a sealed court filing. An employer must give notice of this immunity in any contract with an employee or contractor that governs use of trade secrets or confidential information. If it does not, the employer cannot recover exemplary damages or attorney fees under the DTSA against that person.

The plaintiff bears the burden of proving the information is a trade secret and was misappropriated. Florida courts require the plaintiff to identify the claimed secrets with reasonable particularity, not simply "our business information."

## How courts reason about it

**Was it actually secret?** The first question is whether the information is generally known or readily ascertainable. Prices a customer will share with any vendor, supplier names printed on packaging, a customer list that duplicates a trade directory: none is a trade secret. Courts look at how hard it would be for a competitor to assemble the information legitimately. A list that took years of sales calls to build and carries notes on buying habits and contacts is the classic protectable compilation. Florida courts have protected lists on those facts and refused protection for names in a public industry. *East v. Aqua Gaming, Inc.*, 805 So. 2d 932 (Fla. 2d DCA 2001) (verify citation).

**Reasonable measures.** Courts do not require perfection, but they require something. The evidence that persuades: confidentiality agreements signed by employees and contractors; marking documents; password and access controls limited to those who need the information; locked storage; exit procedures that recover devices and remind the departing worker of obligations; and a policy employees were actually trained on. The evidence that loses: the information was emailed freely, posted on a shared drive open to everyone, shown to customers without an NDA, or discussed at trade shows.

**Use versus memory.** A departing employee may use general skill, knowledge, and experience gained on the job. The line is between knowledge the employee carries in her head as a skilled worker and specific information that belongs to the employer. Taking documents or files is the clearest misappropriation. Reconstructing a customer list from memory is harder; courts split, and the outcome depends on how specific and how secret the information was. Florida has not clearly adopted the "inevitable disclosure" doctrine that enjoins an employee from a new job merely because disclosure seems unavoidable (verify), so an injunction usually requires evidence of actual or threatened use.

**Injunctions and the departing employee.** Injunctions are the main remedy because damages are hard to prove early. Courts tailor them: return the files, do not use or disclose the identified secrets, do not solicit the specific customers whose information was taken. A preliminary injunction requires a substantial likelihood of success, which turns on the particularity with which the plaintiff identifies the secrets and the proof of taking.

## Florida specifics

Florida adopted the Uniform Trade Secrets Act and follows the majority rule. Florida-specific points:

- **Displacement.** § 688.008 displaces conflicting tort and restitutionary claims based on misappropriation, but not contract claims, criminal remedies, or claims not based on misappropriation. A confidentiality agreement claim survives alongside the statutory claim.
- **Restrictive covenants.** § 542.335(1)(b)1 lists trade secrets as a legitimate business interest, and a covenant protecting trade secrets is presumed reasonable up to 5 years and unreasonable beyond 10 (verify). Trade secret protection by statute requires no contract and no time limit.
- **Litigation secrecy.** § 688.006 requires courts to preserve the secrecy of alleged trade secrets during litigation, by sealing and protective orders. Florida also has an evidentiary privilege for trade secrets, § 90.506.
- **Criminal.** Theft of trade secrets is a felony under § 812.081, and the federal Economic Espionage Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1832, covers it too.

## Angles checklist

- What exactly is the information? Can it be listed with particularity?
- Is it generally known or readily ascertainable? Could a competitor assemble it from public sources?
- What did the business do to protect it: NDAs, access limits, marking, training, exit procedures?
- How did the defendant get it: took files, copied a drive, remembered it, received it from someone else?
- Is there a confidentiality agreement, and does it carry the § 1833(b) notice?
- Any restrictive covenant alongside? Any fiduciary duty (a member or manager)?
- Flip it: independent development, reverse engineering, general skill and knowledge, the information was disclosed without restriction, bad-faith claim exposing the plaintiff to fees.
- Remedies and forum: injunction, damages, royalty, exemplary damages, fees; state or federal court under the DTSA; three-year limitation period.

## Drill

Keys Marine Electronics, LLC keeps a spreadsheet of 600 boat owners with purchase history, service dates, and notes on which owners buy upgrades. It lives on a shared drive every employee can access, and the owner has emailed it to a marketing vendor without an NDA. Technicians sign a one-page confidentiality agreement with no immunity notice. A technician quits, forwards the spreadsheet to his personal email the night before, and starts his own service business, emailing the 600 owners. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Trade secret status.** The compilation has economic value and is not readily ascertainable; competitors cannot buy purchase histories and upgrade notes. Element one is likely met.
- **Reasonable measures.** The weakness. Open access on a shared drive and transmission to a vendor without an NDA both undercut secrecy. The confidentiality agreement helps. A court could go either way; the owner's own loose handling is the defendant's best argument.
- **Misappropriation.** Forwarding the file to personal email the night before leaving is acquisition by improper means and breach of the duty of confidence, and emailing the 600 owners is use. Forensics on the email account will prove it. Clear misappropriation if element two survives.
- **Remedies.** Preliminary injunction ordering return and destruction of the file and barring use and solicitation of the listed owners. Damages measured by lost service revenue or a reasonable royalty. Exemplary damages and fees under FUTSA for willful conduct, but under the DTSA the missing § 1833(b) notice bars exemplary damages and fees against the technician.
- **Contract claim.** Breach of the confidentiality agreement survives § 688.008 displacement.
- **No non-compete.** The technician can compete; the injunction limits only use of the taken information.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A competitor buys your product, takes it apart, and copies the design. Trade secret misappropriation?
2. Why does the absence of a § 1833(b) notice matter if the employer sues only under Florida law?
3. A departing salesperson takes no documents but calls the 20 customers he served from memory. Misappropriation?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Reverse engineering is a proper means. Protection against that requires a patent or a design that cannot be discerned from the product.
2. It does not affect the Florida claim. It bars exemplary damages and attorney fees only under the federal DTSA. Many plaintiffs plead both, so the missing notice costs them the federal enhancements.
3. It depends. If the customer identities are readily ascertainable and he is using general knowledge of who buys, probably not. If the customers were secret and the specific terms and contacts he uses were confidential, a court may find use of a trade secret. A customer non-solicitation covenant would have made this an easy contract case.

</details>
