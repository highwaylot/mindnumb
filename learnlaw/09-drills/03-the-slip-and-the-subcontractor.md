# Drill: The slip and the subcontractor

**Modules exercised:** [04 Torts](../04-torts/02-premises-liability.md), [04 Tort reform and comparative fault](../04-torts/07-florida-2023-tort-reform-and-comparative-fault.md), [05 Vicarious liability](../05-agency-employment/02-vicarious-liability.md), [06 Commercial leases](../06-property-ip/01-commercial-leases.md), [03 Common clauses](../03-contracts/10-common-business-clauses.md)
**Time:** ~60 min

## Scenario

Bayfront Outfitters LLC rents a storefront in a Jacksonville shopping plaza from Riverside Plaza Partners, Ltd. The lease says the tenant is responsible for "maintenance, repair, and safe condition of the premises," requires the tenant to carry commercial general liability insurance naming the landlord as an additional insured, and requires the tenant to indemnify the landlord "from all claims arising from the use or condition of the premises."

Bayfront contracts with SparkleCo Services, Inc. to clean the store nightly. The cleaning contract requires SparkleCo to place wet-floor signs and to carry its own liability insurance. On the morning of April 3, 2026, a SparkleCo crew finished a late mop of the entrance aisle twenty minutes after the store opened. No sign was placed. Bayfront's assistant manager walked past the area twice and said nothing.

At 9:40 a.m., Teresa, a customer, walked in reading a text on her phone, slipped on the damp tile, and fractured her wrist. Surgery and therapy have been billed at $31,000; her health insurer paid most of it at negotiated rates. The store's camera recorded the fall and the mopping.

In October 2026 Teresa's lawyer sued Bayfront and Riverside in Duval County circuit court. Neither has yet told its insurer. Riverside's manager says the lease "puts all of this on the tenant." Bayfront's owner says "the cleaning company did it, not us." Today is October 29, 2026.

## Your task

List every legal issue you can see. For each: who could claim what against whom, what rule governs, what the likely outcome is, and what facts you would want to know that the scenario does not give you.

Write your answer before opening the model answer.

<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Step 1, parties.** Teresa (invitee, plaintiff). Bayfront Outfitters LLC (tenant and occupier). Riverside Plaza Partners, Ltd. (landlord, out of possession). SparkleCo Services, Inc. (independent contractor, not yet sued). The SparkleCo crew (employees, acting in scope). Bayfront's CGL carrier, SparkleCo's carrier, and Teresa's health insurer, which may have a subrogation claim.

**Step 2, timeline.** Lease and cleaning contract, dates unknown. April 3, 2026, mop, no sign, two walk-bys, fall at 9:40. April to October, treatment. October 2026, suit filed. The negligence limitation period is 2 years (verify) for claims accruing after March 24, 2023 (§ 95.11, Fla. Stat.), so the deadline is April 3, 2028. Suit is timely.

**Steps 3 and 4.** Each claim, then its flip.

### Issue 1: Teresa against Bayfront for premises negligence
- **Parties and claim:** Invitee against the business in possession, for negligence.
- **Rule:** A business owes invitees a duty to maintain the premises in a reasonably safe condition and to warn of concealed dangers it knows or should know of. For a slip on a transitory foreign substance, the plaintiff must prove the business had actual or constructive knowledge of the condition; constructive knowledge can be shown by the length of time the condition existed or by regular occurrence (§ 768.0755, Fla. Stat.). Courts treat a hazard the business or its contractor created as actual knowledge (verify).
- **Application:** The mop was the business's own cleaning operation and the assistant manager walked past twice. Both actual and constructive knowledge are easy.
- **Likely outcome:** Liability is likely. The fight will be over fault allocation and damages.
- **Facts that would change it:** How visible the water was. Whether the manager was trained to check.

### Issue 2: Comparative fault and the phone
- **Parties and claim:** Bayfront's defense against Teresa.
- **Rule:** Florida apportions fault among the plaintiff and each party or non-party on the verdict form (§ 768.81(3); *Fabre v. Marin*, 623 So. 2d 1182 (Fla. 1993)). In a negligence action a plaintiff more than 50% at fault recovers nothing (verify) (§ 768.81(6), HB 837, 2023). An open and obvious condition discharges the duty to warn but not the duty to maintain.
- **Application:** Reading a phone while walking is some fault. Damp tile with no sign is not open and obvious. A jury would likely put her in the 10% to 35% range (an estimate, not a rule).
- **Likely outcome:** Reduced recovery, not a bar. The more-than-50% bar is the defense's hope and probably a stretch on this video.
- **Facts that would change it:** Lighting, tile color, whether other customers crossed safely.

### Issue 3: Does the independent contractor shield Bayfront?
- **Parties and claim:** Bayfront's defense that SparkleCo, not Bayfront, was negligent.
- **Rule:** The general rule is that one who hires an independent contractor is not liable for the contractor's negligence. Exceptions: the hirer's own negligence, control retained over the work, and non-delegable duties. Florida treats a business's duty to keep its premises reasonably safe for invitees as non-delegable (verify); the business may hire the work out but not the responsibility. Where a defendant is vicariously liable for another's negligence, *Fabre* apportionment does not reduce its share (verify).
- **Application:** Even if SparkleCo was entirely at fault, Bayfront answers to Teresa for it.
- **Likely outcome:** Bayfront cannot point at SparkleCo to escape Teresa. It can and should bring SparkleCo in to share the cost.
- **Facts that would change it:** The cleaning contract's terms on supervision and timing.

### Issue 4: Teresa against Riverside, the landlord
- **Parties and claim:** Invitee against an out-of-possession landlord.
- **Rule:** A landlord who has surrendered possession and control is generally not liable for injuries on the leased premises unless it retained control of the area, knew of a dangerous condition when it leased, or undertook to repair. A transitory condition created by the tenant's operations is not a defect the landlord answers for.
- **Application:** A mopped floor inside the store is an operational condition under the tenant's control.
- **Likely outcome:** Riverside likely wins summary judgment under the 2021 federal standard (Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.510).
- **Facts that would change it:** Whether the entrance aisle is a common area the landlord maintains. Whether the tile itself was unreasonably slick and the landlord installed it.

### Issue 5: Indemnity and insurance between landlord, tenant, and contractor
- **Parties and claim:** Riverside against Bayfront on the lease indemnity; Bayfront against SparkleCo on the cleaning contract; everyone against their insurers.
- **Rule:** Florida enforces contractual indemnity, but a clause that indemnifies a party for its own negligence must say so in clear and unequivocal terms. Common-law indemnity requires the indemnitee to be without fault and only vicariously liable. The anti-indemnity rule in § 725.06 applies to construction contracts only. An additional-insured endorsement gives the landlord a direct right to the tenant's carrier. A liability policy's duty to defend is broader than its duty to indemnify and is triggered by the complaint's allegations.
- **Application:** The lease language covers "all claims arising from the condition of the premises," which reaches this. Riverside should tender to Bayfront and to Bayfront's CGL carrier as an additional insured. Bayfront should tender to its own carrier and to SparkleCo and SparkleCo's carrier, and file a third-party complaint against SparkleCo for contractual indemnity and breach of the sign requirement (Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.180). Six months without notice to any insurer is the most dangerous fact in the story; late notice can forfeit coverage if the insurer is prejudiced.
- **Likely outcome:** Defense and indemnity flow downhill, landlord to tenant to contractor, if the policies are in force and notice is given now.
- **Facts that would change it:** The exact indemnity wording. Whether SparkleCo actually bought insurance. Policy notice provisions.

### Issue 6: Damages after HB 837
- **Parties and claim:** The measure of Teresa's recovery.
- **Rule:** Evidence of past medical expenses is limited to amounts actually paid or owed, and future expenses to what the plaintiff's coverage would pay; letters of protection must be disclosed (§ 768.0427, verify). Non-economic damages for pain and loss of function are unlimited in an ordinary negligence case. Punitive damages require a pre-suit showing of intentional misconduct or gross negligence (§ 768.72) and are not realistic here.
- **Application:** The $31,000 billed shrinks to the negotiated amounts paid. The wrist injury supports a meaningful non-economic claim.
- **Likely outcome:** A six-figure demand and a mid-five-figure settlement range before fault reduction (estimates).
- **Facts that would change it:** Permanence, her occupation, the health insurer's reimbursement claim.

**Step 6, what you do not know.** Both contracts and both policies. SparkleCo's solvency. Whether the video has been preserved and sent to counsel. Whether Teresa's lawyer sent a pre-suit demand.

### Red herrings
- "The lease puts it all on the tenant." Between landlord and tenant, yes. It does not bind Teresa, and the landlord still needs a defense.
- "The cleaning company did it, not us." A non-delegable duty plus the manager's own walk-bys make this a contribution argument, not a defense.
- § 725.06. Construction contracts only.
- Workers' compensation. No employee was injured.
- The phone as a complete defense. It is a percentage, not a bar, unless a jury puts her over 50%.

### Procedural and remedial angles
- Forum: circuit court in Duval County (Fourth Judicial Circuit), since the claim exceeds $50,000 (verify). Jury trial available. Fault allocated among Teresa, Bayfront, SparkleCo as a *Fabre* non-party unless joined, and Riverside.
- Value: paid medical expenses plus non-economic damages, reduced by Teresa's share.
- Fees: American rule. A proposal for settlement under § 768.79 and Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.442 is the main lever; a defendant whose offer Teresa rejects, where she then recovers at least 25% less (verify), gets fees from the offer date.
- Limitations: negligence, 2 years from April 3, 2026 (verify). Contractual indemnity, 5 years from payment (verify). Notice to insurers: now.
- Pre-suit: none required for premises liability. Preservation of the video is a spoliation issue if lost.
