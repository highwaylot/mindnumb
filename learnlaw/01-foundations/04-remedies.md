# Remedies

**Module:** 01 · **Lesson:** 4 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

A claim is only worth what a court can award on it. Two disputes with the same facts can be worth $5,000 or $500,000 depending on whether lost profits are recoverable, whether a fee clause exists, and whether punitive damages are on the table. Remedies decide whether a case is worth bringing and what a contract clause on damages or fees is actually doing. This lesson is the vocabulary; later modules apply it.

## The rule

Remedies divide into money (damages) and orders (equitable relief), plus the add-ons of fees, interest, and costs. The plaintiff bears the burden of proving the amount of damages with reasonable certainty.

**Compensatory damages** put the injured party where it would have been if the wrong had not happened.

- In contract, the usual measure is **expectation**: the value of the promised performance. Alternatives are **reliance**, the out-of-pocket spending, and **restitution**, the benefit conferred on the breaching party.
- In tort, the measure is the loss caused: property damage, medical costs, lost earnings, pain and suffering.
- **Direct** damages flow immediately from the breach. **Consequential** damages are further losses, such as lost profits on other deals, and are recoverable in contract only if they were foreseeable at the time of contracting. *Hadley v. Baxendale*, 9 Exch. 341 (1854). Florida follows this rule.
- Damages must be proved with **reasonable certainty**. Florida allows even a new business to recover lost profits if it proves them with a reasonable yardstick. *W.W. Gay Mechanical Contractor, Inc. v. Wharfside Two, Ltd.*, 545 So. 2d 1348 (Fla. 1989).
- The injured party must **mitigate**: take reasonable steps to limit the loss. Losses that could have been avoided are not recovered.

**Liquidated damages** are a sum the contract fixes in advance. Enforceable if, at contracting, damages were not readily ascertainable and the sum is not grossly disproportionate to the likely loss. Otherwise it is a penalty and void. *Lefemine v. Baron*, 573 So. 2d 326 (Fla. 1991). A clause that lets the non-breaching party choose between the fixed sum and actual damages is a penalty under *Lefemine*.

**Punitive damages** punish and deter. They are not available for breach of contract alone. In Florida they require clear and convincing evidence of **intentional misconduct** or **gross negligence**, § 768.72(2), Fla. Stat., and may not be pleaded without leave of court on a reasonable evidentiary showing, § 768.72(1). They are capped at three times compensatory damages or $500,000 (verify), whichever is greater, with higher caps for conduct motivated by unreasonable financial gain and no cap for specific intent to harm, § 768.73. An employer answers for an employee's conduct only if it actively participated, knowingly condoned, or was itself grossly negligent, § 768.72(3). Due process adds an outer limit; awards beyond a single-digit ratio to compensatory damages are suspect. *State Farm Mutual Automobile Insurance Co. v. Campbell*, 538 U.S. 408 (2003).

**Nominal damages** mark a wrong with no proven loss. **Statutory damages** are fixed by a statute, as in copyright.

**Injunctions** order a party to do or stop doing something. A temporary injunction in Florida requires a substantial likelihood of success on the merits, a likelihood of irreparable harm, no adequate remedy at law, and that the public interest is served. Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.610. The movant posts a bond. Federal court: Fed. R. Civ. P. 65. "Irreparable" means money cannot fix it: loss of customers and goodwill, disclosure of a trade secret.

**Specific performance** orders the contract performed. It is available when the subject is unique, land above all, and damages are inadequate. Courts do not order personal services performed.

**Rescission** unwinds the contract and returns the parties to where they started. Grounds include fraud, mutual mistake, duress, and failure of consideration. The party seeking it must act promptly and return what it received. **Reformation** rewrites a document to match what the parties actually agreed.

**Declaratory relief** is a judgment stating the parties' rights before anyone breaches. Florida's Declaratory Judgment Act is ch. 86, Fla. Stat.; the federal version is 28 U.S.C. § 2201. There must be a real, present controversy.

**Attorney fees** follow the **American rule**: each side pays its own unless a statute or contract shifts them. Common shifters: a contractual prevailing-party clause, which Florida makes reciprocal, § 57.105(7); a proposal for settlement, § 768.79; and statutes such as FDUTPA, § 501.2105, the wage statute, § 448.08, and the restrictive covenant statute, § 542.335(1)(k). Sanctions for frivolous claims come under § 57.105(1).

**Prejudgment interest** is awarded in Florida as a matter of law on liquidated damages from the date of loss. *Argonaut Insurance Co. v. May Plumbing Co.*, 474 So. 2d 212 (Fla. 1985). Post-judgment interest runs at the rate the Chief Financial Officer sets quarterly, § 55.03. **Costs** go to the prevailing party, § 57.041.

## How courts reason about it

The deep divide is between law and equity. Money damages are the default; a court grants an order only when money will not do. That is why every injunction motion fights over "irreparable harm" and why specific performance is routine for land and nearly unavailable for goods you could buy elsewhere. A damages award ends the matter, while an order requires supervision.

**Certainty versus fairness.** Courts refuse speculative damages but do not let a wrongdoer escape because its own conduct made the loss hard to measure. The more clearly the defendant caused a loss, the more latitude the plaintiff gets on the amount. For a startup, the plaintiff needs a yardstick: comparable businesses, contracts in hand, a track record elsewhere.

**Liquidated damages.** Courts ask what the parties could know at contracting. If damages were hard to estimate then and the number was a genuine attempt to estimate them, the clause stands even if actual damages turned out lower. If the number is the same for a trivial breach as for a total one, or the clause gives one side a choice, it reads as coercion, not estimation.

**Punitive damages.** Florida's statute sets a high bar on purpose, to end the practice of pleading punitive damages in every case to inflate settlement value. The pleading gate forces the plaintiff to show evidence first. The caps and the employer-fault rule limit exposure for a company whose employee went rogue.

**Injunctions.** Courts weigh the harm to each side if they get it wrong. A restrictive covenant case is the common business example. Florida tilts the scale: § 542.335(1)(j) presumes irreparable injury from violation of an enforceable covenant, and the 2025 CHOICE Act requires a court to preliminarily enjoin a breach of a covered agreement. Module 05 covers the details.

**Election of remedies.** A plaintiff cannot both rescind a contract and recover expectation damages on it. Courts let plaintiffs plead alternatives but make them choose before judgment.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the majority rule on expectation damages, *Hadley* foreseeability, mitigation, and the American rule.
- The punitive damages scheme in §§ 768.72 and 768.73, with its pleading gate, clear and convincing standard, and caps, is stricter than many states.
- The *Lefemine* rule that an option between liquidated and actual damages makes the clause a penalty is a Florida refinement worth checking in any contract with a liquidated damages clause.
- Under § 57.105(7), a one-way fee clause becomes two-way. Many form contracts drafted elsewhere do not account for this.
- Prejudgment interest on liquidated damages is a right under *Argonaut*; on an unliquidated tort claim it is generally not awarded.
- FDUTPA allows actual damages and fees but not consequential damages or damages for personal injury, § 501.211 (verify scope).

## Angles checklist

- What does each party want: money, an order, or a declaration of rights?
- For money: direct loss, consequential loss, foreseeability, certainty, mitigation.
- Is there a liquidated damages clause? Is it an estimate or a penalty?
- Is there intentional misconduct or gross negligence that could support punitive damages? What is the evidentiary showing?
- Is any harm irreparable? Is an injunction realistic, and can the movant post a bond?
- Is the subject unique enough for specific performance?
- Are there grounds to rescind, and has the party acted promptly?
- Who pays fees: contract clause, statute, proposal for settlement?
- Is the claim liquidated, so prejudgment interest runs from the loss date?

## Drill

A Jacksonville printer agrees to produce 50,000 catalogs for a retailer's holiday season, delivery by November 1, for $60,000. The contract says the printer pays $500 per day for late delivery and that the retailer may instead recover its actual losses. The printer delivers November 20. The retailer says it lost $300,000 in holiday sales and wants punitive damages because the printer "lied" about its capacity. List the remedies issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Liquidated damages clause.** The retailer's option to take actual damages instead makes the clause a penalty under *Lefemine*. The retailer is left to prove actual damages. A fixed daily sum alone would turn on whether $500 a day was a reasonable estimate at contracting.
- **Consequential damages.** Lost holiday sales are consequential and recoverable under *Hadley* only if foreseeable at contracting. A holiday catalog with a stated delivery date makes them foreseeable. The fight is certainty: can the retailer prove $300,000 from prior years' sales and response rates, and did it mitigate through other channels?
- **Punitive damages.** Not available for breach of contract. The retailer would need an independent tort such as fraud in the inducement, clear and convincing evidence, and leave of court under § 768.72. "Lied about capacity" needs proof the statement was false when made.
- **Fees.** None unless the contract has a fee clause, which would be reciprocal, or a proposal for settlement is served.
- **Interest.** The $60,000 price is liquidated. Any damages for lost sales are unliquidated, so prejudgment interest on them is doubtful.
- **Open questions.** What did the printer say about capacity and when? Did the retailer explain the catalogs were its main holiday channel?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A contract says the buyer pays a $25,000 "fee" if it cancels, whether it cancels the day after signing or the day before delivery. Penalty or liquidated damages?
2. A supplier's employee deliberately mislabeled products, causing a customer's loss. Can the customer get punitive damages against the supplier?
3. A lease has a clause that the landlord recovers its attorney fees in any suit to enforce the lease. The tenant sues and wins. Who pays fees?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Likely a penalty. The same sum regardless of when the breach occurs or how much harm it causes suggests no attempt to estimate loss, and it is grossly disproportionate to the damages from an early cancellation.
2. Only if the supplier itself actively participated in, knowingly condoned, or was grossly negligent in a way that contributed to the conduct, § 768.72(3). The employee's intent alone is not enough.
3. The tenant. Under § 57.105(7), a one-way fee clause is reciprocal, so the prevailing tenant recovers fees from the landlord.

</details>
