# Marketing, telemarketing, and advertising

**Module:** 07 · **Lesson:** 5 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Advertising law turns a routine growth tactic into a statutory claim with per-violation damages. A text campaign to 2,000 leads without the right consent is a potential $1 million lawsuit under Florida's telemarketing statute. A fake review is a federal rule violation. A made-up former price is deception. The rules are specific and knowable, which is why plaintiffs' lawyers like them.

## The rule

**Federal deception, FTC Act § 5 (15 U.S.C. § 45).** An advertisement is deceptive if it contains a representation or omission that is likely to mislead a reasonable consumer and is material to the purchasing decision. Implied claims count. Objective claims must be substantiated before they are made. Florida imports this through FDUTPA's § 501.204(2).

**Endorsements and reviews.** The FTC Endorsement Guides, 16 C.F.R. part 255, require disclosure of any material connection between an endorser and the seller, including payment, free product, or employment. The Rule on Consumer Reviews and Testimonials, 16 C.F.R. part 465 (effective October 2024) (verify), bans fake or AI-generated reviews, buying positive or negative reviews, review suppression, and undisclosed insider reviews, with civil penalties per violation.

**Email: CAN-SPAM (15 U.S.C. §§ 7701 to 7713).** Commercial email must have accurate header and subject lines, identify itself as an advertisement, include a physical postal address, offer an opt-out that works for 30 days and is honored within 10 business days (verify). No private right of action; the FTC and states enforce.

**Calls and texts: TCPA (47 U.S.C. § 227).** Prior express written consent is required for marketing calls or texts to a cell phone made with an automatic telephone dialing system or an artificial or prerecorded voice. *Facebook, Inc. v. Duguid*, 592 U.S. 395 (2021), narrowed "autodialer" to systems that use a random or sequential number generator. Calls to numbers on the National Do Not Call Registry require prior express consent or an established business relationship (47 C.F.R. § 64.1200). Statutory damages of $500 (verify) per violation, trebled to $1,500 (verify) if willful.

**Florida Telephone Solicitation Act, § 501.059.** Florida's TCPA, broadened in 2021 and narrowed in 2023 (verify). A telephonic sales call, including a text, may not be made using an "automated system for the selection and dialing of telephone numbers or the playing of a recorded message" without prior express written consent. The 2023 amendment requires that the system do both selecting and dialing, allows consent by the consumer's own act such as checking a box or replying, and requires a text recipient to reply "STOP" and give the sender 15 days (verify) before suing. Calls are allowed only 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. (verify), no more than three (verify) in 24 hours on the same subject. Damages of $500 (verify) per violation, trebled for willful violations. Private right of action and a per se FDUTPA violation.

**Florida Telemarketing Act, §§ 501.601 to 501.626.** Commercial telephone sellers must be licensed by FDACS and post a bond unless exempt.

**Recording calls, § 934.03.** Florida requires consent of all parties to record a phone call or in-person conversation where there is a reasonable expectation of privacy. Violation is a felony and supports a civil suit under § 934.10. An announcement at the start, with the caller staying on, is consent.

**Sweepstakes, § 849.094.** A game promotion with prizes over $5,000 (verify) must be filed with FDACS at least seven days (verify) before start, with the rules and a trust account or bond for the prize value. No purchase may be required. A promotion that requires consideration is an illegal lottery.

**Pricing claims.** The FTC Guides Against Deceptive Pricing, 16 C.F.R. part 233, require that a "former price" be one at which the item was actually offered in good faith for a reasonable period. "Free" offers must disclose conditions. Price gouging during a declared emergency violates § 501.160.

**Automatic renewal, § 501.165.** Subscriptions renewing for more than 12 months must disclose the renewal clearly and give notice before renewal (verify details). The FTC's broader "click to cancel" rule was vacated in 2025 (verify).

**Burden.** The FTC or the private plaintiff must prove the elements. For substantiation, the advertiser must have had a reasonable basis before making the claim; "we later found it was true" is not a defense.

## How courts reason about it

- **Reasonable consumer.** Courts ask what net impression an ad leaves on a reasonable member of the target audience, reading the whole ad, not a disclaimer buried in a footnote. A disclaimer must be clear and conspicuous and must not contradict the main claim.
- **Puffery.** Subjective superlatives are not actionable. Specific, measurable claims are.
- **Consent fights.** TCPA and FTSA cases turn on whether consent was obtained in writing, what it covered, and whether it was revoked. A checkbox that says "I agree to receive marketing texts from [company] at the number provided, consent not a condition of purchase" is the standard. Courts read the scope of consent narrowly against the sender.
- **What is an "automated system."** After *Duguid* and the 2023 Florida amendment, plaintiffs plead that the platform selected and dialed numbers automatically; defendants show human intervention in selecting numbers or pressing send.
- **Statutory damages and class actions.** Per-violation damages with no injury requirement make these claims ideal for class treatment. Settlement value is driven by the count of messages, not the harm.
- **Review manipulation as deception.** The FTC's position, now a rule, is that a fake review deceives whether or not any statement in it is literally false, because the reader believes it came from a real customer.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows federal deception law through FDUTPA's incorporation of FTC interpretations, and most FTC rule violations are per se FDUTPA violations under § 501.203(3).
- Florida is stricter than federal law on recording: all-party consent under § 934.03 where most states require one party.
- The FTSA is among the most litigated state telemarketing statutes in the country. The 2023 amendment reduced but did not end that litigation.
- Sweepstakes registration under § 849.094 is a Florida-specific filing that national promotions must handle for Florida entrants.

## Case anchors

- [`cases/pnr-v-beacon-property-management.md`](../cases/pnr-v-beacon-property-management.md): the scope of FDUTPA, through which every FTC rule becomes a Florida claim.
- [`cases/carlill-v-carbolic-smoke-ball.md`](../cases/carlill-v-carbolic-smoke-ball.md): an advertisement specific enough to be an offer is also specific enough to be a deceptive claim if false.
- [`cases/new-york-times-v-sullivan.md`](../cases/new-york-times-v-sullivan.md): false statements about a competitor raise defamation and disparagement, with a different fault standard than deception.

## Angles checklist

- Every express claim: is it true, and was it substantiated before publication? Every implied claim: what will the reader take away?
- Former prices real? "Free" conditions disclosed? Comparison claims fair and verifiable?
- Reviews and influencers: material connections disclosed, no incentives tied to positivity, no suppression of negatives?
- Email: header, address, opt-out, suppression list maintained?
- Calls and texts: written consent captured and stored with timestamp and text of disclosure, DNC scrubbing, hours and frequency limits, STOP handling?
- Recording: all-party consent announced?
- Contests: filed with FDACS if over threshold, no purchase required, rules posted?
- Vendors: does the agency contract allocate TCPA and FTSA liability?
- Flip it: a competitor's false ad is a Lanham Act § 43(a) and FDUTPA claim; a competitor's false statement about you may be defamation.

## Drill

SunCoast Solar LLC buys a lead list of 5,000 Florida homeowners and uses a platform that sends a text to each: "Hi! Cut your power bill 60% with SunCoast. Reply YES for a free quote." The platform picks numbers from the uploaded list and sends on a schedule. Two hundred people reply STOP; the platform keeps texting some of them. SunCoast's website shows 48 five-star reviews, 30 written by employees. A sales rep records calls with customers without telling them. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **FTSA.** A marketing text sent by a system that selects numbers from a list and dials them on a schedule is likely an "automated system for the selection and dialing" after the 2023 amendment. No prior express written consent from a purchased list. Exposure: $500 per text, trebled if willful, times 5,000. Texts after STOP are separate, clearly willful violations once 15 days have passed.
- **TCPA.** Whether the platform is an autodialer under *Duguid* depends on whether it uses a random or sequential generator; most list-based platforms are not. But the texts to numbers on the Do Not Call Registry violate the DNC rules regardless of autodialer status. Federal private claims likely.
- **"Cut your power bill 60%."** A specific, measurable claim requiring substantiation. If typical savings are lower, it is deceptive under FTC Act § 5 and FDUTPA. "Free quote" is fine if truly free.
- **Reviews.** Employee-written reviews without disclosure violate the FTC's review rule and the Endorsement Guides, and are a per se FDUTPA violation.
- **Recording.** Recording customer calls without all-party consent violates § 934.03, a felony with a civil remedy under § 934.10.
- **Red herring.** CAN-SPAM does not apply to texts; it covers email. The TCPA and FTSA cover texts.
- **Facts that would change it.** If each homeowner had submitted a web form with a compliant consent checkbox naming SunCoast, the FTSA and TCPA claims collapse, and the case becomes about the STOP failures and the reviews.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A customer gave written consent to receive texts from a gym. The gym sells its list to a supplement company, which texts her. Is the supplement company covered by her consent?
2. Why does Florida require all-party consent to record when the federal wiretap statute requires only one party's?
3. Is an honest, unpaid five-star review from the owner's sister a problem?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Consent is read narrowly and is specific to the party named in the disclosure. The supplement company has no consent and faces TCPA and FTSA exposure.
2. Because Florida's Legislature chose a stricter rule in § 934.03, and states may be more protective than federal law where federal law does not preempt. Florida's rule applies to calls with a Florida participant, which creates risk for out-of-state callers.
3. Yes. The family relationship is a material connection that must be disclosed under the Endorsement Guides, and an undisclosed insider review is covered by the 2024 rule. Honesty of the content does not cure the omitted disclosure.

</details>
