# Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz, 471 U.S. 462 (U.S. Supreme Court 1985)

**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]` · personal jurisdiction · contracts · franchise · choice-of-law clauses
**Used in:** [01-foundations/02-jurisdiction-and-venue.md](../01-foundations/02-jurisdiction-and-venue.md), [03-contracts/10-common-business-clauses.md](../03-contracts/10-common-business-clauses.md), [08-disputes-procedure/08-when-you-are-sued.md](../08-disputes-procedure/08-when-you-are-sued.md)

## One-line takeaway

A contract with a forum resident does not by itself create jurisdiction, but a long-term relationship deliberately aimed at the forum, reinforced by a choice-of-law clause and ongoing obligations there, does.

## Facts

Burger King was a Florida corporation headquartered in Miami. John Rudzewicz and Brian MacShara, Michigan residents, applied for a franchise in Drayton Plains, Michigan. They dealt with Burger King's Michigan district office and with Miami headquarters. The twenty-year franchise agreement said Florida law governed, required monthly payments to Miami, and placed Miami in charge of policy. MacShara attended training in Miami. The parties negotiated several terms directly with the Miami office. After the restaurant opened, business fell off and the franchisees stopped paying. Burger King terminated the franchise. The franchisees kept operating under the Burger King name.

## Procedural posture

Burger King sued in the Southern District of Florida for breach of the franchise agreement, invoking diversity jurisdiction, and for trademark infringement under the Lanham Act. It served the defendants under Florida's long-arm statute, which reached a person who breached a contract in Florida by failing to perform acts required to be performed there. The district court found jurisdiction and after a bench trial awarded damages and an injunction. The Eleventh Circuit reversed, holding that jurisdiction over Rudzewicz was fundamentally unfair because he had no physical ties to Florida and the franchisor held the stronger bargaining position. The Supreme Court granted certiorari. MacShara did not appeal.

## Issue

Did the Florida federal court's exercise of jurisdiction over a Michigan franchisee, whose contacts with Florida consisted of a contract with a Florida franchisor and the relationship that followed, violate due process?

## Holding

No. Rudzewicz had purposefully established minimum contacts with Florida, and nothing made jurisdiction unreasonable. The Eleventh Circuit was reversed.

## Reasoning

Justice Brennan restated the two-part structure that grew out of *International Shoe*. First, has the defendant purposefully directed activities at residents of the forum and does the claim arise out of those activities? Second, if so, do other factors make jurisdiction unreasonable? Those factors include the burden on the defendant, the forum's interest, the plaintiff's interest in relief, and the shared interests of the states.

On the first part, the Court was explicit that a contract with an out-of-state party "alone" does not automatically establish contacts. Courts must look at prior negotiations, contemplated future consequences, the contract's terms, and the parties' actual course of dealing. Here those facts pointed at Florida. Rudzewicz deliberately reached out to a Florida corporation for a long-term franchise and the benefits of its Miami-based organization. He entered a twenty-year relationship with continuing and wide-reaching obligations to Miami. The contract documents said policy and decisions came from Miami. The Florida choice-of-law clause did not itself confer jurisdiction, but it reinforced his deliberate affiliation with Florida and his reasonable foreseeability of litigation there.

On the second part, the Court found no unfairness. Rudzewicz was a sophisticated businessman represented by counsel, and nothing suggested the choice-of-law clause was obtained by fraud or duress. The Court rejected a rule that would protect franchisees generally; unfairness must be shown on the facts.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Justice Stevens, joined by Justice White, dissented. The franchisee ran a Michigan restaurant, had no Florida office or employees, and dealt mostly with the Michigan district office. Nothing gave him fair warning that he might be hauled a thousand miles to Florida. The dissent agreed with the Eleventh Circuit that the disparity in bargaining power and the one-sided boilerplate made the result unfair.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

For a Florida company, this case is good news. A customer, franchisee, distributor, or licensee in another state who signs a long-term agreement with you, pays you in Florida, takes direction from Florida, and agrees to Florida law may be suable in Florida. For a Florida company signing with an out-of-state counterparty, the lesson runs the other way. Read the choice-of-law and forum clauses in the agreement, because they help decide where you will litigate.

The "mere contract" point is the one to remember. A single purchase order from an out-of-state vendor will usually not expose you to suit in the vendor's state. A years-long relationship with continuing obligations there probably will.

## Florida connection

The long-arm provision applied in this case is now § 48.193(1)(a)7, Fla. Stat.: breaching a contract in Florida by failing to perform acts required by the contract to be performed in Florida. Florida courts still require the two-step *Venetian Salami* analysis: statute first, then due process. Florida also has a statute that enforces forum and choice-of-law clauses in larger contracts. Under §§ 685.101 and 685.102, Fla. Stat., parties to a contract involving at least $250,000 may choose Florida law and consent to Florida jurisdiction even without other contacts, provided the contract meets the statute's conditions (verify the current thresholds and conditions). The Eleventh Circuit, whose decision this case reversed, is the federal appellate court for Florida.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. The agreement is a one-year supply contract with no choice-of-law clause and all contacts run through the Michigan office. Same result?
2. What would Rudzewicz have needed to show on the second, fairness part of the test?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? Think about a Florida software company with annual subscribers in forty states who sign the company's Florida-law terms.
