# Drill: The departing salesperson

**Modules exercised:** [05 Agency and employment](../05-agency-employment/06-non-competes-and-restrictive-covenants.md), [05 Wage and hour](../05-agency-employment/05-wage-and-hour.md), [06 Trade secrets](../06-property-ip/04-trade-secrets.md), [04 Tortious interference](../04-torts/04-tortious-interference.md), [08 Disputes](../08-disputes-procedure/01-life-of-a-lawsuit.md)
**Time:** ~60 min

## Scenario

Bayshore Staffing LLC places accountants and bookkeepers with employers in the Tampa area. Priya joined in March 2023 as an account executive. On her first day she signed a two-page agreement with a two-year non-compete covering "staffing services within Hillsborough, Pinellas, and Pasco Counties," a two-year non-solicitation of "any client of the Company," and a confidentiality clause. She had no time to review it.

Priya was paid $58,000 a year plus commissions, averaging $81,000 total. She worked from the office, called clients from her desk, and visited clients two or three afternoons a month. She logged 50 to 55 hours most weeks and was never paid overtime.

On September 12, 2026, Priya downloaded the "Active Clients" spreadsheet, about 300 rows with contact names, direct lines, billing rates, and contract renewal dates, to a personal cloud drive. The spreadsheet lived on a shared server any employee could open. On September 15 she resigned. On September 22 she started at Gulfside Talent, a direct competitor in Tampa, which she told about the agreement. In October she began calling Bayshore clients. Three have moved to Gulfside so far.

Bayshore wants her stopped and wants Gulfside to pay. Priya's lawyer has mentioned unpaid overtime. Today is October 29, 2026.

## Your task

List every legal issue you can see. For each: who could claim what against whom, what rule governs, what the likely outcome is, and what facts you would want to know that the scenario does not give you.

Write your answer before opening the model answer.

<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Step 1, parties.** Bayshore Staffing LLC (former employer). Priya (former employee, bound by a signed covenant). Gulfside Talent (competitor and new employer, with knowledge). The three clients who moved and the 297 who have not (third parties with business relationships).

**Step 2, timeline.** March 2023, agreement signed at hire. March 2023 to September 2026, 50 to 55 hour weeks with no overtime. September 12, 2026, download. September 15, resignation. September 22, new job. October, solicitation and three lost clients. Today, October 29, 2026. The covenant runs to September 2028. The FLSA look-back reaches to October 2024, or 2023 if willful.

**Steps 3 and 4.** Issues, with the flip on each.

### Issue 1: Is the non-compete enforceable?
- **Parties and claim:** Bayshore against Priya for breach of the restrictive covenant; injunction and damages.
- **Rule:** § 542.335, Fla. Stat. The covenant must be in a writing signed by the person against whom enforcement is sought (§ 542.335(1)(a)). The employer must plead and prove one or more legitimate business interests, which include trade secrets, confidential business information, substantial relationships with specific prospective or existing customers, and goodwill (§ 542.335(1)(b)), and that the restraint is reasonably necessary to protect them (§ 542.335(1)(c)). For a former employee, 6 months or less is presumed reasonable and more than 2 years presumed unreasonable (verify) (§ 542.335(1)(d)1). A court must modify an overbroad covenant rather than strike it (§ 542.335(1)(c)) and may not consider hardship to the employee (§ 542.335(1)(g)1). Violation creates a presumption of irreparable injury (§ 542.335(1)(j)). The prevailing party gets fees (§ 542.335(1)(k)). See [`cases/white-v-mederi-caretenders.md`](../cases/white-v-mederi-caretenders.md) and [`cases/proudfoot-consulting-v-gordon.md`](../cases/proudfoot-consulting-v-gordon.md).
- **Application:** Signed, two years, three counties, a staffing firm's client relationships. Two years sits between the presumptions. Client relationships are a textbook legitimate interest. Continued employment is sufficient consideration in Florida (verify), and here the covenant was signed at hire. The non-solicit of "any client" is probably overbroad and will be trimmed to clients she dealt with.
- **Likely outcome:** An injunction is likely, possibly narrowed to clients she worked with and perhaps shortened. Priya's best defense is prior material breach: several Florida courts hold that an employer's own material breach, such as unpaid wages, can bar enforcement (verify).
- **Facts that would change it:** Whether the three clients were hers or house accounts. Whether unpaid overtime counts as a breach of the same agreement.

### Issue 2: Does the CHOICE Act apply?
- **Parties and claim:** Same parties; the question is which statute governs.
- **Rule:** The CHOICE Act (§§ 542.41–542.45, verify numbering), effective July 1, 2025 (verify), governs "covered" non-competes for employees earning more than twice the annual mean wage of the county of the employer's principal place of business, signed with 7 days to review and written notice of the right to counsel. Covered agreements can run up to 4 years and a court must preliminarily enjoin a breach (verify).
- **Application:** Priya's agreement predates the Act, she had no review period and no counsel notice, and $81,000 is well under twice Hillsborough's mean wage (verify the figure). Nothing about it is covered.
- **Likely outcome:** § 542.335 governs. CHOICE would change everything for a six-figure hire signed after July 2025.
- **Facts that would change it:** Date of signing, compensation, and notice formalities.

### Issue 3: Trade secret misappropriation
- **Parties and claim:** Bayshore against Priya, and against Gulfside if it uses the list, under the Florida Uniform Trade Secrets Act (ch. 688) and the federal Defend Trade Secrets Act (18 U.S.C. § 1836).
- **Rule:** A trade secret is information that derives independent economic value from not being generally known or readily ascertainable and is the subject of reasonable efforts to maintain its secrecy (§ 688.002(4)). Misappropriation includes acquisition by improper means or use after acquiring it under a duty of confidence. Remedies: injunction (§ 688.003), damages including unjust enrichment (§ 688.004), double damages for willful misappropriation, and fees (§ 688.005). A compiled customer list with rates and renewal dates can qualify (*East v. Aqua Gaming, Inc.*, 805 So. 2d 932 (Fla. 2d DCA 2001), verify citation).
- **Application:** Rates and renewal dates are the valuable part. The weak link is "reasonable efforts." A spreadsheet any employee could open with no password and no access log invites the argument that Bayshore did not protect it. The confidentiality clause helps.
- **Likely outcome:** A close call on secrecy measures; strong on improper acquisition if secrecy is shown. The download three days before resigning also breaches her common-law duty of loyalty while still employed, and it is the fact a judge will remember.
- **Facts that would change it:** Server permissions, written policies, whether the data was marked confidential, whether Gulfside has opened the file.

### Issue 4: Tortious interference against Gulfside
- **Parties and claim:** Bayshore against Gulfside for interfering with the Priya covenant and with Bayshore's client relationships.
- **Rule:** Elements: a contract or business relationship, the defendant's knowledge, intentional and unjustified interference, and damage. Relationships must be with identifiable customers, not the market at large (*Ethan Allen, Inc. v. Georgetown Manor, Inc.*, 647 So. 2d 812 (Fla. 1994)). Fair competition is a justification unless improper means are used. See [`cases/ethan-allen-v-georgetown-manor.md`](../cases/ethan-allen-v-georgetown-manor.md).
- **Application:** Gulfside knew of the covenant and hired her into the same territory anyway. The three lost clients are identifiable. Using a stolen list is improper means; ordinary recruiting is not.
- **Likely outcome:** Viable as to the three identified clients and the covenant; the "at large" claim fails.
- **Facts that would change it:** Whether Gulfside told her not to solicit; why the clients left.

### Issue 5: Priya's overtime claim
- **Parties and claim:** Priya against Bayshore under the FLSA for unpaid overtime, liquidated damages, and fees (29 U.S.C. §§ 207, 216(b)).
- **Rule:** Overtime is owed over 40 hours unless an exemption applies. The outside sales exemption requires the employee to be customarily and regularly engaged away from the employer's place of business (29 C.F.R. § 541.500). The administrative exemption requires a salary of at least $684 per week (verify; the 2024 increase was vacated) and a primary duty of office work directly related to management or general business operations, with discretion and independent judgment. Selling the employer's core service is generally "production" work, not administrative. Florida has no state overtime statute.
- **Application:** Desk-based selling with occasional visits is not outside sales. Courts split on staffing account executives under the administrative exemption. The fight is over duties.
- **Likely outcome:** A real claim. Two years of half-time pay on roughly 12 overtime hours a week, doubled as liquidated damages, plus fees. It is also leverage against the injunction.
- **Facts that would change it:** Her actual duties, time records, and whether Bayshore relied on counsel in classifying her.

**Step 6, what you do not know.** The full agreement text, including fee, venue, and choice-of-law clauses. Whether the three clients had written contracts with Bayshore. What Gulfside's offer letter says. Whether a forensic image of her drive can be obtained.

### Red herrings
- Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. She was authorized to open the file; *Van Buren v. United States*, 593 U.S. 374 (2021), closes that door. Florida's computer abuse statute (§§ 668.801–668.805, verify) has the same authorization problem.
- "Two years is unfair to her." Hardship is not a factor under § 542.335(1)(g)1.
- "She signed on day one with no review." Only a CHOICE Act agreement needs a review period.
- The three counties. Geography matches where Bayshore operates; it is not overbroad.

### Procedural and remedial angles
- Forum: circuit court in Hillsborough County (Thirteenth Judicial Circuit) for the injunction, or federal court in the Middle District under the DTSA with the state claims attached. Check the agreement for a venue or arbitration clause.
- Procedure: verified complaint and motion for temporary injunction under Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.610; a bond is required; the irreparable-harm presumption shortens the fight. Send preservation letters to Priya and Gulfside first.
- Value: lost margin on three clients, more if others follow; injunction value exceeds damages. FLSA exposure is five figures plus fees.
- Fees: § 542.335(1)(k) and § 688.005 shift to the prevailing party; FLSA fees to a prevailing employee only.
- Limitations: written contract 5 years (verify); trade secrets 3 years from discovery (§ 688.007); tortious interference 4 years (verify); FLSA 2 years, 3 if willful (verify).
