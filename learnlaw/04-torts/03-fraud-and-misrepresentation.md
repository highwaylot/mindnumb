# Fraud and misrepresentation

**Module:** 04 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Fraud turns a contract dispute into something worse. It opens punitive damages, survives the liability cap, is not discharged in bankruptcy, and gets pleaded against owners personally. It is also the claim you may need when a seller or vendor lied to get your money. The elements are strict, the pleading rules stricter, and Florida courts work hard to keep broken promises from being recast as fraud.

## The rule

**Fraudulent misrepresentation** (also called fraud in the inducement or deceit). The plaintiff must prove:

1. A false statement of material fact;
2. The defendant knew it was false, or made it recklessly without knowledge of its truth;
3. The defendant intended the statement to induce the plaintiff to act;
4. The plaintiff justifiably relied on it; and
5. The plaintiff suffered damage as a result.

**Negligent misrepresentation.** Same structure, but the defendant need only have been careless about the truth, and the defendant must have made the statement in the course of business or a transaction in which it had a pecuniary interest, intending to supply information for the plaintiff's guidance. Restatement (Second) of Torts § 552. Comparative negligence applies.

**Fraudulent concealment and non-disclosure.** Silence is fraud only where there is a duty to speak: a fiduciary or confidential relationship, a partial statement that is misleading without the rest, or facts peculiarly within one party's knowledge that the other cannot discover. In arm's-length commercial deals between sophisticated parties, there is generally no duty to volunteer.

**What is a statement of fact.** Opinion, prediction, and sales puffery are not actionable. "This is a great location" is puffery. "This location did $2 million last year" is fact. A promise to do something in the future is not fraud unless the promisor had no intention of performing when the promise was made.

**Pleading.** Fraud must be pleaded with particularity: who said what, to whom, when, and how it was false. Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.120(b); Fed. R. Civ. P. 9(b).

**Remedies.** Rescission of the induced contract, or affirmance plus damages. Damages are measured by the benefit of the bargain in Florida, the difference between the value as represented and the actual value, plus consequential losses. Punitive damages are available on the showing required by § 768.72, Fla. Stat.

**Limitation.** Four years (verify) from when the fraud was or should have been discovered, with a 12-year (verify) outer limit from the fraud itself. §§ 95.11(3), 95.031(2)(a).

The plaintiff bears the burden on every element, by the greater weight of the evidence in most Florida civil cases (verify for specific contexts).

## How courts reason about it

Courts want to punish lies. They also know every disappointed contract party is tempted to say the other side never meant it. The tension shows in two places: justifiable reliance and the line between fraud and breach.

**Justifiable reliance.** Can a plaintiff who could have checked the facts and did not still sue? The Florida Supreme Court answered in *Butler v. Yusem*, 44 So. 3d 102 (Fla. 2010): justifiable reliance is not reasonable reliance, and a plaintiff need not investigate a representation before relying on it. The court reaffirmed *Besett v. Basnett*, 389 So. 2d 995 (Fla. 1980): a recipient may rely on a representation of fact without investigation unless the recipient knows it is false or its falsity is obvious. A liar should not escape by arguing the victim was too trusting. The limit is obviousness: a plaintiff told the roof was new while standing under a visible leak has no claim.

For negligent misrepresentation the court is less protective. *Gilchrist Timber Co. v. ITT Rayonier, Inc.*, 696 So. 2d 334 (Fla. 1997), held comparative negligence applies, so a plaintiff who could easily have checked public records shares the fault.

**Fraud versus breach of contract.** A promise that is not kept is a breach, not a lie. Courts require proof that the defendant had no intent to perform at the moment of promising. Evidence is usually circumstantial: the defendant did nothing to perform, made the same promise to others, or concealed facts making performance impossible. Where the alleged fraud is just the contract restated, courts dismiss it. Florida's independent tort doctrine, discussed in lesson 6, holds that a fraud claim survives alongside a contract claim only if it is based on conduct independent of the breach. Fraud in the inducement, lying to get the signature, is independent. Fraud in the performance, lying while performing, usually is not.

**Merger and non-reliance clauses.** A merger clause does not bar fraud in the inducement in Florida. *Oceanic Villas, Inc. v. Godson*, 148 Fla. 454, 4 So. 2d 689 (1941). Some DCAs hold that where the contract expressly contradicts the alleged misrepresentation, reliance is not justified as a matter of law; others say the question is for the jury. Treat this as unsettled and verify the current position in your district.

**Particularity.** A complaint that says "defendant made false statements about revenue" is dismissed. One that says "on March 3, Smith emailed Jones that 2024 revenue was $1.4 million, and the tax return shows $600,000" survives.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on the elements, on the distinction between fact and opinion, and on pleading with particularity. Points to know:

- *Butler v. Yusem* and *Besett v. Basnett*: no duty to investigate before relying on a factual representation, unless falsity is obvious.
- *Gilchrist Timber*: comparative negligence applies to negligent misrepresentation.
- **Residential real estate.** *Johnson v. Davis*, 480 So. 2d 625 (Fla. 1985), imposes a duty on sellers of residential property to disclose known facts materially affecting value that are not readily observable. Florida courts have declined to extend this to commercial property, where the buyer must ask (verify).
- **Economic loss rule** no longer bars fraud claims outside products liability. *Tiara*. The independent tort doctrine still requires the fraud to be separate from the breach. Lesson 6.
- **FDUTPA** often accompanies a fraud claim. It does not require intent or reliance, only a deceptive act likely to mislead a reasonable consumer, and it carries attorney fees. Module 07.
- **Personal liability.** A corporate officer or LLC member who personally makes a fraudulent statement is personally liable in tort. Limited liability protects against the entity's contract debts, not against one's own torts.
- **Punitive damages** require a pre-pleading showing of a reasonable basis and clear and convincing evidence of intentional misconduct. § 768.72.

## Angles checklist

- What exactly was said, by whom, when, and in what form? Get the emails and texts.
- Was it a statement of existing fact, or opinion, prediction, or promise?
- Was it false when made? What is the proof of knowledge or recklessness?
- Did the plaintiff actually rely on it, and was its falsity obvious?
- Was there a duty to disclose: fiduciary, partial statement, or peculiar knowledge?
- Is this really a broken promise? What shows no intent to perform at the time?
- Is the fraud independent of the contract breach, or just the breach restated?
- Does the contract have a merger or non-reliance clause that contradicts the statement?
- Can it be pleaded with particularity?
- When was the fraud discovered? Four years from then, 12 years outer limit.
- Flip it: is there a FDUTPA claim with fee shifting? A claim against the individual speaker? Punitive damages?

## Drill

A buyer purchases a Pensacola car wash from its owner for $600,000 (verify). During negotiations the owner emails a profit-and-loss statement showing $240,000 in annual net income and says "the equipment was all replaced in 2023." The purchase agreement says the buyer "has conducted its own due diligence and is not relying on any representation not contained in this agreement," and contains no financial representations. After closing, the buyer learns actual net income was $90,000 and the equipment is from 2016. Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Statements of fact.** Both the P&L figures and the equipment date are statements of existing fact, not opinion. Both appear false.
- **Knowledge.** The owner prepared the P&L and knew the equipment age. Knowledge or recklessness is likely provable from the owner's own records.
- **Intent to induce.** Statements made during negotiations to a prospective buyer satisfy this element.
- **Justifiable reliance.** Under *Butler v. Yusem*, the buyer had no duty to audit the P&L before relying on it. The non-reliance clause is the owner's main defense. Under *Oceanic Villas*, a general merger clause does not bar fraud in the inducement. A specific non-reliance clause is stronger, and some Florida courts have held it defeats justifiable reliance as a matter of law while others leave it to the jury. The contract does not contradict the specific representations; it just disclaims reliance generally. This is the contested issue.
- **Damages.** Benefit of the bargain: the difference between the value of a car wash earning $240,000 and one earning $90,000, plus the cost of the equipment gap. Or rescission, returning the business for the price.
- **Independent tort.** The lies were made to induce the contract, not in performing it. The fraud claim is independent of any contract claim and is not barred by the economic loss rule.
- **Likely outcome.** A strong fraud claim, with the non-reliance clause the main fight. FDUTPA is a possible add-on. Punitive damages are available if the evidence of intentional deception is clear and convincing.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the P&L was labeled "unaudited estimate" and the buyer had the tax returns showing $90,000, obviousness and the non-reliance clause gain force. If the contract itself represented income and equipment age, the buyer also has a breach of warranty claim.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A supplier says "we will have the parts to you by June" and misses the date. Fraud?
2. A commercial landlord does not mention that the roof leaks. The tenant never asks. Fraud by non-disclosure?
3. Why does a fraud judgment matter more than a contract judgment of the same amount?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Not without more. It is a promise of future performance. It becomes fraud only if the supplier had no intention of performing when it spoke, which the buyer must prove with evidence beyond the missed date.
2. Probably not in a commercial lease between arm's-length parties with no fiduciary relationship, unless the landlord made a partial statement that was misleading or the leak was not discoverable by inspection and was peculiarly within the landlord's knowledge. *Johnson v. Davis* applies to residential sales.
3. Fraud supports punitive damages, is not limited by contractual caps or consequential damages waivers, can be asserted against the individual who lied, and is generally not dischargeable in bankruptcy under 11 U.S.C. § 523(a)(2).

</details>
