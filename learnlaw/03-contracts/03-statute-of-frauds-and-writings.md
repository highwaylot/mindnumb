# The statute of frauds and what counts as a writing

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~40 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Some deals are not enforceable without a signed writing, no matter how clearly both sides agreed. The list is short but it covers things a business does all the time: leases, guaranties, multi-year service deals, and sales of goods above a modest threshold. The rule cuts both ways. It can void a handshake deal you wanted to enforce, and it can free you from one you want out of. In the age of email, "writing" and "signed" mean more than they used to.

## The rule

A contract within the statute of frauds is unenforceable unless there is a writing that states its essential terms and is signed by the party against whom enforcement is sought. The statute is a defense. It does not make the contract void, only unenforceable against the party who did not sign. It must be pleaded as an affirmative defense or it is waived.

Florida's general statute is § 725.01, Fla. Stat. It covers:

1. A promise to answer for the debt, default, or miscarriage of another person. This is a **guaranty**.
2. An agreement made in consideration of marriage.
3. A contract for the sale of land or any interest in land, including a lease for more than 1 year (verify). Section 689.01 adds its own formalities for conveyances.
4. Any agreement that is not to be performed within 1 year (verify) from its making.
5. A promise by an executor or administrator to pay an estate's debt personally.

The UCC adds § 672.201: a contract for the sale of goods for $500 or more (verify) needs a writing sufficient to show a contract was made, signed by the party to be charged, and stating a quantity. Other terms can be missing or wrong. The writing is enforceable only up to the quantity stated.

**Exceptions** that take a contract out of the statute:

- **Full performance** by one party. Florida recognizes this for the one-year provision and others.
- **Part performance** for land contracts, but in Florida only in a suit for specific performance in equity, not in an action for damages. The acts must be unequivocally referable to the contract: taking possession, paying, making improvements.
- **UCC exceptions** in § 672.201(2) and (3): a written confirmation between merchants not objected to within 10 days (verify); specially manufactured goods not suitable for sale to others once the seller has begun; admission in pleadings or testimony that a contract was made; and payment made and accepted or goods received and accepted.
- **Main purpose rule** for guaranties. If the guarantor's main purpose was its own economic benefit, courts often treat the promise as original and outside the statute.

**What is a writing and a signature.** Under Florida's Uniform Electronic Transactions Act, § 668.50, a record or signature cannot be denied legal effect solely because it is electronic, and an electronic record satisfies a law requiring a writing. An electronic signature is any electronic symbol or process attached to a record and adopted with intent to sign. A typed name at the end of an email can qualify. The federal E-SIGN Act, 15 U.S.C. § 7001, says the same for interstate commerce.

## How courts reason about it

The statute was written in 1677 to prevent perjured claims of oral contracts. Courts have never liked it much, because it is also a tool for breaking real promises. So they read the categories narrowly and the exceptions generously, and they watch for a party using the statute to keep a deal's benefit while escaping its burden.

**The one-year provision** is read narrowly in most states. The question is whether performance was *possible* within a year, not whether it was expected. A lifetime employment contract is outside the statute because the employee could die within a year. A two-year lease is inside. Florida has its own gloss, below.

**Which writing, and signed by whom.** The writing need not be the contract. A memo, an invoice, a series of emails, or a check notation can do. It must come from the party being sued. This creates asymmetry: a buyer who signed a purchase order can be sued, while a seller who signed nothing cannot. Several writings can be combined if at least one is signed and they clearly relate to the same transaction.

**Email signatures.** Courts routinely hold that a name typed into an email can be a signature if the sender intended it to authenticate the message. An auto-appended signature block is weaker evidence than a name typed deliberately under "agreed."

**Equity.** Courts will order specific performance of an oral land contract if the buyer took possession and improved the land, because the acts themselves prove the contract and letting the seller keep the improvements would be a fraud.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on the categories and on electronic records. Points where Florida has its own doctrine:

- **One-year rule.** Florida courts look at whether the parties intended the agreement to extend beyond one year and whether it was in fact performable within a year. *Yates v. Ball*, 181 So. 341 (Fla. 1937) (verify citation), is the root case and is still cited. In practice Florida courts hold an oral agreement for an indefinite term that the parties expected to last years, such as a multi-year distributorship, within the statute. Indefinite-term employment is usually outside it.
- **Promissory estoppel does not override the statute.** *Tanenbaum v. Biscayne Osteopathic Hospital, Inc.*, 190 So. 2d 777 (Fla. 1966). This is a minority position and it matters.
- **Part performance is equitable only.** An oral land deal cannot support damages, only specific performance, and only on unequivocal acts of performance.
- **Leases.** Section 689.01 governs instruments creating estates in land for more than one year (verify). A 2020 amendment removed the two-witness requirement for leases (verify). Deeds still need two witnesses.
- **Guaranties.** A personal guaranty on a commercial lease or line of credit must be signed by the guarantor.
- **Credit agreements.** Under § 687.0304, a debtor cannot sue a lender on a promise to lend, extend, or modify credit above $50,000 (verify) unless the agreement is in a writing signed by both parties. Oral promises by a bank to renew are not actionable.
- **The merchant confirmation rule** in § 672.201(2) catches businesses that ignore paperwork. If a supplier sends a written confirmation of an oral order and the buyer does not object in writing within 10 days (verify), the buyer loses the statute of frauds defense even though it never signed.

## Angles checklist

- Is the contract in a statute-of-frauds category: land, lease over a year, guaranty, not performable in a year, goods of $500 or more, credit over $50,000?
- Is there any signed writing from the party you want to sue? Emails, texts, invoices, checks?
- Does the writing state the essential terms, and for goods the quantity?
- Has either side fully performed? Has the buyer of land taken possession or improved it?
- Between merchants: was there a written confirmation and did the other side object within 10 days?
- Flip it: if your side never signed, do you have the defense? Did you waive it by not pleading it?
- Is estoppel being argued to get around the statute? In Florida it cannot.
- Is there a tort claim for fraud that does not depend on enforcing the promise?

## Drill

A Fort Lauderdale marine supply company orally agrees to sell a boat dealer 40 engines at $9,000 each, delivery over the next eighteen months. The supplier emails a confirmation the next day listing the quantity, price, and schedule, signed "Best, Maria, Sales Manager." The dealer never replies. After the first 10 engines ship and are paid for, the dealer finds a cheaper source and refuses the rest. Can the supplier enforce the deal?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Is the contract within the statute?** Yes. Goods over $500, so § 672.201 controls. The UCC statute has no one-year provision (verify how Florida courts treat the overlap with § 725.01).
- **Is there a signed writing from the dealer?** No. The dealer signed nothing.
- **Merchant confirmation exception.** Both are merchants. The supplier sent a written confirmation within a reasonable time, it was sufficient against the sender, and the dealer did not object within 10 days. Under § 672.201(2) the dealer loses its statute of frauds defense. The emailed confirmation is a writing and "Best, Maria" is likely an electronic signature under § 668.50.
- **Partial performance exception.** Even without the confirmation, § 672.201(3)(c) takes the contract out of the statute for goods received and accepted, but only for the 10 engines, not the remaining 30. The confirmation rule is what reaches the full 40.
- **Likely outcome.** The contract is enforceable for all 40 engines. The dealer's refusal is a breach. The supplier's remedies are resale damages or, if it is a lost-volume seller, lost profit. See lesson 8.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the confirmation omitted the quantity, it would fail. If the dealer had objected in writing within 10 days, the defense survives.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. An LLC member orally promises a vendor that she will personally pay the LLC's invoice if the LLC does not. Enforceable against her?
2. A tenant takes possession under an oral three-year lease, pays rent, and spends money on improvements. The landlord tries to evict after six months. What can the tenant do?
3. Does an exchange of emails where the buyer writes "confirmed, 200 units at $15, ship by the 30th" satisfy § 672.201?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Generally no. A promise to answer for the debt of another must be in a signed writing under § 725.01. The main purpose rule might apply if she stood to gain personally, but that is an uphill argument for an ordinary owner.
2. Sue for specific performance in equity based on part performance: possession, payment, and improvements that are unequivocally referable to the lease. The tenant cannot recover damages at law for breach of the oral lease in Florida. If specific performance fails, unjust enrichment for the improvements is the fallback.
3. Likely yes. It is an electronic record under § 668.50, it states a quantity, and the buyer's name and email are a signature if intended to authenticate. The seller can enforce it against the buyer. Whether the buyer can enforce against the seller depends on whether the seller sent a signed writing too.

</details>
