# Drill: The lease and the buildout

**Modules exercised:** [06 Commercial leases](../06-property-ip/01-commercial-leases.md), [07 Licensing and registrations](../07-regulatory-compliance/01-licensing-and-registrations.md), [03 Remedies](../03-contracts/07-remedies.md), [08 Asset protection basics](../08-disputes-procedure/07-asset-protection-basics.md), [08 Pre-suit demand letters and notice](../08-disputes-procedure/03-pre-suit-demand-letters-and-notice.md)
**Time:** ~60 min

## Scenario

Salt & Ember LLC signed a five-year lease in March 2026 for a vacant restaurant space on Las Olas Boulevard in Fort Lauderdale. Rent is $9,000 a month starting July 1, 2026. The lease requires the tenant to open for business within 120 days of signing, gives the landlord the right to declare a default after 15 days' written notice of any non-monetary breach, and contains a personal guaranty signed by Luis, the LLC's managing member, "for all obligations of Tenant under this Lease." The lease permits the tenant's buildout and requires the tenant to "deliver the premises free of liens."

In April, Luis hired Dennis to do the $140,000 buildout. Dennis's business card said "General Contractor." Luis never checked a license. The LLC paid $70,000 in two installments. Dennis demolished the old kitchen, roughed in framing, had a plumbing supply house deliver $18,000 in fixtures and pipe, and stopped showing up in August. His phone is off. The DBPR website shows no license in his name.

On September 20 the supply house, which had served a Notice to Owner on the LLC in June, recorded a claim of lien for $18,000. On October 15 the landlord sent a notice of default for failing to open and for the lien. Today is October 29, 2026.

## Your task

List every legal issue you can see. For each: who could claim what against whom, what rule governs, what the likely outcome is, and what facts you would want to know that the scenario does not give you.

Write your answer before opening the model answer.

<summary>Model answer</summary>

**Step 1, parties.** Salt & Ember LLC (tenant, "owner" for lien purposes). Luis (member and personal guarantor, two different hats). The landlord. Dennis (unlicensed, absent). The plumbing supply house (lienor without privity). The city (permits) and DBPR (licensing).

**Step 2, timeline.** March 2026, lease and guaranty signed; opening deadline about mid-July. April, Dennis hired. June, Notice to Owner served. July 1, rent begins. August, Dennis abandons the job. September 20, lien recorded. October 15, default notice; the 15-day cure period expires about October 30. Today is October 29. One day matters.

**Steps 3 and 4.** Issues and the flip.

### Issue 1: The unlicensed contractor
- **Parties and claim:** Salt & Ember against Dennis for breach of contract, fraud, and statutory damages; Dennis against nobody.
- **Rule:** A contract entered into by an unlicensed contractor for work requiring a license is unenforceable in law or in equity by the unlicensed contractor (§ 489.128, Fla. Stat.), and the unlicensed contractor has no lien rights. Unlicensed contracting is a crime (§ 489.127). A person harmed by unlicensed contracting may recover three times actual damages (§ 768.0425, verify scope).
- **Application:** The LLC can sue Dennis for the $70,000 paid, the cost to complete above the unpaid balance, and possibly treble damages. Dennis cannot sue for anything and cannot lien.
- **Likely outcome:** An easy judgment that may be hard to collect. A DBPR complaint and police report are cheap.
- **Facts that would change it:** Whether Dennis pulled permits under someone else's license, which adds a licensee to the defendant list.

### Issue 2: The supplier's lien
- **Parties and claim:** The supply house against the LLC's leasehold, and possibly the landlord's fee; the LLC's defenses.
- **Rule:** A lienor not in privity with the owner must serve a Notice to Owner within 45 days of first furnishing (§ 713.06(2)(a)), record its claim of lien within 90 days of final furnishing (§ 713.08(5)), and sue to enforce within 1 year of recording (§ 713.22(1)). The owner can shorten that to 60 days by recording a Notice of Contest of Lien (§ 713.22(2)). A lien attaches to the interest of the person who contracted for the improvement; when a tenant contracts, it attaches to the leasehold, and reaches the landlord's fee only if the lease required the improvement or the landlord failed to record the lease or a notice disclaiming liability (§ 713.10). An owner who pays the contractor after receiving a Notice to Owner, without getting lien releases, makes an improper payment and can be made to pay twice (§ 713.06(3)). The lien can be transferred to a bond (§ 713.24). Fees go to the prevailing party in lien litigation (§ 713.29).
- **Application:** The supplier served its Notice to Owner in June and recorded within 90 days. It is probably a valid lien on the leasehold. The LLC's $70,000 to Dennis after the Notice to Owner was an improper payment, so "we already paid Dennis" is not a defense. Whether the landlord's fee is exposed depends on the lease's lien language and what was recorded.
- **Likely outcome:** The LLC owes the $18,000 or must bond it off.
- **Facts that would change it:** Dates of first and final furnishing. Whether the landlord recorded under § 713.10. Whether the amount is inflated (§ 713.31).

### Issue 3: The lease default
- **Parties and claim:** The landlord against the LLC for default, possession, and rent; the LLC's defenses.
- **Rule:** A nonresidential lease is enforced by its terms. The landlord may remove a tenant who fails to cure a non-monetary breach after 15 days' written notice, or who fails to pay rent after 3 days' notice (§ 83.20, verify). After a default, Florida courts allow a commercial landlord to choose among standing by and suing for rent as it comes due, retaking possession for the tenant's account and suing for the deficiency, or retaking for its own account and ending the tenant's liability (*Williams v. Aeroland Oil Co.*, 20 So. 2d 346 (Fla. 1944), verify citation). A commercial landlord has no statutory duty to mitigate; mitigation turns on the lease and the remedy elected (verify). The landlord has a statutory lien for rent on the tenant's property on the premises (§ 83.08).
- **Application:** The opening deadline passed and the lien breaches the "free of liens" covenant. Both are non-monetary breaches on a 15-day clock. The LLC's cure options before October 30: bond off the lien, and ask in writing for an extension of the opening date given the contractor's abandonment.
- **Likely outcome:** The landlord holds the cards, but usually prefers a paying tenant to a gutted kitchen, so an amendment with a new deadline is realistic. Without it, eviction and a deficiency claim for the remaining term, less what a replacement tenant pays.
- **Facts that would change it:** Exact notice, cure, and acceleration language. Whether rent is current.

### Issue 4: Luis's personal guaranty
- **Parties and claim:** The landlord against Luis personally.
- **Rule:** A guaranty must be in writing (§ 725.01); it is. A guaranty "for all obligations" is a continuing guaranty covering rent, damages, and fees for the whole term. Defenses: material modification of the lease without the guarantor's consent, payment, and any cap or burn-off in the guaranty itself. The LLC's limited liability (§ 605.0304) is irrelevant because Luis signed in his own name.
- **Application:** Up to about $486,000 in remaining rent through 2031 plus fees, less mitigation, sits on Luis personally. Any amendment will expressly continue the guaranty; Luis should ask for a cap or a rolling 12-month guaranty in exchange.
- **Likely outcome:** Enforceable. The exposure is real and personal.
- **Facts that would change it:** Guaranty text. Whether Luis's spouse signed. What Luis owns: homestead is protected (Fla. Const. art. X, § 4), entireties property is protected from a judgment against one spouse alone (*Beal Bank, SSB v. Almand & Associates*, 780 So. 2d 45 (Fla. 2001)), and head-of-family wages are protected under § 222.11. See [`cases/beal-bank-v-almand.md`](../cases/beal-bank-v-almand.md).

### Issue 5: Permits, licenses, and opening
- **Parties and claim:** The LLC's regulatory exposure.
- **Rule:** Construction requires permits pulled by a licensed contractor or a qualifying owner-builder (§ 489.103(7), verify); unpermitted work can be ordered opened or removed. A restaurant needs a DBPR Division of Hotels and Restaurants license, a certificate of use and local business tax receipt, and an alcohol license if it serves.
- **Application:** If Dennis pulled no permits, the completion contractor may have to redo rough-ins, which changes the budget and the timeline the LLC asks the landlord to accept.
- **Likely outcome:** More cost and delay. Check the permit history before negotiating anything.
- **Facts that would change it:** The permit record.

**Step 6, what you do not know.** The full lease and guaranty. The permit history. Whether rent is current. Dennis's real name and assets. Whether the supplier's invoices match what was delivered.

### Red herrings
- "Dennis quit, so the landlord has to let us out." A contractor's breach is the tenant's problem, not the landlord's.
- The Florida Homeowners' Construction Recovery Fund. Residential only (§ 489.140, verify).
- "We paid Dennis for those fixtures." Payment to the contractor after a Notice to Owner does not defeat the supplier's lien.
- "The LLC signed, not me." The guaranty says otherwise.
- "Dennis can lien us for the unpaid $70,000." An unlicensed contractor has no lien rights and no contract claim.

### Procedural and remedial angles
- Forum: lien foreclosure is equitable, in Broward County circuit court (Seventeenth Judicial Circuit); nonresidential eviction goes to county court under ch. 83, with damages in circuit if over $50,000 (verify); the guaranty claim and the claim against Dennis are circuit court.
- Value: $70,000 plus completion overrun against Dennis, trebled if § 768.0425 applies; $18,000 lien; six figures of rent exposure for Luis.
- Fees: § 713.29 for the lien; the lease almost certainly has a fee clause, made reciprocal by § 57.105(7); none against Dennis unless by statute.
- Limitations: lien enforcement 1 year from recording, or 60 days after a Notice of Contest; written contract 5 years (verify); statutory treble damages 4 years (verify).
- Immediate: answer the default notice in writing before the cure period ends; bond or contest the lien; file the DBPR complaint; pull the permit record; verify the completion contractor's license before signing.
