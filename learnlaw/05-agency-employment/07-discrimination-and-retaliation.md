# Discrimination and retaliation

**Module:** 05 · **Lesson:** 7 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Once you have 15 employees, federal and Florida anti-discrimination law applies to every hiring, pay, promotion, discipline, and termination decision you make. The claims are fee-shifting, they start with an agency charge you must answer, and the single most common one is not discrimination at all but retaliation: punishing someone for complaining. Knowing the thresholds, deadlines, and proof framework lets you make ordinary personnel decisions without creating a case.

## The rule

**The statutes and who they cover** (employee counts are for 20 or more calendar weeks in the current or preceding year; verify each):

| Statute | Protected classes | Employer size |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Title VII of the Civil Rights Act of 1964, 42 U.S.C. § 2000e | race, color, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation, gender identity), national origin | 15+ |
| Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA), 42 U.S.C. § 12101 | disability, and association with a disabled person | 15+ |
| Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA), 29 U.S.C. § 621 | age 40 and over | 20+ |
| Pregnant Workers Fairness Act (2023), 42 U.S.C. § 2000gg | known limitations related to pregnancy and childbirth; requires accommodation | 15+ |
| 42 U.S.C. § 1981 | race and ethnicity in contracts, including employment | any size; no charge required; 4-year limitation |
| Florida Civil Rights Act (FCRA), ch. 760, Fla. Stat. | race, color, religion, sex, pregnancy, national origin, age, handicap, marital status | 15+ |


**What is prohibited.** Discrimination in any term or condition of employment because of a protected class. Three theories:

1. **Disparate treatment.** Intentional discrimination. Proven by direct evidence (rare) or circumstantially under the *McDonnell Douglas* framework: the employee shows a prima facie case (protected class, qualified, adverse action, and circumstances suggesting discrimination such as replacement by someone outside the class or better treatment of a comparator); the employer articulates a legitimate nondiscriminatory reason; the employee proves that reason is a pretext. *McDonnell Douglas Corp. v. Green*, 411 U.S. 792 (1973). The ultimate burden stays on the employee.
2. **Harassment.** Unwelcome conduct because of a protected class that is severe or pervasive enough to alter the conditions of employment. For harassment by a supervisor that ends in a tangible employment action, the employer is strictly liable. For supervisor harassment without a tangible action, the employer has an affirmative defense if it exercised reasonable care to prevent and correct harassment and the employee unreasonably failed to use the complaint procedure. *Faragher v. City of Boca Raton*, 524 U.S. 775 (1998). For co-worker harassment, the employer is liable if it knew or should have known and failed to act.
3. **Failure to accommodate.** Under the ADA, a reasonable accommodation for a known disability unless undue hardship, reached through an interactive process. Under Title VII, accommodation of religious practice unless it imposes a substantial increased cost on the business, *Groff v. DeJoy*, 600 U.S. 447 (2023). Under the PWFA, accommodation of pregnancy-related limitations.

**Retaliation.** An employer may not take action against an employee because the employee opposed a practice made unlawful by the statute, or participated in a charge, investigation, or proceeding. The action must be materially adverse, meaning it might dissuade a reasonable worker from complaining. *Burlington Northern & Santa Fe Railway Co. v. White*, 548 U.S. 53 (2006). The employee need not prove the underlying discrimination was real, only a good-faith reasonable belief.

**Procedure.** A charge must be filed with the EEOC within 300 days of the act in Florida (verify), or with the Florida Commission on Human Relations (FCHR) within 365 days under § 760.11(1). Suit under Title VII requires a right-to-sue notice, and the complaint must be filed within 90 days of receiving it. Under the FCRA, if the FCHR finds reasonable cause or fails to decide within 180 days, the employee may sue within one year of the determination (§ 760.11(4), (5), (8)) (verify).

**Remedies.** Back pay, front pay or reinstatement, compensatory and punitive damages, fees. Title VII and ADA compensatory and punitive damages are capped by employer size: $50,000 (15 to 100 employees), $100,000 (101 to 200), $200,000 (201 to 500), $300,000 (over 500) (verify). The FCRA has no cap on compensatory damages and caps punitive damages at $100,000 (§ 760.11(5)) (verify).

## How courts reason about it

**Pretext is the whole case.** Most claims have no direct evidence, so courts ask whether the employer's stated reason is believable. Shifting explanations, deviation from written policy, treating a comparator outside the class more leniently for the same conduct, a decision-maker's remarks, and timing all support pretext. Consistent documentation that predates any complaint is the employer's best answer. A reason can be wrong or unfair and still not be pretext, as long as the employer honestly believed it.

**Comparators.** The Eleventh Circuit requires a comparator "similarly situated in all material respects," *Lewis v. City of Union City*, 918 F.3d 1213 (11th Cir. 2019) (en banc) (verify citation): same conduct, supervisor, policy, and history.

**What counts as adverse.** The Supreme Court lowered the bar in *Muldrow v. City of St. Louis*, 601 U.S. 346 (2024) (verify citation): a discriminatory transfer need only cause "some harm" to a term or condition of employment, not significant harm. For retaliation, the test remains whether a reasonable worker would be dissuaded.

**Harassment thresholds.** Courts weigh frequency, severity, whether it was threatening or humiliating, and whether it interfered with work. The *Faragher* defense fails if the employer had no policy, did not communicate it, or ignored complaints.

**Retaliation timing.** Close temporal proximity between protected activity and adverse action supports causation, but courts have said a gap of several months, by itself, is not enough. Retaliation requires but-for causation, *University of Texas Southwestern Medical Center v. Nassar*, 570 U.S. 338 (2013), a higher standard than the motivating-factor test for discrimination under Title VII.

**Why retaliation is the most common claim.** The employee need not win the underlying complaint, the adverse action standard is broader, and timing is easy to prove.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows federal law. The FCRA is patterned on Title VII and Florida courts apply federal case law to it. Departures and additions:

- **Marital status** is a protected class under the FCRA but not Title VII.
- **Pregnancy** was added to the FCRA in 2015 after *Delva v. Continental Group, Inc.*, 137 So. 3d 371 (Fla. 2014), read "sex" to include it (verify).
- **Charge deadline** is 365 days with the FCHR against 300 with the EEOC.
- **Damages**: no cap on FCRA compensatory damages, which is why plaintiffs plead the FCRA alongside Title VII.
- **Local ordinances** in Miami-Dade, Broward, Orange, and elsewhere reach employers with as few as five employees (verify locally).

## Angles checklist

- How many employees, for how many weeks? Which statutes apply, and any local ordinance?
- Protected class, and did the decision-maker know of it?
- What is the stated reason, when was it first documented, and has it changed?
- Comparators: who else did the same thing, and what happened to them?
- Any complaint, charge, or request for accommodation before the adverse action? How long before?
- Harassment: is there a written policy, was it distributed, did the employee use it, what did the employer do?
- Deadlines: 300 days EEOC, 365 days FCHR, 90 days after right-to-sue, one year under the FCRA (verify).
- Flip it: the *Faragher* defense; failure to mitigate; after-acquired evidence of misconduct, which cuts off back pay from discovery.

## Drill

Sunstate Dental, LLC has 22 employees. A hygienist tells the office manager that the lead dentist makes comments about her body and asks her to stay late alone. The office manager tells her to "try not to be alone with him." Three weeks later the hygienist asks for a schedule change for prenatal appointments and is told the practice "needs reliable people." She is fired a month after that for "attendance," which was never previously raised. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Coverage.** 22 employees: Title VII, ADA, PWFA, ADEA, and FCRA all apply.
- **Sexual harassment.** Comments about her body and pressure to stay late alone by the lead dentist, a supervisor, may be severe or pervasive. The practice's response was to shift the burden to her rather than investigate. If there was no policy or the complaint was ignored, the *Faragher* defense fails.
- **Pregnancy.** The request for prenatal appointments is a PWFA accommodation request and also puts the practice on notice of pregnancy. "Needs reliable people" is close to direct evidence of pregnancy bias.
- **Retaliation.** The harassment complaint and the accommodation request are both protected activity. Termination within two months, for a reason never raised before, is a strong retaliation case under Title VII, the PWFA, and the FCRA.
- **Procedure.** Charge with the EEOC within 300 days or FCHR within 365 days (verify). Right-to-sue, then suit within 90 days. FCRA claim for uncapped compensatory damages; Title VII damages capped at $50,000 at this employer size (verify).
- **Employer side.** Whether attendance was documented before the complaint; whether a harassment policy existed; whether the office manager escalated.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. An employee complains of discrimination that, after investigation, did not occur. The employer then fires her for making a false complaint. Risk?
2. A 12-employee Florida company fires a worker because of his race. What claim does he have?
3. Why do plaintiffs' lawyers prefer the FCRA to Title VII for damages?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. High. Retaliation protects complaints made in good faith with a reasonable belief, even if wrong. Firing for the complaint is retaliation unless the employer can prove the complaint was knowingly false, which is hard.
2. Title VII and the FCRA do not apply below 15 employees. But 42 U.S.C. § 1981 covers race discrimination in contracts with no size threshold, no charge requirement, and a four-year limitation period. A local ordinance may also apply (verify).
3. Because the FCRA has no cap on compensatory damages, while Title VII caps compensatory and punitive damages together by employer size, as low as $50,000 for small employers (verify).

</details>
