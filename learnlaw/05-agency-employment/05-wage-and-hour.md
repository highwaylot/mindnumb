# Wage and hour

**Module:** 05 · **Lesson:** 5 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Wage claims are the most common employment lawsuit against small businesses, because the rules are mechanical, the records are the employer's, damages are doubled, and the employee's lawyer gets paid by the employer. A "salaried" manager who does not meet an exemption, an off-the-clock setup routine, or a tip pool that includes a supervisor can each become a collective action.

## The rule

**Coverage.** The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), 29 U.S.C. § 201 et seq., applies to an enterprise with annual gross sales of $500,000 or more (verify) and two or more employees engaged in commerce, and to any individual employee who personally engages in interstate commerce, which includes handling goods that crossed state lines. Most Florida businesses are covered one way or the other. Florida's minimum wage law, § 448.110, Fla. Stat., and Fla. Const. art. X, § 24, borrows the FLSA's definitions of employer and employee.

**Minimum wage.** Federal: $7.25 per hour (verify). Florida: $14.00 from September 30, 2025 and $15.00 from September 30, 2026, after which it indexes to inflation (verify). The higher rate applies. Tipped employees: Florida allows a tip credit of $3.02, so the tipped cash wage is $11.98 from September 30, 2026 (verify); the employee must actually receive enough tips to reach the full minimum, and the employer must give notice of the credit.

**Overtime.** One and one-half times the regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. The regular rate includes commissions and non-discretionary bonuses. There is no daily overtime under federal law and Florida has no general overtime statute.

**Exemptions.** The employer must prove them. The main "white collar" exemptions require a salary basis, a salary at or above the threshold, and duties that fit a category:

- **Executive:** primary duty is management, regularly directs two or more full-time employees, has authority or real input on hiring and firing.
- **Administrative:** office or non-manual work directly related to management or general business operations, with discretion and independent judgment on significant matters.
- **Professional:** advanced knowledge in a field of science or learning acquired by prolonged study, or creative professionals.
- **Computer employees**, **outside sales** (no salary test), and **highly compensated employees**.

The salary threshold is $684 per week, or $35,568 a year (verify). A 2024 Department of Labor rule raised it in two steps, but a federal court vacated that rule in November 2024 and the earlier threshold is back in force (verify current status). Paying a salary is not enough: a salaried bookkeeper with no discretion over significant matters is non-exempt and owed overtime.

**Hours worked.** All time the employer knows or has reason to know the employee is working: pre-shift setup, post-shift cleanup, required training, travel between job sites, short breaks under 20 minutes, and work done remotely that the employer suffers or permits. Meal periods of 30 minutes or more, free of duties, are unpaid.

**Recordkeeping.** Employers must keep accurate records of hours worked and wages paid: payroll records for three years and time records for two years (29 C.F.R. part 516) (verify). When the employer's records are missing, the employee's reasonable estimate is accepted.

**Remedies.** Unpaid wages, an equal amount as liquidated damages unless the employer proves good faith and reasonable grounds, and attorney fees. Two years back, three if willful. Florida minimum wage claims: four years, five if willful (verify), with the same doubling and fees. Retaliation for a wage complaint is separately actionable.

## How courts reason about it

**Exemptions are construed from the text.** For decades courts read exemptions narrowly against the employer. The Supreme Court rejected that canon in *Encino Motorcars, LLC v. Navarro*, 584 U.S. 79 (2018), holding that exemptions get a fair reading, not a narrow one. The fights are about duties: whether an assistant manager "primarily" managed while stocking shelves 70% of the time, whether a loan processor exercised "discretion" or applied guidelines. What the person actually did, week by week, controls.

**Salary basis is fragile.** An exempt employee must receive the full salary for any week in which any work is done, with narrow exceptions. Docking pay for partial-day absences or for quality of work destroys the exemption for that employee, and a pattern can destroy it for the whole class. *Helix Energy Solutions Group v. Hewitt*, 598 U.S. 39 (2023), held that a highly paid worker paid a daily rate was not paid on a salary basis and was owed overtime.

**Suffer or permit.** The employer's knowledge is the test for off-the-clock work. A written policy forbidding unrecorded work does not help if managers see it happening or the schedule cannot be met without it.

**Regular rate arithmetic.** Courts recompute. A nondiscretionary bonus or commission paid for a period must be spread back across the hours in that period and overtime recalculated.

**Tips.** Under 29 U.S.C. § 203(m)(2)(B), an employer, including managers and supervisors, may not keep any part of an employee's tips, whether or not a tip credit is taken. A mandatory tip pool among non-managerial employees is allowed. The Department of Labor's "80/20" side-work rule was vacated in 2024 (verify), but a tip credit still covers only work in the tipped occupation.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the FLSA on nearly everything and adds a higher minimum wage and a longer limitation period. The departures that matter:

- **Pre-suit notice.** Under § 448.110(6), before filing a Florida minimum wage claim the employee must notify the employer in writing of the intent to sue, identifying the minimum wage claimed, the dates, and the total amount. The employer has 15 calendar days to pay (verify). If it pays, the claim is resolved. Whether this requirement can be applied to a claim brought directly under the constitutional provision has been litigated; verify the current state of the case law. The notice does not apply to federal FLSA claims.
- **No overtime statute.** An old provision, § 448.01, defines a legal day's work as 10 hours for manual labor. It is rarely used and its scope is unclear (verify).
- **Final paycheck.** Florida has no statute requiring a final paycheck on termination or within a set number of days. The FLSA requires payment on the next regular payday. Accrued vacation is owed only if the employer's policy or contract says so.
- **Deductions.** No Florida statute adds to the FLSA rule that deductions for the employer's benefit, such as uniforms or cash shortages, may not take the employee below minimum wage.
- **Poster.** Employers must post the Florida minimum wage notice (§ 448.109) alongside the federal FLSA poster.

## Angles checklist

- Is the business covered? Gross sales over $500,000, or individual employees in interstate commerce?
- For every salaried person: salary basis, threshold, and actual duties. Any docking?
- Hours worked: setup, cleanup, travel, remote email, training, meal breaks interrupted.
- Regular rate: commissions and bonuses folded in?
- Tips: who is in the pool, is anyone a manager or supervisor, was tip-credit notice given?
- Records: are time records complete, and who keeps them?
- Florida notice: was the 15-day pre-suit notice served on a Florida minimum wage claim?
- Exposure: unpaid wages, doubled, two or three years federal, four or five Florida, plus fees. Collective action if the practice was company-wide.

## Drill

A Tampa restaurant pays its servers $11.98 per hour plus tips. The kitchen manager, paid a $40,000 salary, spends most shifts on the line cooking and has no authority to hire or fire. Servers are required to arrive 30 minutes before open to roll silverware, off the clock. The tip pool includes the "shift lead," who schedules servers and can send them home. A server complains to the owner about the tip pool and is cut to one shift a week. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Servers' minimum wage.** The tipped cash wage is correct if tips bring servers to $15.00 and notice of the tip credit was given (verify rates). Thirty minutes of unpaid pre-shift work each day is hours worked; paid at the full minimum wage, not the tip credit rate, if it is non-tipped work, and it also creates overtime if weekly hours exceed 40.
- **Kitchen manager.** Salary is above $684 per week, but the primary duty is cooking, not management, and there is no hiring and firing authority. The executive exemption fails. He is owed overtime for every week over 40 hours, two or three years back, doubled.
- **Tip pool.** The shift lead schedules and sends servers home. That is supervisory authority, and a supervisor may not share in tips under § 203(m)(2)(B). The employer loses the tip credit for the affected period and must repay the tips taken, plus liquidated damages.
- **Retaliation.** Cutting the complaining server's shifts after an oral complaint is actionable under 29 U.S.C. § 215(a)(3) and § 448.110(5).
- **Procedure.** FLSA collective action in federal court is likely. A Florida claim needs the 15-day pre-suit notice.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. An employer pays a $50,000 salary to an inside sales rep with no supervisory duties. Is the rep exempt from overtime?
2. Why is the absence of time records worse for the employer than for the employee?
3. Must a Florida employer pay a fired employee on the day of termination?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Probably not. Salary alone does not create an exemption. Inside sales does not fit the outside sales exemption, and selling is not administrative work unless the duties involve discretion on matters of significance. The rep is likely owed overtime over 40 hours. The commissioned retail or service exemption under 29 U.S.C. § 207(i) is the one to check.
2. Because the employer has the legal duty to keep them. When records are missing, the employee's reasonable recollection is accepted and the employer must rebut it with specific evidence.
3. No. Florida has no final-paycheck statute. Pay is due on the next regular payday under the FLSA. A contract or policy can require more.

</details>
