# Defenses to enforcement

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 6 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Sometimes a contract was formed and still should not be enforced. The other side lied to get your signature. Both of you were wrong about a basic fact. A supplier's factory burned down. These defenses are the second half of every contract dispute. They are also how you will be attacked when you enforce your own agreements, so knowing them tells you how to paper deals so they hold.

## The rule

Defenses fall into three groups. The party asserting a defense bears the burden of proving it.

**Defects in assent.** The agreement was tainted at formation.

1. **Mutual mistake.** Both parties shared a mistaken belief about a basic assumption, the mistake materially affects the exchange, and the party seeking relief did not bear the risk of the mistake. Remedy: rescission.
2. **Unilateral mistake.** One party was mistaken. Relief is harder. Most courts require that the other party knew or should have known of the mistake, or that enforcement would be unconscionable, and that the mistaken party was not grossly negligent.
3. **Misrepresentation.** A false statement of material fact that induced assent, on which the other party justifiably relied. Fraudulent if knowing; innocent misrepresentation can also support rescission. Non-disclosure counts where there is a duty to speak.
4. **Duress.** An improper threat that leaves no reasonable alternative. Economic duress requires a wrongful act, not merely hard bargaining or financial pressure the defendant did not create.
5. **Undue influence.** Unfair persuasion by a party in a position of dominance or trust.
6. **Incapacity.** Minors, under 18 (verify), may disaffirm most contracts. Persons mentally incapable of understanding the transaction, and persons so intoxicated the other party knew it, may avoid.

**Defects in substance or legality.** The bargain itself is unacceptable.

7. **Unconscionability.** Procedural unfairness in how the contract was formed, such as hidden terms, no opportunity to read or negotiate, or gross disparity in bargaining power, plus substantive unfairness in the terms themselves. Courts may refuse to enforce the contract or strike the offending clause. UCC § 672.302, Fla. Stat.
8. **Illegality and public policy.** Contracts to do illegal acts, or that violate licensing statutes or public policy, are unenforceable. Courts leave the parties where they find them.

**Changed circumstances.** The contract was fine but the world changed.

9. **Impossibility and impracticability.** Performance has become objectively impossible or, under the UCC and the Restatement, impracticable because of an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption of the contract, and the party seeking excuse did not assume the risk. Increased cost alone is not enough. UCC § 672.615.
10. **Frustration of purpose.** Performance is possible but an unforeseen event has destroyed the purpose both parties knew the contract served.

Most of these are affirmative defenses that must be pleaded or they are waived. Rescission also requires promptly returning what was received.

## How courts reason about it

Courts start from a strong preference for enforcing contracts as written. Each defense is a narrow exception, and the opinions keep asking the same question: who should bear the risk of this problem, and did the parties already allocate it?

**Mistake** is about allocation of risk. *Raffles v. Wichelhaus* found no contract at all because each party meant a different ship Peerless and neither had reason to know of the other's meaning. Where one party should have known, the contract forms on the innocent party's understanding. In mutual mistake cases, courts ask whether the mistake went to the identity or essence of the thing, such as a cow thought to be barren, or only to its value, which is the buyer's risk. A party who contracted with conscious ignorance of a fact bears the risk of being wrong.

**Unconscionability** is the defense courts are most cautious about, because it invites judges to rewrite bargains. In *Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture*, a cross-collateral clause let the store repossess everything a customer had ever bought if she missed a payment on the latest item. Judge Wright held a clause may be unenforceable where there is an absence of meaningful choice plus terms unreasonably favorable to the other party. Courts find procedural unconscionability in take-it-or-leave-it forms, fine print, and pressure, and substantive unconscionability in terms that shock the conscience: waivers of all remedies, one-sided arbitration, grossly excessive fees. Between businesses, courts rarely find it.

**Impracticability** is usually denied. A party who makes an unconditional promise assumes the risk of market changes, cost increases, labor problems, and supplier failures. The defense succeeds for destruction of the specific subject matter, death in a personal services contract, and government prohibition. Most pandemic-era claims lost where a force majeure clause did not cover the event or where performance, such as paying rent, remained possible.

**Duress** turns on whether the threat was wrongful. Threatening to breach unless the other side agrees to a modification can be duress if the victim had no reasonable alternative. Threatening to sue, or to decline future business, is not.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on mistake, misrepresentation, duress, incapacity, illegality, impossibility, and frustration. Points to know:

- **Unilateral mistake.** *Maryland Casualty Co. v. Krasnek*, 174 So. 2d 541 (Fla. 1965), allows rescission for a unilateral mistake if the mistake did not result from an inexcusable lack of due care, the other party has not relied to its detriment, and rescission would not be unconscionable. Florida is somewhat more generous here than many states.
- **Unconscionability** requires both procedural and substantive elements, applied on a sliding scale: more of one means less of the other is needed. *Basulto v. Hialeah Automotive*, 141 So. 3d 1145 (Fla. 2014). The question goes to the court, not a jury.
- **Unlicensed contractors.** Under § 489.128, a construction contract entered by a contractor who lacks the required license is unenforceable by the contractor. The homeowner or customer keeps the benefit. Similar rules apply in other licensed trades. This is illegality with real teeth.
- **Age of majority** is 18 under § 743.07 (verify).
- **Fraud in the inducement** survives a merger clause under *Oceanic Villas, Inc. v. Godson*, 148 Fla. 454, 4 So. 2d 689 (1941), with the limits discussed in lesson 4.
- **Economic duress** in Florida requires a wrongful act by the party asserting the contract that caused the pressure. Pre-existing financial trouble is not duress.
- **Impossibility** in Florida is narrow. Courts refuse the defense where the event was foreseeable at contracting and the contract is silent, on the theory that the promisor could have bargained for protection. A force majeure clause is read as the parties' own allocation of these risks and generally displaces the common law doctrine within its scope.

## Angles checklist

- Was anyone lied to, or kept in the dark where there was a duty to speak?
- Were both parties wrong about a basic fact? Who bore that risk under the contract?
- Was there any wrongful pressure, or a relationship of trust that was abused?
- Was the contract a form the other side could not read or negotiate? Is any term shockingly one-sided?
- Does any party need a license to do this work? Does it have one?
- What changed after signing? Was it foreseeable? Is there a force majeure clause and what does it list?
- Did the party asserting the defense act promptly and return what it received?
- Flip it: did the other side ratify the contract by continuing to perform after learning of the problem? Ratification waives most assent defenses.

## Drill

A Naples event company contracts with a hotel to host a three-day conference for $90,000 (verify) with a 50% non-refundable deposit. Two months before the event, the county issues an emergency order banning gatherings over 50 people for 30 days, covering the conference dates. The contract's force majeure clause lists "acts of God, war, strikes, and natural disasters" and says the affected party is excused. The event company demands its deposit back. The hotel refuses. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Force majeure.** Is a government gathering ban within the clause? It is not listed, and the clause has no catch-all. Florida courts read these clauses narrowly. The hotel will argue the list is exclusive. The event company will argue a pandemic-driven order is an act of God. The text probably favors the hotel.
- **Impossibility or illegality.** Hosting a conference of several hundred people is prohibited by law on those dates. Performance by the hotel is objectively impossible and illegal. This is one of the classic categories for excuse. Both parties are discharged from further performance.
- **Frustration of purpose.** The event company could still pay, but the purpose both parties knew the contract served, a conference, is destroyed. Also supports discharge.
- **The deposit.** When a contract is discharged, each side generally must restore what it received to the extent the other side conferred a benefit. The "non-refundable" label governs breach and cancellation, not discharge by impossibility, unless it clearly allocates that risk. Restitution of the deposit, less the hotel's reasonable reliance costs, is the likely result.
- **Likely outcome.** The contract is discharged. The event company probably recovers most of the deposit under restitution. The hotel keeps documented out-of-pocket costs.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the clause said "including government orders" and allocated the deposit to the hotel in that event, the hotel keeps it. If the order had expired before the conference, performance was possible and cancellation would be a breach.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A buyer signs a contract for a used excavator "as is." Both parties believe the engine was rebuilt last year; it was not. Mutual mistake?
2. An unlicensed contractor finishes a $40,000 renovation and the owner refuses to pay. Can the contractor sue in Florida?
3. A supplier's costs triple after a tariff. Can it refuse to deliver on impracticability grounds?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Probably not. "As is" allocates the risk of the equipment's condition to the buyer. A party who agrees to take something as is bears the risk of mistakes about its condition. The result might differ if the seller made an affirmative representation about the rebuild, which would be misrepresentation.
2. Generally no. Section 489.128 makes the contract unenforceable by the unlicensed contractor, and courts have refused unjust enrichment claims that would let it recover through the back door (verify current case law).
3. Almost certainly not. Increased cost, even a large increase, is not impracticability. A tariff is a foreseeable commercial risk. The supplier should have bargained for a price adjustment clause. Section 672.615 requires an event whose non-occurrence was a basic assumption, and price changes are not that.

</details>
