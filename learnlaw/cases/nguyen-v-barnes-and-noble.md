# Nguyen v. Barnes & Noble Inc., 763 F.3d 1171 (9th Cir. 2014)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · online contracts · browsewrap · notice · arbitration
**Used in:** [`03-contracts/09-online-and-electronic-contracts.md`](../03-contracts/09-online-and-electronic-contracts.md), [`03-contracts/01-formation.md`](../03-contracts/01-formation.md), [`08-disputes-procedure/05-mediation-and-arbitration.md`](../08-disputes-procedure/05-mediation-and-arbitration.md)

## One-line takeaway

A website's terms of use do not bind a user who never clicked to accept them and had no actual notice, even if a link to the terms appears on every page near the buttons the user clicked.

## Facts

In August 2011 Barnes & Noble ran a "fire sale" on discontinued HP TouchPad tablets through its website. Kevin Nguyen ordered two and received an email confirming the order. The next day he received an email cancelling it because of unexpectedly high demand.

The Barnes & Noble website had a "Terms of Use" hyperlink in the bottom left corner of every page and near the buttons a customer clicked to complete a purchase. The terms said that using the site meant the user agreed to them, and they contained an arbitration clause. Nothing on the site required a customer to click "I agree" or to view the terms before buying. Nguyen testified he never saw them.

## Procedural posture

Nguyen filed a class action in California state court alleging deceptive business practices and false advertising under California and New York law. Barnes & Noble removed the case to the Central District of California and moved to compel arbitration under the terms of use. The district court denied the motion, finding no agreement to arbitrate. Barnes & Noble appealed to the Ninth Circuit.

## Issue

Does a "browsewrap" agreement, where terms are available by hyperlink but the user takes no affirmative act to accept them, bind a user who had no actual knowledge of the terms?

## Holding

No. The Ninth Circuit affirmed. Where a website makes its terms available by a conspicuous hyperlink on every page but gives no other notice and does not prompt the user to take any affirmative action to show assent, the user is not bound, even when the hyperlink sits close to the buttons the user must click to proceed. The court reached the same result under California and New York law.

## Reasoning

The court began with the basic rule that contracts require mutual assent, which online is measured by whether the user had reasonable notice of the terms. It sorted online agreements into two types. A clickwrap requires the user to click a box or button stating agreement. A browsewrap simply posts terms and declares that use of the site means acceptance. Enforcing a browsewrap turns on whether the user had actual or constructive knowledge of the terms.

Nguyen had no actual knowledge. So the question was constructive notice: would a reasonably prudent user have known the terms existed? The court surveyed the cases. Where the link is buried at the bottom of a page or visible only by scrolling, courts refuse enforcement. Where the site also tells the user, next to the button being clicked, that clicking means agreeing to the terms, courts enforce. Barnes & Noble's site did neither. It had a visible link but no statement anywhere telling a buyer that completing a purchase bound the buyer to the terms.

The court rejected the argument that proximity to the checkout buttons was enough. A link alone, without a prompt, does not put a consumer on notice. It also rejected the argument that Nguyen's experience with other websites' terms meant he should have known.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The strongest counterpoint comes from *Register.com, Inc. v. Verio, Inc.*, 356 F.3d 393 (2d Cir. 2004), where a sophisticated repeat user with actual knowledge of the terms was bound by continued use. Actual knowledge changes the result.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Every website and app that relies on terms of service needs to decide how it obtains assent. This case is the reason to use a clickwrap or at least a sign-in wrap: an unchecked box next to "I agree to the Terms of Service," or a statement directly above the "Place order" button that placing the order means agreeing to the linked terms. A footer link does not do it. The terms most at stake are arbitration clauses, class action waivers, limitation of liability, choice of law, and forum selection. If assent fails, all of them fall with it. Keep screenshots and version history so you can prove what the user saw on the date of the transaction.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the same approach. Florida courts ask whether the user had reasonable notice of the terms and manifested assent, and treat clickwrap as the safe method. In *Vitacost.com, Inc. v. McCants*, 210 So. 3d 761 (Fla. 4th DCA 2017) (verify citation), the Fourth DCA refused to compel arbitration where the terms were available only through a link and the customer was not required to view or accept them. The Florida Uniform Electronic Transaction Act, § 668.50, Fla. Stat., makes electronic assent effective but does not create assent where none was given. Arbitration clauses in Florida are enforced under the Federal Arbitration Act and the Revised Florida Arbitration Code, ch. 682, Fla. Stat., but only after the party seeking arbitration proves an agreement to arbitrate exists.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Does the outcome change? Suppose the checkout page said, directly above the "Submit order" button, "By placing your order you agree to our Terms of Use," with the phrase hyperlinked. Same result?
2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What evidence about Nguyen specifically could have established actual notice?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? Your app updates its terms by posting a new version and emailing a notice. A user keeps using the app. Is the user bound by the new arbitration clause?
