# UCC Article 2: sales of goods

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 8 · **Time:** ~55 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

If you buy or sell physical things, a different rulebook applies. Article 2 of the Uniform Commercial Code, enacted in Florida as chapter 672, overrides common law contract rules for goods. It implies warranties you never wrote, gives buyers a right to reject imperfect deliveries, gives sellers a right to cure, and sets its own damages and limitation rules. A business that sells goods on a one-page invoice is making promises it never typed.

## The rule

**Scope.** Article 2 applies to transactions in goods: movable things, including specially manufactured goods. § 672.102, § 672.105, Fla. Stat. For mixed contracts of goods and services, most courts use the predominant purpose test: if the main thrust is the goods, Article 2 governs the whole deal.

**Merchants.** A merchant deals in goods of the kind or holds itself out as having skill in them. § 672.104. Several rules are stricter for merchants: firm offers, the confirmation exception to the statute of frauds, the implied warranty of merchantability, and the battle of the forms.

**Formation.** A contract can form in any manner that shows agreement, even with open terms. § 672.204. Open price, delivery, and payment terms are filled by statute. §§ 672.305 to 672.310. Acceptance with different terms is still acceptance. § 672.207. Modifications need no consideration. § 672.209. Writing required at $500 (verify). § 672.201.

**Warranties.**

- **Express** (§ 672.313): any affirmation of fact, promise, description, or sample that becomes part of the basis of the bargain. No magic words needed. Puffery and opinion do not count.
- **Implied warranty of merchantability** (§ 672.314): goods sold by a merchant must be fit for their ordinary purpose and pass without objection in the trade.
- **Implied warranty of fitness for a particular purpose** (§ 672.315): when the seller knows the buyer's particular purpose and that the buyer is relying on the seller's skill to pick suitable goods.
- **Disclaimers** (§ 672.316): merchantability can be disclaimed only by language that mentions merchantability and, if written, is conspicuous. Fitness can be disclaimed by a conspicuous writing. "As is" or "with all faults" disclaims all implied warranties. Express warranties cannot be meaningfully disclaimed.

**Performance.** The **perfect tender rule** lets a buyer reject goods that fail in any respect to conform to the contract. § 672.601. The seller may **cure** a defective tender if time for performance has not run, or if the seller reasonably believed the tender would be acceptable. § 672.508. Installment contracts are governed by substantial impairment, not perfect tender. § 672.612. A buyer who accepts goods may **revoke acceptance** only if a nonconformity substantially impairs value and was hard to discover or the seller promised to cure. § 672.608. A buyer must inspect within a reasonable time and give notice of breach or lose the claim. § 672.607(3).

**Buyer's remedies** (§§ 672.711 to 672.715): cancel; recover the price paid; **cover** by buying substitute goods and recovering the difference; or recover market-minus-contract damages; plus incidental and consequential damages, including foreseeable lost profits and injury to person or property caused by a breach of warranty. Accepted goods: the difference between the value as warranted and as delivered. § 672.714.

**Seller's remedies** (§§ 672.703 to 672.710): withhold delivery; stop delivery in transit; resell and recover the difference; recover market damages; recover lost profit if resale would not make the seller whole, which protects the **lost volume seller**; or recover the price for accepted goods or goods that cannot be resold.

**Agreed remedies.** Parties may limit remedies, for example to repair or replacement, and exclude consequential damages. § 672.719. If the limited remedy fails of its essential purpose, the buyer gets the general remedies. Excluding consequential damages for personal injury from consumer goods is prima facie unconscionable.

**Limitation period.** Four years (verify) from when the breach occurs, which for warranty is tender of delivery, regardless of the buyer's knowledge. Parties may shorten it to not less than 1 year (verify) but may not extend it. § 672.725.

**Risk of loss** passes under §§ 672.509 and 672.510, generally on delivery to the carrier for shipment contracts and on tender at destination for destination contracts.

## How courts reason about it

Article 2 was drafted to match how merchants actually behave, and courts read it that way. Where the common law asks "was there a mirror-image acceptance," Article 2 asks "did these businesses think they had a deal and act on it." It gives the buyer a sharp rejection right but balances it with the seller's right to cure and the buyer's duty of good faith.

- **Express warranty disputes** turn on whether a statement was fact or puffery and whether it became part of the basis of the bargain. "This press will run 500 sheets a minute" is a warranty. "This is the best press on the market" is puffery. Specifications in a quote, website descriptions, and samples all create warranties.
- **Disclaimers** are read strictly against the seller. Fine print on the back of an invoice, delivered after the deal is made, often fails. A conspicuous "AS IS" in the signed order works.
- **Perfect tender** is softened by good faith. A buyer who rejects over a trivial defect to escape a bad price can be found to have acted in bad faith. Rejection must be timely and particularized so the seller can cure.
- **Failure of essential purpose.** A repair-or-replace remedy fails when the seller cannot make the goods work after reasonable attempts. Courts split on whether a separate consequential damages exclusion also falls; the majority treats the two clauses independently.
- **Lost volume seller.** A dealer with ample supply who resells the cancelled unit to another customer still lost a sale, because it would have made both sales. Courts award lost profit on the cancelled sale.

## Florida specifics

Florida enacted Article 2 as chapter 672 without major departures. Section numbers track the uniform act with "672." replacing "2-".

- **Privity.** Florida requires privity of contract for implied warranty claims. A buyer cannot sue the manufacturer for breach of implied warranty when it bought from a dealer, though strict products liability and negligence remain. *Kramer v. Piper Aircraft Corp.*, 520 So. 2d 37 (Fla. 1988). This is a minority position in some respects (verify current status and exceptions).
- **Economic loss rule.** A buyer whose defective product damages only itself is limited to contract and warranty remedies. See module 04, lesson 6.
- **Statute of limitations.** Four years (verify) under § 672.725, not the five-year written contract period in § 95.11. Courts apply § 672.725 even if the contract was written.
- **Consumer overlay.** Federal Magnuson-Moss Warranty Act, 15 U.S.C. § 2301 and following, governs written warranties on consumer products and limits disclaimers of implied warranties when a written warranty is given. FDUTPA (module 07) adds a deceptive-practices claim for misrepresented goods.

## Angles checklist

- Is this goods, services, or mixed? What is the predominant purpose?
- Is each party a merchant? Which merchant rules apply?
- What statements did the seller make, in writing or orally, that could be express warranties?
- Is there a conspicuous disclaimer, and when was it delivered?
- Did the buyer inspect and give timely notice of defects?
- Did the buyer reject, accept, or revoke, and were the steps done right?
- Did the seller offer to cure?
- What is the remedy clause? Has it failed of its essential purpose?
- Is the claim within 4 years of tender?
- Flip it: can the buyer be charged with bad faith rejection, or the seller with a non-conforming tender it should have known about?

## Drill

A Miami restaurant buys a commercial walk-in cooler from a dealer for $18,000 (verify). The dealer's quote says "holds 34 to 38 degrees under full load." The signed invoice says, in bold capitals, "SOLD AS IS. NO WARRANTIES." The cooler runs at 44 degrees. The dealer sends a technician twice; it still runs warm. Three months later the restaurant loses $9,000 in spoiled inventory and sues. Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Scope.** A cooler is goods. Both the dealer and the restaurant are merchants, though the restaurant is not a merchant in coolers.
- **Express warranty.** The temperature statement in the quote is an affirmation of fact that became part of the basis of the bargain. It is an express warranty under § 672.313. The "AS IS" language cannot disclaim an express warranty; § 672.316(1) says words of disclaimer that cannot be reconciled with an express warranty are inoperative. The restaurant has a breach of express warranty claim.
- **Implied warranties.** The conspicuous "AS IS" effectively disclaims merchantability and fitness under § 672.316(3). Those claims are gone.
- **Acceptance and revocation.** The restaurant accepted the cooler and used it. It can revoke acceptance under § 672.608 if the warm-running substantially impairs value and the failed repairs were an unfulfilled cure. Three months with two failed repairs is plausible. The service calls likely satisfy the notice requirement of § 672.607(3).
- **Damages.** Under § 672.714, the difference between the value as warranted and as delivered, or on revocation the price paid. The $9,000 spoilage is consequential damage under § 672.715(2)(b), injury to property proximately resulting from breach of warranty. It is recoverable unless the contract excluded consequential damages, and the invoice here did not.
- **Mitigation.** The dealer will argue a restaurant that knew the cooler ran at 44 degrees for three months should not have kept perishable inventory in it. That cuts the spoilage claim.
- **Likely outcome.** Express warranty claim succeeds. Recovery of the price difference or the price on revocation, plus spoilage reduced for failure to mitigate.
- **Facts that would change it.** A conspicuous consequential damages exclusion would cut the spoilage claim. If the temperature statement appeared only in marketing the restaurant never saw, it did not become part of the bargain.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A supplier delivers 1,000 units, 20 of which are the wrong color. Can the buyer reject the whole shipment?
2. A buyer discovers a defect three years after delivery and sues four and a half years after delivery. Timely?
3. A dealer cancels a buyer's order for a standard forklift and the dealer resells it next week at the same price. Does the dealer have damages?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Under the perfect tender rule, yes, if this is a single-delivery contract and rejection is made in good faith within a reasonable time with notice of the defect. The seller then has a right to cure if time remains. In an installment contract, the buyer can reject only for substantial impairment.
2. No. Section 672.725 runs 4 years from tender of delivery regardless of discovery, unless the warranty explicitly extends to future performance. The claim is time-barred even though the defect was hidden.
3. Yes, if the dealer is a lost volume seller: it could have sold a forklift to both customers and therefore lost the profit on one sale. Section 672.708(2) awards lost profit plus incidental damages where the resale measure does not make the seller whole.

</details>
