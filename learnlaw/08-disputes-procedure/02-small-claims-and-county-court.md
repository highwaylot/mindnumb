# Small claims and county court

**Module:** 08 · **Lesson:** 2 · **Time:** ~40 min
**Tags:** `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Most business disputes are small: a $4,000 invoice, a deposit a vendor kept, a disputed $1,200 repair. These belong in small claims court, where the rules are short, a business can appear through its own officer or employee, and judgment can come in two to three months. Above $8,000, the case is in county court under the full civil rules and the business needs a lawyer.

## The rule

**County court jurisdiction (§ 34.01).** Civil cases where the amount in controversy does not exceed $50,000 (verify), exclusive of interest, costs, and attorney fees. Above that, circuit court. Since January 1, 2021 (verify), appeals from county court go to the District Court of Appeal, not the circuit court.

**Small claims (Fla. Sm. Cl. R. 7.010).** Civil actions in county court where the demand does not exceed $8,000 (verify), exclusive of costs, interest, and attorney fees. The Small Claims Rules govern; the Rules of Civil Procedure apply only if the court orders (Rule 7.020).

**Starting the case.** The plaintiff files a statement of claim, a short form stating the claim and the amount (Rule 7.050). Filing fees are tiered by amount under § 34.041: roughly $55 (verify) for claims up to $100, $80 (verify) up to $500, $175 (verify) up to $2,500, and $300 (verify) up to $8,000. County court claims above $8,000 cost about $300 (verify) up to $15,000 and $400 (verify) above.

**Service.** By sheriff or certified process server, or by certified mail with return receipt signed by the defendant in small claims (Rule 7.070) (verify).

**Who may appear (Rule 7.050(a)(2)).** A party may appear without a lawyer. A corporation may be represented by an officer or by an employee authorized in writing by an officer. The rule's text names corporations; courts apply it to LLCs through their managers or members in practice (verify). Outside small claims, an entity must appear through a licensed attorney.

**Pretrial conference (Rule 7.090).** The summons sets a pretrial conference, usually four to eight weeks (verify) out. If the defendant does not appear, default. If the plaintiff does not, dismissal. The judge or a mediator tries to settle the case on the spot; most counties send small claims cases to mediation at the pretrial. If no settlement, the court sets trial, often within 60 days (verify).

**No formal answer required.** The defendant need not file a written answer; appearing at pretrial is the response. Counterclaims should be filed in writing at least five days (verify) before pretrial (Rule 7.100). A counterclaim over $8,000 moves the case out of small claims.

**Discovery.** Not available as of right. Only by court order on a showing of need (Rule 7.020(c)) (verify).

**Trial and judgment.** Informal. Jury trial is available if demanded (Rule 7.150). Judgment is entered on a form that includes the debtor's address and instructions to complete a fact information sheet (Rule 7.221 and Form 7.343) if the judgment is unpaid.

**Default (Rules 7.170, 1.500).** In small claims, failure to appear at pretrial is a default, and the plaintiff can get judgment on liquidated damages at once. In county court civil, failure to serve any paper within 20 days (verify) leads to a clerk's default, then a motion for default judgment with a hearing if damages are unliquidated.

**Fees and costs.** The American rule applies. Fees are recoverable only under a contract clause (reciprocal under § 57.105(7)), a statute such as FDUTPA § 501.2105 or the minimum wage act, or a proposal for settlement under § 768.79, which applies in small claims because it is substantive law (verify). Costs under § 57.041 go to the prevailing party.

**Burden.** Same as any civil case: the plaintiff proves each element by a preponderance; the defendant proves affirmative defenses.

## How courts reason about it

- **Jurisdiction is by the demand, not the recovery.** A plaintiff may not split a $12,000 claim into two $6,000 cases. But a plaintiff may deliberately waive the excess and sue for $8,000 to stay in small claims; the waived amount is gone.
- **Informality has limits.** Small claims judges relax evidence rules but still require the plaintiff to prove the debt. A printed invoice and a witness who knows the account usually suffice. Hearsay from a bookkeeper who did not create the record can be rejected.
- **Entity representation.** Judges enforce the line between small claims and county court on representation. The managing member who handles a $7,000 claim may not handle the $9,000 claim, and a filing by a non-lawyer on behalf of an entity in county court is a nullity.
- **Defaults are set aside readily but not automatically.** Florida policy favors deciding cases on the merits. A defendant with excusable neglect, a meritorious defense, and prompt action usually gets a default vacated under Rule 1.540(b). "I was busy" is not excusable neglect; "the registered agent never forwarded it" sometimes is.
- **Mediation at pretrial is where most cases end.** Judges set pretrials in batches and send parties to a hallway mediator. The pressure to settle is real because trial is weeks away and both sides have taken a morning off.
- **Counterclaims change the forum.** A customer sued for $3,000 who counterclaims for $20,000 in alleged defective work moves the whole case to county court civil, and both entities now need lawyers.

## Florida specifics

- Florida's $8,000 small claims ceiling is near the national middle; some states go to $10,000 or more. The $50,000 county court ceiling was raised from $30,000 in 2023 (verify).
- The Small Claims Rules' permission for a corporation to appear through an officer or authorized employee is Florida's departure from the general rule that entities need lawyers.
- Florida small claims courts can issue the full set of collection tools after judgment: fact information sheet, writs, garnishment (lesson 6).
- A FDUTPA or minimum wage claim within the $8,000 limit still carries its statutory fee provision, which can exceed the claim.

## Angles checklist

- Amount: under $8,000 exclusive of interest, costs, and fees? Under $50,000? Any waiver of the excess?
- Who is the defendant: individual or entity, and who is the registered agent?
- Who will appear for the business, and do they have written authorization and personal knowledge?
- Documents: contract, invoices, statements, photos, texts, in order, with a witness who can authenticate them.
- Counterclaim risk: what will the defendant say, and does it push the case above $8,000?
- Fees: contract clause, statute, or proposal for settlement? Is the fee exposure bigger than the claim?
- Venue: county where the defendant resides, where the cause of action accrued, or where the property is (§ 47.011); any contractual venue clause?
- Collectability: does the defendant have assets, a job, a bank account? Is it an individual with exemptions?
- Limitation: four years on an open account or oral contract, five on a written one.

## Drill

Keys Pool Service LLC did monthly maintenance for a homeowner for 14 months under a one-page signed agreement with a "prevailing party pays attorney fees" clause. The homeowner stopped paying and owes $6,400. She says the pump failed because of Keys' neglect and it cost her $9,500 to replace. Keys' office manager wants to file in small claims and handle it herself. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Forum.** $6,400 is within small claims. The office manager may appear if an officer or manager authorizes her in writing and she knows the account.
- **Counterclaim.** The homeowner's $9,500 pump claim arises from the same agreement and is compulsory. If she files it, the case exceeds $8,000 and transfers to county court civil, where Keys needs a lawyer.
- **Fees.** The clause is reciprocal under § 57.105(7). If Keys wins, it recovers fees; if the homeowner wins on the counterclaim, she recovers hers. In a $6,400 case, fees can exceed the claim. A proposal for settlement can add pressure after the 90-day window.
- **Proof.** Signed agreement, monthly invoices, service logs, and a technician who can say what was done and that the pump failure was not neglect.
- **Collectability.** An individual homeowner with homestead and possibly head-of-family status may be hard to collect from. A lien on the homestead is not available. Wages may be exempt.
- **Limitation.** Written contract, five years. No problem.
- **Red herring.** The homeowner's complaint about the pump is not a defense to the entire invoice balance unless it is a prior material breach; it is a setoff or counterclaim.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the homeowner does not counterclaim and only argues setoff at pretrial, the case stays in small claims and the judge can net the amounts.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A business has a $9,500 claim. Can it sue for $8,000 in small claims to avoid hiring a lawyer?
2. The defendant in a small claims case does not show up at the pretrial conference. What happens?
3. Why does a counterclaim over $8,000 take the case out of small claims?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Yes, by waiving the excess. The $1,500 is forfeited; it cannot be sued for later because of claim splitting rules.
2. Default. The court may enter judgment at once if the damages are liquidated, such as an invoice amount. The defendant can move to set it aside for excusable neglect with a meritorious defense, but has the burden.
3. Because small claims jurisdiction is defined by the amount in controversy, which includes compulsory counterclaims. Once the controversy exceeds the ceiling, the Small Claims Rules no longer govern and the case proceeds under the civil rules in county court, with the attorney requirement for entities.

</details>
