# Fiduciary duties and the business judgment rule

**Module:** 02 · **Lesson:** 4 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Once you run a company with other people's money in it, you owe them duties that do not depend on any contract. A manager who steers a deal to his own side company, a majority member who pays herself a salary and never declares a distribution, a partner who quietly bids on the firm's next project: each is a fiduciary duty case. The business judgment rule is the other half: an honest, informed decision that turns out badly is protected from second-guessing.

## The rule

In a Florida LLC the duties are in § 605.04091, Fla. Stat. A member of a member-managed LLC, and a manager of a manager-managed LLC, owes the company and the other members the **duty of loyalty** and the **duty of care**, § 605.04091(1). A non-manager member of a manager-managed LLC owes no duties solely by reason of being a member, § 605.04091(8) (verify subsection).

**Duty of loyalty**, § 605.04091(2). The fiduciary must:

1. account to the company and hold as trustee any property, profit, or benefit derived in the conduct or winding up of the business, from use of company property, or from appropriation of a **company opportunity**;
2. refrain from dealing with the company as or on behalf of a party with an adverse interest, in the conduct or winding up of the business;
3. refrain from competing with the company before its dissolution.

**Duty of care**, § 605.04091(3). The fiduciary must refrain from grossly negligent or reckless conduct, willful or intentional misconduct, or a knowing violation of law. Ordinary negligence is not a breach.

**Good faith and fair dealing**, § 605.04091(4). All duties are discharged consistently with the contractual obligation of good faith and fair dealing. A fiduciary does not violate a duty merely because its conduct also furthers its own interest, § 605.04091(5).

**Consent cures.** After full disclosure of all material facts, all members may authorize or ratify a specific act that would otherwise violate the duty of loyalty, § 605.04091(6). The operating agreement may also narrow the duties within § 605.0105's limits, as lesson 3 describes.

The plaintiff bears the burden of proving a breach. Where the fiduciary stood on both sides of a transaction, the burden shifts to the fiduciary to prove the transaction was fair.

**The business judgment rule.** A court will not second-guess a business decision made by a disinterested fiduciary, on an informed basis, in good faith, and in the honest belief it was in the company's best interest. The plaintiff must rebut one of those elements before the court examines the merits of the decision. The rule is a presumption, not a defense the fiduciary must prove. Florida applies it to corporate directors under §§ 607.0830 and 607.0831 and to LLC managers through the gross negligence standard of § 605.04091(3).

**Who sues.** A member may sue directly for an injury to the member personally, or derivatively on behalf of the LLC for an injury to the company, §§ 605.0801 to 605.0806. A derivative plaintiff must first demand that the company sue, or plead why demand would be futile, § 605.0802, and the LLC may appoint a special litigation committee to decide whether the claim should proceed, § 605.0804. Any recovery on a derivative claim belongs to the LLC.

## How courts reason about it

Four cases carry most of the doctrine, and each shows a different pressure point.

**Loyalty is strict.** In *Meinhard v. Salmon*, Salmon managed a hotel lease as a joint venture with Meinhard's money. When the landlord offered Salmon a new, larger lease on adjacent property near the end of the term, Salmon took it for himself without telling Meinhard. Cardozo held he had to share it. The standard was not the morals of the marketplace but "the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive." The reasoning: the opportunity came to Salmon because of the venture, and a co-venturer who learns of it owes at least disclosure, so the other can bid too. Courts ever since have asked whether the opportunity arrived through the fiduciary role, and whether the fiduciary disclosed before acting. Secrecy is almost always fatal.

**Opportunity has a test.** *Guth v. Loft* refined the rule for corporations. Guth, Loft's president, acquired the Pepsi-Cola formula and trademark personally while using Loft's facilities, employees, and money. Delaware held it was Loft's opportunity. The factors: the company was financially able to take it; it was in the company's line of business; the company had an interest or expectancy in it; and the fiduciary's taking it put him in conflict with the company. Section 605.04091(2)(a) codifies the concept without factors, and Florida courts borrow *Guth*'s.

**Care is about process, not outcome.** *Smith v. Van Gorkom* is the warning. Trans Union's board approved a cash-out merger at $55 a share after a two-hour meeting, with no written summary, no valuation, and the price set by the chairman's rough calculation. The deal was a premium over market. Delaware still found the directors grossly negligent because they were uninformed. The business judgment rule protects decisions, not the absence of a decision process. Courts look for what the fiduciaries had in front of them: financials, advice, time, alternatives. A bad outcome with a good record is protected; a good outcome with no record is not.

**Judgment is protected, but there are limits.** *Dodge v. Ford Motor Co.* shows both halves. Henry Ford stopped paying special dividends to fund expansion and lower prices, saying the company had made too much money. The Michigan court ordered the dividend because Ford had framed the decision as charity rather than business. But it refused to enjoin the River Rouge expansion, because how to grow the company was a business judgment. The reasoning that survives: a fiduciary who states a non-business purpose loses the presumption; a fiduciary who states a business purpose, even a debatable one, keeps it.

The edges courts fight over in LLC cases: whether salary to a working majority member is compensation or a disguised distribution that starves the minority; whether a side business is competing or merely adjacent; whether a transaction between the LLC and a member's other company was fair in price and process; and whether a member's claim is direct or derivative. Florida's test for the last is from *Dinuro Investments, LLC v. Camacho*, 141 So. 3d 731 (Fla. 3d DCA 2014): a claim is direct only if the member suffered harm separate from the other members and the defendant owed a duty to the member separate from the duty to the company, unless the operating agreement provides otherwise.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the uniform act's approach of codified duties with contractual flexibility. The gross negligence standard of care is more protective of managers than the ordinary-negligence standard some states use.
- For corporations, § 607.0830 sets the director standard: good faith, with the care an ordinarily prudent person in a like position would exercise, in a manner reasonably believed to be in the corporation's best interests. Section 607.0831 then limits personal liability to cases involving a criminal violation, an improper personal benefit, an unlawful distribution, conscious disregard of the corporation's interest, or recklessness or bad faith. The practical effect is close to the LLC standard.
- Direct versus derivative in Florida LLCs is governed by *Dinuro*, which is stricter than many states and often forces a minority member into a derivative suit with its demand requirement.
- Breach of fiduciary duty is a four-year claim under § 95.11(3) (verify).
- The duty of loyalty ends at dissociation except as to matters already pending, § 605.0603 (verify).

## Angles checklist

- Who is a fiduciary here: member of a member-managed LLC, manager, or both? Is anyone a mere non-manager member?
- Did an opportunity come to someone through the company? Was it disclosed before it was taken?
- Is anyone on both sides of a transaction? Was the price and process fair, and who bears the burden?
- Is anyone competing with the company while still a member or manager?
- Was the challenged decision informed? What was in front of the decision-maker, and is there a record?
- Was the purpose stated a business purpose?
- Does the operating agreement narrow or expand any duty, or carve out this activity?
- Did all members consent after full disclosure?
- Is the claim direct or derivative, and has demand been made?

## Drill

Lena manages a three-member Fort Myers property management LLC. She owns 60%. Over two years she raises her salary from $80,000 to $220,000 by her own decision, declares no distributions, and hires her husband's cleaning company for every property at rates 20% above market. She also learns through a client that a 40-unit building is for sale, buys it through her personal trust, and has the LLC manage it. The two minority members sue. List the fiduciary issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Salary and distributions.** Setting her own pay is a self-interested act. The business judgment rule does not apply where the decision-maker is interested. She bears the burden of proving the compensation was fair. Withholding distributions while paying herself more is a classic squeeze-out pattern and a good-faith issue under § 605.04091(4).
- **Husband's cleaning company.** Dealing with the company through a party with an adverse interest, § 605.04091(2)(b). Above-market rates plus no disclosure make fairness hard to prove. The remedy is disgorgement of the overcharge.
- **The building.** The opportunity came through the LLC's client relationship, in the LLC's line of business. Under *Meinhard* and *Guth*, it is a company opportunity. Having the LLC manage it does not cure the taking. Lena needed disclosure and unanimous consent under § 605.04091(6). Remedy: constructive trust or an accounting of profits.
- **Direct or derivative.** The overcharge and the lost opportunity harmed the LLC, so under *Dinuro* those claims are derivative, with demand or futility pleaded. Demand is likely futile because Lena controls the company. The distribution squeeze may be direct if the minority can show harm separate from the company's.
- **Operating agreement.** Does it authorize the manager to set compensation, permit related-party dealings on disclosure, or carve out real estate investments? Any such clause changes the analysis within § 605.0105's limits.
- **Open questions.** What did the minority know and when? Any written consents?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A manager approves a supplier contract after reading the bids and consulting the LLC's accountant. The supplier goes bankrupt and the LLC loses $100,000. Is the manager liable?
2. A member of a member-managed LLC opens a competing business across town while still a member. Breach?
3. A minority member claims the manager wasted company money on a failed expansion. Is the claim direct or derivative?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Almost certainly not. The decision was informed, disinterested, and in good faith, so the business judgment rule applies, and § 605.04091(3) requires gross negligence or worse. A bad outcome from a reasonable process is not a breach.
2. Yes, under § 605.04091(2)(c), unless the operating agreement permits it or all members consented after disclosure. The remedy is an accounting for the profits of the competing business.
3. Derivative. The harm fell on the company, and every member was hurt in proportion. Under *Dinuro* the member must make demand on the LLC or plead why demand would be futile, and any recovery goes to the company.

</details>
