# Operating agreements

**Module:** 02 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

The operating agreement is the constitution of the LLC. Chapter 605 supplies a default answer to every question the agreement does not, and the defaults are written for a generic company, not yours. They give every member a vote in proportion to profit share, require unanimous consent for big decisions, let any member walk away at any time, and give nobody a buyout. If two members disagree about anything that matters, the operating agreement is the first document a lawyer asks for. If there is none, the statute is the operating agreement.

## The rule

Section 605.0105, Fla. Stat., sets the structure. The operating agreement governs relations among the members, between the members and the LLC, the rights and duties of managers, the activities of the company, and the means of amending the agreement, § 605.0105(1). To the extent it does not, ch. 605 governs, § 605.0105(2). The agreement may be oral, implied, in a record, or any combination, § 605.0102(45). It binds the LLC and every member, including those who join later, § 605.0106.

**What the agreement cannot do.** Section 605.0105(3) lists the limits. Among them, an operating agreement may not:

- vary the law that governs the LLC or its capacity to sue and be sued;
- vary the requirement to maintain a registered agent;
- eliminate the duty of loyalty or the duty of care, though it may narrow them as described below;
- eliminate the obligation of good faith and fair dealing, though it may set standards for measuring it;
- unreasonably restrict a member's right to information under § 605.0410;
- vary the court's power to order dissolution under § 605.0702 or to decree winding up;
- unreasonably restrict a member's right to bring a direct or derivative action;
- relieve a person of liability for bad faith, willful or intentional misconduct, or a knowing violation of law;
- restrict the rights of third parties who are not members or managers.

**What it can do to fiduciary duties.** If not manifestly unreasonable, the agreement may identify specific activities that do not violate the duty of loyalty, alter aspects of the duty of loyalty, alter the duty of care so long as it does not authorize intentional misconduct or knowing violation of law, and prescribe standards for good faith, § 605.0105(4). Members may also authorize or ratify a specific conflicted act after full disclosure, § 605.04091(6).

**The defaults that most need changing.** When the agreement is silent:

- **Management.** Member-managed, § 605.0407. Every member has equal rights to participate in management, and ordinary matters are decided by a majority-in-interest.
- **Voting.** In proportion to each member's then-current interest in profits, § 605.04073. Amending the operating agreement or articles, and acts outside the ordinary course, require consent of all members (verify).
- **Distributions.** Before dissolution, shared on the basis of the agreed value of each member's contributions as stated in the company's records, § 605.0404(1). No member has a right to any distribution before dissolution; interim distributions are at the company's discretion, § 605.0404(2) (verify). Distributions that would leave the LLC unable to pay its debts or insolvent are prohibited, § 605.0405.
- **Contributions.** A promise to contribute is enforceable only if in a signed record, § 605.0403 (verify). No member is required to contribute more.
- **Admission of new members.** Consent of all members, § 605.0401(3).
- **Transfer.** A member may transfer the economic interest freely, but the transferee gets only distributions and no management or information rights, §§ 605.0501 and 605.0502. Lesson 6.
- **Dissociation.** Any member may leave at any time by notice, § 605.0601. The departing member becomes a transferee and has no right to be bought out, § 605.0603 (verify). Lesson 9.
- **Information.** Members may inspect company records on reasonable notice, § 605.0410.
- **Indemnification.** The LLC must indemnify a member or manager for liabilities incurred in the ordinary course on the company's behalf, § 605.0408 (verify scope).

## How courts reason about it

An operating agreement is a contract, and courts read it as one: plain meaning first, the whole document together, and extrinsic evidence only if the text is ambiguous. Module 03 covers interpretation. Three patterns recur in LLC disputes.

**Silence is a choice the statute makes for you.** When the agreement does not address a question, courts do not ask what the members would have wanted. They apply ch. 605. A member who assumed she could not be outvoted learns that voting follows profit interests. A member who assumed he could leave and get paid learns he is a transferee. Courts take this approach because the Legislature wrote the defaults precisely so judges would not have to reconstruct unwritten deals.

**Oral and implied agreements are real but hard to prove.** Because § 605.0102(45) recognizes them, a member can argue that a course of dealing, such as years of equal distributions despite unequal contributions, amounts to an implied term. Courts accept the argument when the conduct is consistent and long, and reject it when the writing says otherwise or the conduct is explainable another way. The practical lesson is that what the members actually did becomes evidence of what they agreed, for better or worse.

**The manifestly unreasonable test.** When an agreement narrows fiduciary duties, courts ask whether the limit was manifestly unreasonable when adopted, not with hindsight. A clause letting a member run a competing business in a disclosed line is usually fine. A clause that purports to let a manager take any opportunity he likes without disclosure runs into the ban on eliminating loyalty. *Meinhard v. Salmon* is the backdrop: when the parties did not write anything about opportunities, Cardozo imposed the "punctilio of an honor the most sensitive." The operating agreement is where parties can write something less demanding, within § 605.0105's limits. *Guth v. Loft* supplies the corporate opportunity test a court will apply if the agreement is silent.

Courts also police **good faith** in the exercise of discretion the agreement grants. A manager with "sole discretion" over distributions may still breach the implied covenant by withholding them to starve out a minority member. The agreement can set standards for good faith but cannot eliminate it.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the uniform act's framework of defaults plus a list of non-waivable provisions. Its voting and distribution defaults are keyed to contributions and profit interests, not per capita, which is a departure from the uniform act and from several states (verify).
- Florida does not require the operating agreement to be in writing, filed, or signed, which makes it unusual among states only in that it is explicit about implied agreements.
- Florida courts look to Delaware decisions on LLC and corporate matters as persuasive, especially on fiduciary duty and contract interpretation, but ch. 605 controls where it speaks.
- The company may file a statement of authority under § 605.0302 to make a management limit effective against third parties. An internal limit in the operating agreement alone does not bind a third party without notice.
- A member's right to information under § 605.0410 can be regulated by the agreement but not unreasonably restricted, which matters in a fight where the majority tries to cut off the minority from the books.

**Clauses a Florida operating agreement should cover.** Capital contributions and whether additional calls can be made; percentage interests and how they change; allocations of profit and loss and tax distributions; management structure, officers, and signing authority; voting thresholds for ordinary, major, and fundamental decisions; restrictions on transfer, rights of first refusal, and admission of new members; buy-sell triggers such as death, disability, divorce, bankruptcy, departure, and deadlock, with a valuation method and payment terms; deadlock resolution; dissociation and whether a buyout follows; confidentiality and non-competition among members within § 542.335; dispute resolution, venue, and fees; amendment procedure; and spousal consent where a member is married.

## Angles checklist

- Is there a written operating agreement? Signed by every member? Amended since?
- Does any course of dealing suggest an oral or implied term that contradicts the writing?
- What does the agreement say about this exact question, and if nothing, what does ch. 605 say?
- Does any clause narrow fiduciary duties? Is it within § 605.0105(3) and (4)?
- Who has authority to sign, and does the public record match?
- What are the voting thresholds for the decision in dispute?
- Is there a buy-sell or valuation clause? A deadlock mechanism?
- Does the agreement pick a forum, require arbitration, or shift fees?
- Has anyone been denied information rights?

## Drill

Three members form a Sarasota construction LLC. Alvaro contributes $200,000, Beth contributes $50,000, and Carl contributes labor valued in the books at $50,000. They sign a one-page agreement saying "all decisions by majority vote, profits split equally." Two years in, Alvaro wants to take a $1 million project through a separate company he owns. Beth and Carl object. Carl wants to leave and be paid for his share. List the operating agreement issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Voting.** "Majority vote" is ambiguous: per capita or by interest? The default is by profit interest, and the agreement set profits equal, so each member likely has one-third. Beth and Carl together are a majority. Whether that is enough for an act outside the ordinary course depends on whether the one-page agreement displaced the unanimity default.
- **The competing project.** Nothing in the agreement carves out competing activities. The default duty of loyalty under § 605.04091(2) bars competing with the LLC before dissolution and taking company opportunities. Alvaro needs disclosure and consent of all members under § 605.04091(6), or he answers for the profits. *Meinhard* and *Guth* both point against him.
- **Carl's exit.** He can dissociate by notice under § 605.0601, but the agreement gives no buyout and the statute gives none. He becomes a transferee entitled to his share of distributions when and if made. His leverage is a judicial dissolution petition under § 605.0702 if deadlock or oppression can be shown, which may prompt an election to purchase under § 605.0706.
- **Distributions.** The agreement fixes profits equally but says nothing about the return of Alvaro's larger capital on dissolution, so § 605.0710's default order applies.
- **Open questions.** Was anything said about outside projects when they formed? Have distributions in fact been equal? Is Carl's labor contribution documented in a signed record?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. Can an operating agreement say that managers owe no duty of loyalty to the members?
2. A two-member LLC has no written agreement. One member contributed 80% of the capital. How is a vote on hiring a new employee decided?
3. A member has been excluded from seeing the LLC's bank statements for a year. What does the statute say?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Section 605.0105(3) bars eliminating the duty of loyalty. The agreement may identify specific activities that do not violate it and may alter aspects of it, if not manifestly unreasonable, under § 605.0105(4).
2. By the members' interests in profits, § 605.04073. If profits are shared by contribution, the 80% member decides ordinary matters alone. If the members have in fact shared profits equally, a court may find an implied agreement to that effect, making it a deadlock.
3. Section 605.0410 gives members a right to inspect records on reasonable notice, and § 605.0105(3) bars the agreement from unreasonably restricting it. A year of exclusion supports a court order and is evidence in any dissolution or fiduciary claim.

</details>
