# Lucy v. Zehmer, 196 Va. 493, 84 S.E.2d 516 (Virginia Supreme Court of Appeals 1954)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · formation · objective theory of assent · statute of frauds · specific performance
**Used in:** [03-contracts/01-formation.md](../03-contracts/01-formation.md), [03-contracts/03-statute-of-frauds-and-writings.md](../03-contracts/03-statute-of-frauds-and-writings.md), [03-contracts/07-remedies.md](../03-contracts/07-remedies.md)

## One-line takeaway

Whether a contract was formed depends on what a reasonable person would understand from the parties' words and conduct, not on a party's secret intention or private joke.

## Facts

On December 20, 1952, W. O. Lucy and A. H. Zehmer were drinking in the restaurant Zehmer ran in Dinwiddie County, Virginia. Lucy had wanted to buy Zehmer's Ferguson Farm, about 471 acres, for years. That night he offered $50,000. Zehmer wrote on the back of a restaurant check, "I hereby agree to sell to W. O. Lucy the Ferguson Farm complete for $50,000.00, title satisfactory to buyer." At Lucy's request he rewrote it to read "We," and both Zehmer and his wife signed. The discussion lasted about forty minutes and covered the terms, title examination, and the timing of a closing. Lucy offered $5 to bind the deal. Zehmer refused the money and said it was all a joke. Lucy left with the paper.

Lucy promptly sold a half interest to his brother to raise the price, arranged financing, and had a lawyer examine title. When Zehmer refused to convey, Lucy sued.

## Procedural posture

Lucy sued the Zehmers in Virginia circuit court for specific performance of the written agreement. The Zehmers answered that the writing was a joke, that they never intended to sell, and that Zehmer was too drunk to make a contract. The trial court dismissed the suit, finding Lucy had not established a right to specific performance. Lucy appealed.

## Issue

Did the parties form a binding contract for the sale of the farm when one of them later claimed he was joking and the other had no reason to know it?

## Holding

Yes. The writing was a binding contract. Lucy was entitled to specific performance. The decree was reversed and the case remanded with directions to order conveyance.

## Reasoning

Justice Buchanan began with the evidence. Zehmer's own testimony showed a long discussion of a serious offer, the preparation of two drafts, his wife's signature, talk of examining title, and an exchange of the paper. Lucy testified that he believed, and had every reason to believe, that the deal was genuine. Zehmer's claim that he privately meant to bluff Lucy into admitting he lacked $50,000 was not communicated.

The court then stated the rule. The law imputes to a person an intention corresponding to the reasonable meaning of his words and acts. If his words and acts, judged by a reasonable standard, manifest an intention to agree, it is immaterial what unexpressed intention was in his mind. A contract requires mutual assent, but assent is judged by outward expression, not secret purpose. The only exception is where the other party knows or should know the expression is not serious. Lucy did not know and had no reason to know.

On intoxication, the court found that Zehmer was not so drunk as to be unable to understand what he was doing. He had drafted a coherent agreement and recalled its terms in detail. The writing also satisfied the statute of frauds because it identified the parties, the land, the price, and was signed by the sellers. Specific performance was appropriate because land is unique.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. Zehmer's best argument was context. Two men drinking at a restaurant bar, a deal written on the back of a check, and a refusal to accept the $5 binder are all signs of unseriousness. If Lucy had reason to know it was a bluff, there would have been no contract. The court resolved that question against Zehmer on the record, including Lucy's immediate steps to perform.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Every text, email, and signed napkin is evidence of assent judged from the outside. "I was only kidding," "I did not read it," and "I never really meant it" are not defenses if a reasonable counterparty would have taken you seriously. The lesson runs both ways. Do not sign or send anything you do not intend to be bound by, and understand that an informal writing with the key terms can be a contract even without a formal document. If you want to negotiate without being bound, say so in writing: "subject to a definitive agreement" or "not an offer."

## Florida connection

Florida follows the objective theory. The Florida Supreme Court has said that the making of a contract depends not on the agreement of two minds in one intention but on the agreement of two sets of external signs. See *Gendzier v. Bielecki*, 97 So. 2d 604 (Fla. 1957) (verify citation). A contract for the sale of land must satisfy Florida's statute of frauds, § 725.01, Fla. Stat., which requires a writing signed by the party to be charged; an informal writing with the essential terms will do. Specific performance of a land contract is routinely available in Florida because land is unique. Florida courts also use the objective test for online assent, which is why clickwrap terms are enforced when the manifestation of assent is clear.
