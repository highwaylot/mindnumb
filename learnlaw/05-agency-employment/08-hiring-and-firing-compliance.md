# Hiring and firing compliance

**Module:** 05 · **Lesson:** 8 · **Time:** ~55 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

The earlier lessons taught doctrines. This one is the checklist those doctrines generate at the two moments of highest risk: the day someone starts and the day someone leaves. Most of these obligations are mechanical and cheap to meet. Missing them is expensive because each failure is its own penalty, and because sloppy paperwork is what makes a routine firing look like something else.

## The rule

### Hiring

1. **Form I-9.** Every employer must verify identity and work authorization for every new hire on Form I-9 within three business days of the start date, and retain the form for three years after hire or one year after termination, whichever is later (8 U.S.C. § 1324a) (verify).
2. **E-Verify.** Under § 448.095, Fla. Stat., a private employer with 25 or more employees must use E-Verify for every new hire, certify compliance on its first reemployment tax return each year, and keep the records for three years. Repeated noncompliance after notice can lead to fines and license consequences (verify).
3. **Background checks and the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act** (15 U.S.C. § 1681 et seq.). If you use a third-party screening company, you must give a standalone written disclosure, get written authorization, and, before taking adverse action based on the report, give a pre-adverse action notice with a copy of the report and the federal summary of rights, allow a reasonable time to respond (five business days is the common practice), then send an adverse action notice. Florida has no statewide "ban the box" law for private employers (verify). Florida's § 768.096 gives a presumption against negligent hiring to employers who run a background check and take the other listed steps.
4. **New hire reporting.** Report every new hire, and independent contractors paid $600 or more, to the Florida Department of Revenue within 20 days (§ 409.2576) (verify).
5. **Tax registration.** Get a federal EIN. Register with the Department of Revenue for reemployment tax once you pay $1,500 in wages in a calendar quarter or have an employee for 20 weeks in a year (verify). New employers pay 2.7% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages (verify). Collect a W-4 and withhold.
6. **Workers' compensation.** Required at four or more employees for non-construction businesses, including officers and LLC members who have not elected exemption, and at one or more for construction (ch. 440) (verify).
7. **Posters.** Federal: FLSA minimum wage, OSHA, EEOC "Know Your Rights," Employee Polygraph Protection Act, USERRA, FMLA if 50 or more employees. Florida: minimum wage (§ 448.109), reemployment assistance, workers' compensation, Florida Commission on Human Relations discrimination notice, and child labor if minors are employed.
8. **Documents.** Offer letter stating at-will status, pay, exempt or non-exempt classification; handbook with at-will disclaimer, harassment policy and complaint procedure, and signed acknowledgment; restrictive covenants that meet § 542.335; confidentiality and IP assignment (module 06).

### During employment

Document performance in real time: dated, specific, factual. Apply policies consistently. Investigate complaints promptly and record what you did. Keep personnel files, I-9s, and medical information separate.

### Separation

1. **Final pay.** Florida has no statute setting a deadline; pay on the next regular payday. Accrued vacation is owed only if policy or contract says so.
2. **COBRA.** Employers with 20 or more employees must offer continuation of group health coverage for 18 months after termination (29 U.S.C. § 1161). Florida's mini-COBRA, § 627.6692, covers employers with fewer than 20 employees through the insurer (verify).
3. **Reemployment assistance.** Respond to the Department of Commerce notice of claim within the stated deadline. Benefits are denied for misconduct as defined in § 443.036(29), which is narrower than "fired for cause."
4. **Separation agreements.** A release requires consideration beyond what is already owed. For employees 40 and over, the Older Workers Benefit Protection Act (29 U.S.C. § 626(f)) requires a knowing and voluntary waiver: plain language, specific reference to ADEA rights, advice in writing to consult a lawyer, 21 days to consider (45 for a group program, with disclosure of ages and titles), and 7 days to revoke. FLSA claims cannot be released privately without court or Department of Labor supervision. A release cannot bar filing an EEOC charge, only recovery. Confidentiality and non-disparagement clauses offered to non-supervisory employees have drawn NLRB scrutiny (verify current status).
5. **References.** Under § 768.095, an employer that discloses information about a former employee to a prospective employer is immune from liability unless the information was knowingly false or violated the employee's civil rights (verify). The safe practice remains dates, title, and eligibility for rehire.
6. **Exit mechanics.** Collect devices, cut credentials the same day, and send a letter restating confidentiality and restrictive covenant obligations.
7. **Mass layoffs.** The federal WARN Act requires 60 days' notice at employers with 100 or more employees. Florida has no mini-WARN.

## How courts reason about it

Compliance rules are mostly bright lines, so the reasoning that matters is about what the paperwork proves later.

**Documentation as evidence.** In a discrimination or retaliation case, the employer's documented reason is tested against the record. A contemporaneous written warning is evidence; a memo written after the lawsuit is evidence of pretext. Courts and juries notice when the first negative review follows the first complaint.

**Consistency.** A policy applied to one person and waived for another, without a documented reason, is the plaintiff's best exhibit in the comparator analysis from lesson 7.

**Releases.** Courts enforce releases that are knowing and voluntary and supported by consideration. They refuse to enforce ADEA waivers that miss any OWBPA requirement, even if the employee understood everything, because the statute makes the formalities mandatory. *Oubre v. Entergy Operations, Inc.*, 522 U.S. 422 (1998), held the employee could keep the severance and still sue when the waiver was defective.

**Negligent hiring.** Courts ask what a reasonable investigation would have revealed and whether the position gave the employee access that made the harm foreseeable. The § 768.096 presumption rewards the check but does not excuse ignoring what it finds.

**References and defamation.** Florida's conditional privilege for employer references and § 768.095 protect truthful, good-faith statements. Opinion is protected; false statements of fact are not; knowing falsity loses the privilege. Most employers avoid the question by saying little.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows federal law on I-9, FCRA, COBRA for larger employers, OWBPA, and WARN. Florida adds or differs on:

- **E-Verify** at 25 employees (§ 448.095), since July 1, 2023 (verify).
- **No final-paycheck statute**, no severance requirement, no mini-WARN, no statewide ban-the-box for private employers, no paid sick leave mandate, and preemption of local wage and benefit ordinances (verify).
- **Reference immunity** under § 768.095.
- **Negligent hiring presumption** under § 768.096.
- **Mini-COBRA** under § 627.6692 for small employers (verify).

## Angles checklist

- Headcount, and which thresholds it crosses: 1, 4, 10, 15, 20, 25, 50, 100.
- Is every I-9 complete and on file? Is E-Verify required and being run?
- Were FCRA disclosures and the two-step adverse action process used for background checks?
- Is the employee classified correctly, and does the offer letter say so?
- Is there a signed handbook acknowledgment with an at-will disclaimer and a harassment policy?
- For the person being let go: what is documented, when, and by whom? Any protected activity in the last year?
- Is the employee 40 or older? Does the release meet OWBPA?
- COBRA or mini-COBRA notice sent? Final pay on the regular payday?
- Flip it: what will the employee's lawyer ask for first? Personnel file, comparators' files, handbook, emails around the decision.

## Drill

Ridgeline Roofing, LLC has grown from 6 to 30 employees in two years. It never used E-Verify, keeps I-9s in each personnel file, and ran background checks through an online service using a checkbox buried in the job application. It now wants to let go a 52-year-old estimator who is slow to adopt new software, with two weeks' severance and a release the owner found online. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **E-Verify.** At 30 employees the company has been required to use E-Verify since it crossed 25 (§ 448.095). It must start now and certify on its reemployment tax return. Retroactive penalties are possible after notice (verify).
- **I-9 storage.** Keeping I-9s in personnel files is not a violation, but it exposes the whole file in an audit. Separate I-9 files are the standard.
- **FCRA.** A disclosure buried in the application is not the "clear and conspicuous" standalone disclosure the statute requires. Every applicant screened under it has a potential statutory damages claim, and class actions on this exact fact pattern are common.
- **The estimator.** He is in the ADEA and FCRA protected class. "Slow to adopt new software" is a legitimate reason if documented and if younger employees with the same problem were treated the same. If nothing is documented, this looks like age-coded language.
- **The release.** For a 52-year-old, the release must meet OWBPA: ADEA reference, counsel advice, 21 days, 7-day revocation. An online form likely misses some. If so, he keeps the severance and can still sue.
- **Separation mechanics.** COBRA applies at 30 employees. Final pay next regular payday. Respond to the reemployment claim; "slow with software" is not misconduct, so benefits will be paid and charged to the account.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. Can a Florida employer give a candid negative reference?
2. An employee is fired and the employer withholds the final paycheck until a company laptop is returned. Lawful?
3. A 38-year-old signs a release without 21 days to consider. Is it void?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Yes, with care. § 768.095 immunizes disclosures to prospective employers unless knowingly false or in violation of civil rights, and truth is a complete defense to defamation. The risk is in opinion stated as fact or in statements the employer cannot prove.
2. Probably not. The FLSA requires wages on the regular payday, and withholding all pay drops the employee below minimum wage for the period. Deducting the value of unreturned property is also limited by the minimum wage floor.
3. No. OWBPA applies only to waivers of ADEA claims by employees 40 and over. A release by a 38-year-old is tested under ordinary contract principles: knowing, voluntary, supported by consideration.

</details>
