# Choosing an entity

**Module:** 02 · **Lesson:** 1 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

The entity you pick decides who pays when the business owes money, how its income is taxed, how much paperwork keeps the shield up, and what happens when an owner leaves. Most of the trouble in later modules, from personal liability to partner fights, traces back to a choice made here, or to no choice at all. The law supplies a default entity to anyone who does not choose, and the default is the worst one.

## The rule

Florida recognizes four main forms for a for-profit business. Each is a bundle of answers to the same four questions: who is liable, how is it taxed, what formalities keep it alive, and what does it cost.

**Sole proprietorship.** One person doing business without forming anything. No filing creates it. If the owner trades under a name other than her own, she must register a fictitious name under § 865.09, Fla. Stat., for $50 (verify), good for five years. Liability: unlimited. Every business debt and every tort is the owner's personally. Tax: the owner reports on Schedule C and pays self-employment tax. Formalities: none. Cost: nearly zero.

**General partnership.** Two or more persons carrying on as co-owners a business for profit. Under § 620.8202(1), that association **is** a partnership "whether or not the persons intend to form a partnership." No filing is needed. Liability: each partner is jointly and severally liable for all partnership obligations, § 620.8306. Each partner is an agent who can bind the firm in the ordinary course, § 620.8301. Tax: pass-through; the partnership files an information return and partners pay on their shares. Formalities: none required, which is the danger. A partnership can register as a **limited liability partnership** under § 620.9001 to shield partners from the firm's debts (verify filing fee). A **limited partnership** under §§ 620.1101 and following has general partners who run it and are liable, and limited partners who invest and are not. Lesson 8 covers partnerships.

**Limited liability company.** Created by filing Articles of Organization with the Department of State under § 605.0201. Governed by the Florida Revised Limited Liability Company Act, ch. 605. Liability: a member or manager is not personally liable for the company's debts solely by reason of being a member or manager, § 605.0304(1). Tax: by default, a one-member LLC is a disregarded entity and a multi-member LLC is a partnership for federal income tax; either can elect to be taxed as an S corporation or a C corporation. Florida has no personal income tax, and an LLC taxed as a partnership, disregarded entity, or S corporation generally owes no Florida corporate income tax. Formalities: an annual report and a registered agent. Management: member-managed unless the articles or operating agreement say manager-managed. Cost: $125 (verify) to form and $138.75 (verify) a year.

**Corporation.** Created by filing Articles of Incorporation under § 607.0202 and governed by the Florida Business Corporation Act, ch. 607. Liability: shareholders are not liable for corporate debts. Tax: a **C corporation** pays federal corporate tax and Florida's 5.5% (verify) corporate income tax, with the first $50,000 (verify) exempt, and shareholders pay again on dividends. An **S corporation** elects pass-through treatment on IRS Form 2553 if it has 100 or fewer shareholders, all individuals, estates, or certain trusts, none nonresident aliens, and one class of stock. Formalities: a board of directors, officers, bylaws, annual shareholder meetings, and minutes. Cost: $70 (verify) to form and $150 (verify) a year. Lesson 7 covers corporations.

Professionals such as lawyers, doctors, and accountants use a professional association or professional limited liability company under ch. 621. Nonprofits use ch. 617.

## How courts reason about it

Courts do not choose entities; owners do. But courts decide what entity exists when the owners did not decide, and whether the one they chose will be respected.

**The default is partnership.** When two people run a business together and share profits without forming anything, a court asked "what are they" applies § 620.8202. Sharing profits raises a presumption of partnership unless the profits were received as wages, rent, interest on a loan, or payment of a debt, § 620.8202(3). Intent to be partners is irrelevant; intent to run a business together is enough. The reasoning is protective: outsiders dealt with a firm, and the people behind it should not escape by saying they never signed anything. *Meinhard v. Salmon* shows what follows once the relationship exists: the co-venturers owe each other the highest fiduciary duty, and the one who takes an opportunity for himself answers for it.

**The shield is respected if it was real.** Courts honor limited liability because the legislature granted it and because credit markets depend on it. *Walkovszky v. Carlton* is the classic statement: a man who split a taxi fleet into ten thinly capitalized corporations was not personally liable for an accident, because the complaint did not allege he was running the business as his own rather than the corporation's. Florida requires more than thin capitalization: the plaintiff must show the entity was used for an improper purpose. *Dania Jai-Alai Palace, Inc. v. Sykes*. Lesson 5 covers the test. The shield covers only the entity's debts, never a member's own torts or personal guaranties.

**Tax drives the choice more than liability does.** Since an LLC can elect any tax treatment, the liability answer for LLC and corporation is the same and the real decision is tax. An S election can reduce self-employment tax by splitting owner compensation into a reasonable salary and distributions; the IRS polices whether the salary is reasonable. A C corporation makes sense for a business that will retain earnings or seek venture investment, and its stock may qualify for the federal small business stock exclusion. These are accountant questions, but the entity choice does not lock them in.

**Formality still matters.** Corporations require meetings and minutes; LLCs do not, and Florida says an LLC's failure to observe formalities is not a ground for piercing, § 605.0304(2). But the question in every owner dispute is what the owners agreed, and the entity with no records has no answer.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the majority rule on all four forms, which are built on the uniform acts. The LLC Act is Florida's version of the Revised Uniform Limited Liability Company Act with changes, and the partnership chapter is the Revised Uniform Partnership Act.
- Florida's LLC default rules differ from the uniform act in places: voting and distributions follow contribution and profit interests, not per capita, § 605.04073 and § 605.0404 (verify). Lesson 3 covers the defaults.
- The charging order is the exclusive remedy of a member's personal creditor against a multi-member LLC, but not against a single-member LLC, § 605.0503. That is a reason a one-owner business sometimes adds a genuine second member. *Olmstead v. FTC* is the history. Lesson 6.
- Fees: LLC formation $125, annual report $138.75; corporation formation $70, annual report $150; late annual report $400 (verify) after May 1 and administrative dissolution if not filed by the third Friday in September.
- Fictitious names are registered with the Department of State under § 865.09 for any form that trades under a name other than its legal name.
- The federal Corporate Transparency Act's beneficial ownership report no longer applies to U.S.-formed companies after FinCEN's March 2025 interim final rule (verify).

## Angles checklist

- Who are the owners, and is there any writing among them? If none, is there a partnership by default?
- Is there a filed entity? Is it in good standing on Sunbiz?
- Who signed each contract, lease, and loan: the entity or a person? Any personal guaranties?
- How is the entity taxed, and has any election been made?
- Are the business's finances separate from the owners'?
- Is anyone trading under a name that is not registered?
- If an owner leaves, dies, or divorces, what document says what happens?

## Drill

Maria and Devon start a mobile dog-grooming service in Tampa. Maria buys the van in her name and Devon does the grooming. They split what is left after expenses each month, call the business "Pawsitively Clean," and have never filed anything. A customer's dog is injured during a groom and the customer threatens to sue. A van payment is overdue. List the entity issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **What entity exists.** Two people, co-owners, sharing profits, no filing. Under § 620.8202 this is a general partnership whether they meant it or not. The profit split is presumed to be a partner's share, not wages, unless they can show Devon was being paid for services as an employee or contractor.
- **Liability for the injured dog.** The customer can sue the partnership and both partners. Devon is liable for his own negligence regardless of entity. Maria is jointly and severally liable as a partner under § 620.8306 even though she never touched the dog.
- **The van.** Maria signed personally, so she is liable on the loan regardless. Whether the van is partnership property depends on how it was acquired and used, § 620.8204, and that matters if the partnership dissolves.
- **The name.** "Pawsitively Clean" is a fictitious name that should have been registered under § 865.09. Failure bars suing on contracts made under the name until registration is cured (verify consequence).
- **What they should have done.** An LLC would have confined the dog claim to the LLC's assets and insurance, except Devon's own negligence. The van loan would still have needed Maria's guaranty.
- **Open questions.** Any text or email about ownership? Insurance? Did Devon get a fixed amount or a share?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A single owner forms an LLC and is the only member. Who is liable when the LLC defaults on its lease, which the owner signed as "Manager"?
2. Two friends agree by handshake to split profits 50/50 on a landscaping business. They never discussed being partners. Are they?
3. Why might a Florida LLC elect S corporation tax treatment while a venture-backed startup elects C corporation treatment?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The LLC, not the owner, assuming the owner signed in a representative capacity and gave no personal guaranty. The shield under § 605.0304 does not depend on the number of members. If the landlord required a personal guaranty, the owner is liable on the guaranty.
2. Yes. Section 620.8202 forms a partnership from the association to carry on as co-owners a business for profit, whether or not they intended to form one. Sharing profits is presumptive evidence.
3. An S election lets owners who work in the business split income into salary and distributions, reducing self-employment tax, and avoids Florida corporate income tax. A C corporation can retain earnings at the corporate rate, has no restrictions on who can own it, and can issue preferred stock to investors, which S corporations cannot.

</details>
