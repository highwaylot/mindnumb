# Raffles v. Wichelhaus, 2 Hurl. & C. 906, 159 Eng. Rep. 375 (Ex. 1864)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · formation · mutual mistake · ambiguity · meeting of the minds
**Used in:** [`03-contracts/01-formation.md`](../03-contracts/01-formation.md), [`03-contracts/04-interpretation-and-parol-evidence.md`](../03-contracts/04-interpretation-and-parol-evidence.md), [`03-contracts/06-defenses.md`](../03-contracts/06-defenses.md)

## One-line takeaway

When a material term is ambiguous, each party honestly attaches a different meaning to it, and neither knows or has reason to know of the other's meaning, there is no contract.

## Facts

Raffles agreed to sell Wichelhaus 125 bales of Surat cotton "to arrive ex Peerless from Bombay." Two ships named Peerless sailed from Bombay that year. One left in October. One left in December. Wichelhaus meant the October ship. Raffles meant the December ship. When the December Peerless arrived, Raffles tendered the cotton. Wichelhaus refused to accept or pay. Nothing in the contract identified which Peerless was meant, and nothing suggested either party knew there were two.

## Procedural posture

Raffles sued in the Court of Exchequer for the price. Wichelhaus pleaded that he meant the October ship, that the cotton tendered was not from that ship, and that he had never agreed to buy cotton from the December ship. Raffles demurred, arguing the plea was no defense: the contract named a ship and cotton arrived on a ship of that name. The court heard argument on the demurrer.

## Issue

Where the parties use the same words but each means a different thing, and the words apply equally to both, is there a binding contract?

## Holding

No. The court gave judgment for the defendant without a written opinion. Counsel for the defendant, Mellish, argued that there was no consensus ad idem, no meeting of the minds, because the parties were agreeing to different things. The court stopped the plaintiff's counsel mid-argument and ruled for the defendant. The case stands for the rule that a latent ambiguity which the parties resolve differently, in good faith, prevents a contract from forming.

## Reasoning

Because the court wrote nothing, the reasoning comes from the argument and from later commentary. Three points carried the day.

First, the ambiguity was latent. On its face "Peerless from Bombay" was clear. Only outside evidence showed there were two ships. Where a term is latently ambiguous, courts let in evidence of what each party meant. That evidence showed the parties meant different ships.

Second, the name of the ship was material, not a detail. The ship fixed the time of delivery and so the market price at delivery. Buying October cotton and buying December cotton were different bargains.

Third, neither party was more at fault. Neither knew of the other ship. There was no basis for holding one party to the other's meaning.

Oliver Wendell Holmes later explained the case in objective terms, which is why American courts accepted it. The law does not care what the parties privately thought. It cares what they said. But here the parties said different things with the same word. There was no single objective meaning to enforce. The rule is now Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 20: no contract if the parties attach materially different meanings and neither knows or has reason to know the other's meaning. If one party knows the other's meaning and the other does not, the contract is formed on the innocent party's meaning.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The strongest argument for Raffles was the objective theory itself. The contract said Peerless from Bombay. A ship named Peerless from Bombay arrived with the cotton. Wichelhaus's private intent should not matter. The answer is that the objective theory needs a shared referent. Two ships with the same name meant there was none. The case is narrow for that reason. Courts do not find "no contract" whenever one side regrets a deal and claims it meant something else. They find it only when the term is genuinely ambiguous and the evidence shows an honest divergence.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Most business disputes about meaning are not Peerless cases. One side usually had reason to know what the other meant, or the words have a plain meaning in the trade. But the case explains why precision in identifying the subject matter matters: the exact product, model, lot, delivery date, location, and version. When a dispute arises over a term both sides read differently, the questions are: was the term ambiguous on its face or only in light of outside facts, which party knew or should have known of the other's reading, and was the term material.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the majority rule. Florida courts hold that mutual assent requires agreement on the essential terms and that a latent ambiguity which the parties resolved differently can mean no contract was formed. For latent ambiguity, Florida admits parol evidence to show what the parties meant. *Ace Electric Supply Co. v. Terra Nova Electric, Inc.*, 288 So. 2d 544 (Fla. 1st DCA 1973) (verify citation). Where a contract was formed but rests on a shared mistaken assumption about a basic fact, Florida allows rescission for mutual mistake, and allows rescission for unilateral mistake in limited circumstances. *Maryland Casualty Co. v. Krasnek*, 174 So. 2d 541 (Fla. 1965). For sales of goods, Florida's UCC lets a court use course of dealing, usage of trade, and course of performance to fix meaning before finding there was none. § 672.202, Fla. Stat.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Does the outcome change? Suppose Raffles knew there were two ships and said nothing. Would there be a contract, and on whose terms?
2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What evidence would have let Raffles hold Wichelhaus to the December ship?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? A purchase order says "50 units of the Pro model." Your catalog has had two Pro models, old and new, in the same year. What questions decide whether there is a contract at all?
