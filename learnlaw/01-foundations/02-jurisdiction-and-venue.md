# Jurisdiction and venue

**Module:** 01 · **Lesson:** 2 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

A court needs two kinds of power: **subject-matter jurisdiction** over the type of case and **personal jurisdiction** over the defendant. **Venue** is the smaller question of which county or district. If you sell to customers in other states, you can be sued there. If you sue a customer who never set foot in Florida, you may not be able to do it here.

## The rule

### Personal jurisdiction

The constitutional floor comes from the Due Process Clause as read in *International Shoe Co. v. Washington*: the defendant must have **minimum contacts** with the forum such that the suit does not offend **traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice**. The plaintiff bears the burden.

The doctrine has two branches.

- **General jurisdiction.** The defendant is "at home" in the state and can be sued there on anything: an individual's domicile, a corporation's state of incorporation and principal place of business, and almost nowhere else. *Daimler AG v. Bauman*, 571 U.S. 117 (2014).
- **Specific jurisdiction.** Three questions: did the defendant **purposefully avail** itself of the forum, does the claim **arise out of or relate to** those contacts, and is jurisdiction **reasonable**. *Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz*, 471 U.S. 462 (1985); *Ford Motor Co. v. Montana Eighth Judicial District Court*, 592 U.S. 351 (2021).

In Florida the analysis has two steps: the claim must fit a category in the long-arm statute, § 48.193, Fla. Stat., and the constitutional test must be met. *Venetian Salami Co. v. Parthenais*, 554 So. 2d 499 (Fla. 1989). Section 48.193(1)(a) lists specific acts: operating a business in Florida, committing a tort here, owning Florida real property, breaching a contract by failing to perform an act required here, and others. Section 48.193(2) gives general jurisdiction over substantial and not isolated activity in Florida.

Personal jurisdiction can be **waived**. A defendant who answers without objecting consents. Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.140(h); Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(h)(1). A forum selection clause is consent in advance.

### Subject-matter jurisdiction

Federal courts need a statutory door. The two main ones:

- **Federal question**, 28 U.S.C. § 1331. The claim arises under federal law, and the federal issue must appear in the plaintiff's own complaint, not in an anticipated defense.
- **Diversity**, 28 U.S.C. § 1332. Every plaintiff is a citizen of a different state from every defendant, and the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000 (verify), exclusive of interest and costs. An individual is a citizen of the state of domicile. A corporation is a citizen of its state of incorporation and its principal place of business, *Hertz Corp. v. Friend*, 559 U.S. 77 (2010). An **LLC is a citizen of every state of which any member is a citizen**, *Rolling Greens MHP, L.P. v. Comcast SCH Holdings L.L.C.*, 374 F.3d 1020 (11th Cir. 2004). Partnerships follow the same rule.

Related state claims ride along under **supplemental jurisdiction**, 28 U.S.C. § 1367. A defendant sued in state court can **remove** a case that could have been filed in federal court within 30 days (verify) of service, 28 U.S.C. §§ 1441, 1446, but not on diversity grounds if any defendant is a citizen of the forum state. Subject-matter jurisdiction cannot be waived, and a court raises it on its own.

### Venue

Florida's general rule, § 47.011, Fla. Stat.: a case is brought in the county where the defendant resides, where the cause of action accrued, or where the property in litigation is located. For a domestic corporation, § 47.051 substitutes the county where it keeps an office for its customary business; for a foreign corporation, where it has an agent or representative. Florida courts have applied these rules to LLCs (verify). With multiple defendants, § 47.021 allows any county where any defendant resides. Transfer for convenience is under § 47.122. Federal venue is in 28 U.S.C. § 1391.

Venue is waived if not raised promptly. Florida enforces forum selection clauses unless unreasonable or unjust. *Manrique v. Fabbri*, 493 So. 2d 437 (Fla. 1986).

## How courts reason about it

*International Shoe* replaced a rule about physical presence with a rule about fairness. A company with salesmen and sales in Washington could be made to answer there, because a company that benefits from a state's market should bear the burden of its courts. Ever since, courts have asked what a defendant **did**, not what the plaintiff suffered.

*Burger King* applied that to a contract. A Michigan franchisee signed a twenty-year franchise agreement with a Miami franchisor, sent payments to Miami, dealt with Miami headquarters, and agreed that Florida law governed. He never visited Florida. The Court held Florida could hear the franchisor's suit. A contract alone is not enough, but the negotiations, the contemplated future dealings, the terms, and the course of dealing together showed he had reached out to Florida and created a continuing relationship there. Facts that push toward jurisdiction over a contract defendant: a long-term relationship, performance or payment directed into the forum, repeated communications into the forum, a governing-law clause pointing there. Facts that push the other way: a one-off purchase, the forum party initiating the contact, performance elsewhere.

Two further patterns matter for a Florida business.

- **Torts by remote communication.** A defendant who commits a tort through calls, emails, or electronic communications into Florida commits a "tortious act within this state" under § 48.193(1)(a)2 without being physically present. *Wendt v. Horowitz*, 822 So. 2d 1252 (Fla. 2002). A defamatory web post accessed in Florida can qualify. *Internet Solutions Corp. v. Marshall*, 39 So. 3d 1201 (Fla. 2010). The constitutional step still applies.
- **Plaintiff's contacts do not count.** The claim must arise from the defendant's own contacts with the forum, not from the plaintiff's living there. *Walden v. Fiore*, 571 U.S. 277 (2014). A Floridian harmed by a defendant who never aimed anything at Florida may have to sue elsewhere.

For diversity, the LLC rule is a trap. Every member's citizenship must be pleaded, down through any member that is itself an LLC. Courts dismiss cases years in for a missing member.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the majority two-step approach: long-arm statute first, then due process. The statute is narrower than due process in some categories, so both steps matter.
- The procedure for contesting jurisdiction is from *Venetian Salami*: the defendant files an affidavit denying the jurisdictional facts; the plaintiff must answer with evidence; if the affidavits conflict, the court holds a limited hearing.
- Contracts of $250,000 (verify) or more may choose Florida law and a Florida forum under §§ 685.101 and 685.102, Fla. Stat., and a Florida court will have jurisdiction on that basis alone under § 48.193(1)(a)9 (verify numbering).

## Angles checklist

- Where is each party domiciled or headquartered? For every LLC, where does each member live?
- Did the defendant do anything in or aimed at Florida: sign, ship, pay, communicate, post, visit?
- Is there a forum selection clause or a § 685.102 clause?
- Is there a federal claim? Complete diversity? More than $75,000? Any forum-state defendant blocking removal?
- Which county: defendant's residence, where the cause accrued, or where the property is?
- Has the defendant already waived the objection by answering?

## Drill

A Fort Lauderdale LLC with members in Florida and North Carolina builds custom boats. A Texas buyer visited the Fort Lauderdale yard twice, signed a $400,000 build contract there, wired deposits to the LLC's Florida bank, and now refuses to pay the balance, claiming the boat is defective. The contract has no forum or governing-law clause. The buyer has filed suit in Texas state court. List the jurisdiction and venue issues from both sides.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Can Florida reach the buyer?** Likely yes. He came to Florida twice, signed here, and paid into Florida. Failing to make a payment due in Florida fits § 48.193(1)(a)7, and *Burger King* supports minimum contacts: a deliberate, sustained relationship aimed at a Florida business. The boat and witnesses are here, so jurisdiction is reasonable.
- **Can Texas reach the LLC?** Harder. The LLC built the boat in Florida for a buyer who came to it. Unless it advertised in Texas, delivered there, or dealt with the buyer there, its Texas contacts are really the buyer's, which do not count under *Walden*. The LLC should move to dismiss in Texas rather than answer, or it waives the objection.
- **Federal court.** The LLC is a citizen of Florida and North Carolina; the buyer of Texas. Complete diversity exists and the amount exceeds $75,000. The LLC could sue in the Southern District of Florida, and the buyer could remove a state suit, since no defendant is a Florida citizen.
- **Venue in Florida.** Broward County, where the LLC keeps its office and where payment was due, under §§ 47.011 and 47.051.
- **Open questions.** Did the LLC solicit the buyer in Texas? Where was delivery to occur?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A Florida LLC has three members: two in Florida and one in Michigan. It is sued by a Michigan company. Can the case be in federal court on diversity?
2. A Georgia company sends one email to a Florida business offering a product, and the Florida business buys it. The product fails. Does Florida have personal jurisdiction over the Georgia company?
3. A defendant believes it was sued in the wrong county. It files an answer denying the claim and says nothing about venue. Can it raise venue later?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. The LLC is a citizen of Florida and Michigan. The plaintiff is a Michigan citizen. A Michigan citizen on each side destroys complete diversity.
2. Probably not on these facts alone. One sale, one email, and no ongoing relationship is thin for purposeful availment. Repeated sales into Florida, shipping here, a Florida-directed website, or a warranty performed here would change it.
3. Generally no. Improper venue must be asserted in the first response, by motion or in the answer. Raising it after answering on the merits is usually a waiver.

</details>
