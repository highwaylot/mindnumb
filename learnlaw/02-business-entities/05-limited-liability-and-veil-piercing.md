# Limited liability and veil piercing

**Module:** 02 · **Lesson:** 5 · **Time:** ~55 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Limited liability is the reason to form an entity. It means the company's debts are the company's, and a creditor who wins against the LLC gets the LLC's assets and nothing more. Piercing the veil is the exception: a court disregards the entity and reaches the owner. Every owner should know exactly what the shield covers, what it never covered, and what conduct lets a plaintiff through it. The answers are more favorable to a Florida owner than the folklore suggests, and less favorable than the folklore about what you can sign without personal risk.

## The rule

**The shield.** Section 605.0304(1), Fla. Stat.: a debt, obligation, or liability of an LLC, whether in contract, tort, or otherwise, is solely the company's. A member or manager is not personally liable solely by reason of being or acting as a member or manager. Section 605.0304(2) adds that the failure of the LLC to observe formalities relating to the exercise of its powers or management is not a ground for imposing liability on members or managers. Shareholders of a corporation have the same protection under § 607.0622 (verify).

**What the shield never covered.** The statute protects against liability "solely by reason of" status. It does not protect against liability for what a person did or promised:

- **Personal guaranties.** A member who signs a guaranty of the LLC's lease, loan, or credit line is liable on the guaranty. Most small business credit requires one.
- **Own torts.** A member or employee who personally commits negligence or fraud is liable for it, with the LLC liable alongside. The entity shields owners from the company's torts, not from their own.
- **Signing in the wrong capacity.** A member who signs a contract without indicating she signs for the LLC may be personally bound.
- **Unlawful distributions.** A member or manager who consents to a distribution that leaves the LLC unable to pay its debts is liable to the company for the excess, § 605.0406, and a member who knowingly receives one must return it.
- **Trust fund taxes.** A responsible person who fails to pay over withheld payroll taxes is personally liable under 26 U.S.C. § 6672, and Florida imposes personal liability on responsible officers for unpaid sales tax, § 213.29 (verify).
- **Statutory employer liability.** Federal wage law treats an individual with operational control as an "employer" liable for unpaid wages.
- **Pre-formation and post-dissolution acts.** Lesson 2 and lesson 9.

**Piercing.** When none of those applies, a creditor may still reach the owner by piercing. Florida's test comes from *Dania Jai-Alai Palace, Inc. v. Sykes*, 450 So. 2d 1114 (Fla. 1984), as later organized by the DCAs. The plaintiff must prove three things:

1. the owner dominated and controlled the entity to such an extent that its independent existence was in fact non-existent and the owner was its alter ego;
2. the entity form was used fraudulently or for an improper purpose; and
3. the fraudulent or improper use caused injury to the plaintiff.

*Gasparini v. Pordomingo*, 972 So. 2d 1053 (Fla. 3d DCA 2008), states the three-part formulation. Florida courts apply the same test to LLCs (verify leading LLC case). The plaintiff bears the burden on every element, and the second element is the one that usually fails.

## How courts reason about it

**Why the shield holds.** *Walkovszky v. Carlton* is the foundation. Carlton owned ten corporations, each holding two taxicabs and the minimum insurance New York required. A pedestrian hit by one cab sued Carlton personally, arguing the structure was a fraud on the public. The New York Court of Appeals dismissed the claim. Splitting a business into thin entities is what the law permits; if the minimum insurance is too low, that is the Legislature's problem. The complaint would have stated a claim only if it alleged Carlton was running the business in his individual capacity, shuttling funds in and out for personal ends. The reasoning that survives: undercapitalization and a web of entities are not enough on their own. The plaintiff must show the owner treated the entity as his pocket.

**What Florida added.** Before *Dania Jai-Alai*, the Third DCA had allowed piercing on a showing that the corporation was a mere instrumentality of the shareholder, with no separate wrongdoing. The Florida Supreme Court rejected that. The corporate entity is to be respected unless it was organized or used to mislead creditors or to perpetrate a fraud on them. Domination, even total domination by a single owner, is lawful. Improper conduct is the requirement. This puts Florida among the states most protective of the entity, and it is why plaintiffs' lawyers in Florida look first for a guaranty or a personal tort rather than a piercing claim.

**What counts as improper use.** The cases find it where the owner: formed the entity to escape an existing obligation; stripped its assets after a claim arose, leaving a shell; used it to commit fraud; or ran personal and business money through one account so that creditors could not tell what belonged to whom and the owner could pull money out ahead of them. The facts that push toward piercing are timing and intent: transfers after the dispute, assets moved to a new entity with the same business, representations to the creditor that the company was solvent. The facts that push away: a legitimate business that simply failed, capital adequate when the business started, separate books even if sloppy, and a creditor who could have asked for a guaranty and did not.

**Formalities.** For corporations, courts in many states list failure to hold meetings and keep minutes among the piercing factors. Florida's § 605.0304(2) takes that off the table for LLCs. Courts still look at commingling and record-keeping, not as formality failures but as evidence on the first and second elements. The distinction is real: not holding an annual meeting proves nothing; paying your mortgage from the LLC account proves something.

**Reverse piercing and single-member LLCs.** A creditor of the member sometimes tries to reach the LLC's assets, called reverse piercing. Florida courts have recognized the theory in limited circumstances (verify). For a single-member LLC, § 605.0503 gives the member's creditor a different route, foreclosure on the interest, which lesson 6 covers. Single-member status is not itself a ground to pierce in favor of the LLC's creditors.

Courts also reason about **who the defendant should be**. A plaintiff who sues only the LLC and wins a judgment it cannot collect cannot simply add the owner later. Piercing is a claim that must be pleaded and proved, usually in the original case or in a separate action, with the owner as a party.

## Florida specifics

- Florida departs from the states that allow piercing on domination or undercapitalization alone. *Dania Jai-Alai* requires improper conduct. Say so when comparing to out-of-state cases.
- Section 605.0304(2)'s no-formalities rule is a statutory refinement that most states have in their LLC acts but Florida courts apply firmly.
- Florida's Uniform Fraudulent Transfer Act, ch. 726, is the companion tool. A creditor who cannot pierce can often unwind a transfer the owner made to hinder collection. The two claims are usually pleaded together.
- A personal guaranty is governed by contract law; a continuing guaranty covers future debts until revoked in writing. Module 03.
- Punitive damages for an owner's own fraud, and § 768.72's pleading gate, apply as in module 01.

## Angles checklist

- Did any owner sign a guaranty, or sign without showing a representative capacity?
- Did any owner personally commit the tort or make the misrepresentation?
- Was the LLC adequately capitalized for its ordinary risks when it started? Insured?
- Were personal and company funds kept apart? Were distributions taken while the company could not pay its debts?
- Were assets moved after the dispute arose? To whom, for what?
- Was the entity formed or used to escape a specific obligation?
- Is there a fraudulent transfer claim alongside the piercing claim?
- Is a responsible-person tax or wage liability in play regardless of the shield?
- Has the owner been named as a defendant with piercing pleaded?

## Drill

Rafael is the sole member of a Miami event-planning LLC. The LLC signs a $90,000 venue contract for a client's wedding; Rafael signs "Rafael Soto, Member." The LLC takes the client's $60,000 deposit, Rafael transfers $50,000 to his personal account to cover his mortgage, and the LLC then cancels the venue and cannot refund the client. Rafael forms a new LLC the next month and books the same vendors. The client sues the LLC and Rafael. List the liability issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Contract liability.** The LLC is liable for the refund. Rafael signed in a representative capacity, so he is not liable on the contract itself under § 605.0304(1).
- **Rafael's own conduct.** Taking the client's deposit and moving it to his personal account may be conversion or civil theft, § 772.11, and if he knew the LLC could not perform when he took the deposit, fraud. He is liable for his own torts regardless of the shield.
- **Unlawful distribution.** The $50,000 transfer, if it left the LLC unable to pay its debts, is a distribution in violation of § 605.0405, and Rafael is liable to the LLC under § 605.0406. The client can reach that through a creditor's remedy.
- **Piercing.** Element one is easy: sole member, total domination. Element two is the fight: the transfer of client money to pay a personal mortgage, followed by a new LLC with the same vendors, looks like use of the entity to defeat a creditor. Element three: the transfer caused the inability to refund. This is a stronger piercing case than most, because the improper use is specific and timed to the debt.
- **Fraudulent transfer.** The $50,000 and any assets moved to the new LLC are candidates under ch. 726, which may be easier than piercing.
- **Open questions.** Was the LLC solvent before the transfer? Did Rafael know the venue would cancel? What assets went to the new LLC?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. An LLC never holds meetings, has no minutes, and the sole member makes every decision alone. A creditor argues this alone justifies piercing. Result in Florida?
2. A member personally drives the company van into a customer's fence while making a delivery. Who is liable?
3. A landlord asks the LLC's member to sign the lease "individually and as Manager." What has the member just done?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No piercing. Section 605.0304(2) says failure to observe formalities is not a ground, and *Dania Jai-Alai* requires improper conduct. Domination alone, even total, is lawful.
2. Both. The member is liable for his own negligence. The LLC is vicariously liable because he was acting in the course of the business. The shield protects the member from the LLC's debts, not from his own torts.
3. Become personally liable on the lease. Signing individually is a guaranty in substance. The shield does not cover an obligation the member took on in his own name.

</details>
