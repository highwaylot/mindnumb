# Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (U.S. Supreme Court 1803)

**Tags:** `[US]` · constitutional law · judicial review · separation of powers · jurisdiction
**Used in:** [00-how-law-works/01-sources-and-hierarchy.md](../00-how-law-works/01-sources-and-hierarchy.md), [01-foundations/01-federal-and-state-courts.md](../01-foundations/01-federal-and-state-courts.md)

## One-line takeaway

Courts decide what the law is, and a statute that conflicts with the Constitution is void and will not be applied.

## Facts

In the last days of John Adams's presidency, Congress created new judgeships and the Senate confirmed Adams's nominees. William Marbury was confirmed as a justice of the peace for the District of Columbia. His commission was signed by Adams and sealed by the Secretary of State, John Marshall, who was about to become Chief Justice. It was not delivered before Thomas Jefferson took office. Jefferson told his new Secretary of State, James Madison, not to deliver the remaining commissions. Marbury never received his.

## Procedural posture

Marbury filed an original action in the U.S. Supreme Court asking for a writ of mandamus ordering Madison to deliver the commission. He relied on section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789, which he read as authorizing the Supreme Court to issue mandamus to federal officers. There was no lower court decision. Madison did not appear.

## Issue

Can the Supreme Court, acting under a statute, issue a writ of mandamus in a case that Article III of the Constitution does not place within the Court's original jurisdiction?

## Holding

No. Marbury had a legal right to the commission and the law gave him a remedy, but the Supreme Court could not grant it. Article III fixes the Court's original jurisdiction to a short list that does not include this case. To the extent section 13 added mandamus to that list, it conflicted with the Constitution and was void. The petition was dismissed.

## Reasoning

Chief Justice Marshall, writing for a unanimous Court, asked three questions in order.

First, did Marbury have a right to the commission? Yes. The appointment was complete when the commission was signed and sealed. Delivery was a ministerial act, not a discretionary one.

Second, did the law give a remedy? Yes. Where there is a legal right there is a legal remedy. Marshall distinguished political acts of the executive, which courts cannot review, from acts the law requires an officer to perform, which courts can order.

Third, could this Court issue the writ? Here Marshall read section 13 as granting original mandamus jurisdiction, then read Article III as limiting original jurisdiction to cases involving ambassadors and states. If Congress could add to that list, the constitutional text would be meaningless. He then reached the step that made the case famous. The Constitution is paramount law. A legislative act repugnant to it cannot be law. Courts must apply the law to cases before them, and when two laws conflict the court must decide which governs. "It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is." The judicial oath and the Supremacy Clause confirmed it.

The result was politically careful. The Court declared a power to review statutes while denying itself the power to order Jefferson's administration to do anything.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The strongest counterpoints are two. Jefferson's view was that each branch judges constitutionality for itself in its own sphere, and that the Court had lectured the executive in a case it admitted it could not decide. Scholars have also noted that section 13 could have been read as adding mandamus only to the Court's appellate jurisdiction, which would have avoided the constitutional question entirely. Marshall chose the reading that let him announce the power.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

This case is why a statute is not the end of the inquiry. Florida businesses have seen it in practice. Caps on damages, licensing schemes, and tax provisions have all been struck by courts as unconstitutional. When you read a statute, ask whether it has been challenged and whether a court has limited it. The same principle runs down the hierarchy. A regulation that goes beyond its statute is invalid, and an ordinance that conflicts with a state statute is preempted. Module 00, Lesson 1 builds the whole hierarchy on this idea.

It also sets the habit of separating "do I have a right" from "which court can enforce it." A clear right with no available forum is worth nothing until you find the right forum.

## Florida connection

Florida courts exercise the same power under the Florida Constitution. The Florida Supreme Court reviews statutes against the Florida Constitution and strikes those that conflict, as it did with the noneconomic damages cap in wrongful death medical malpractice cases in *Estate of McCall v. United States*, 134 So. 3d 894 (Fla. 2014) (verify citation). Separation of powers is written into Fla. Const. art. II, § 3. The Florida Supreme Court's rulemaking power over court procedure comes from art. V, § 2, which is why a statute that tries to dictate procedure can be struck. Florida courts presume statutes constitutional and read them to avoid constitutional conflict where possible, which echoes the care Marshall showed in reaching the question only when he had to.
