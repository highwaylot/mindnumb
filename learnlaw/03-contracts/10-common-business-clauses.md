# Common business clauses and what they actually do

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 10 · **Time:** ~55 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

The back half of every commercial contract is boilerplate nobody reads until something goes wrong. Those clauses decide who pays when a customer gets hurt, whether you can walk away, where you will be sued, and whether you will pay the other side's lawyer. This lesson walks the clauses that come up most and flags where Florida law changes the answer.

## The rule

Each clause is a rule the parties wrote for themselves. Courts enforce them as written unless a statute or public policy intervenes.

**Indemnification.** One party agrees to cover the other's losses, usually from third-party claims. Read for: whose claims are covered, whether it covers the indemnitee's own negligence, whether defense costs are included, and whether there is a cap. Indemnity for the indemnitee's own negligence must be stated in clear and unequivocal terms. *Charles Poe Masonry, Inc. v. Spring Lock Scaffolding Rental Equipment Co.*, 374 So. 2d 487 (Fla. 1979). In construction contracts, § 725.06, Fla. Stat., voids indemnity for the indemnitee's own negligence unless the clause has a monetary cap that bears a reasonable commercial relationship to the contract and is part of the bid documents (verify current requirements).

**Limitation of liability and consequential damages waiver.** Caps the amount recoverable, often at fees paid, and excludes lost profits and other indirect losses. Enforceable between commercial parties. Does not cover fraud or intentional misconduct. Watch for carve-outs: indemnity obligations, confidentiality breaches, and gross negligence are often excluded from the cap.

**Term and termination.** For cause, with or without a cure period; for convenience, on notice. A termination-for-convenience right is enforceable in Florida and its exercise does not breach the implied covenant. Lesson 5.

**Confidentiality.** Defines confidential information, excludes public information, sets a duration, and requires return or destruction. It supports a trade secret claim under ch. 688 by showing reasonable efforts to maintain secrecy. Module 06.

**Non-solicitation and non-compete.** Restrictive covenants on customers, employees, or competition. Governed by § 542.335: must be in a signed writing, supported by a legitimate business interest, and reasonable in time, area, and scope. Module 05, lesson 6, covers this and the 2025 CHOICE Act.

**Force majeure.** Excuses performance delayed by listed events beyond a party's control. Read narrowly. Lists are usually treated as exhaustive unless there is a catch-all, and even a catch-all is read in light of the listed items. Requires notice and mitigation. It displaces the common law impossibility doctrine within its scope.

**Choice of law.** Picks the governing law. Florida enforces the parties' choice unless the chosen law has no reasonable relationship to the deal or applying it would violate a strong Florida public policy. Statutory consumer and employee protections often cannot be contracted around.

**Forum selection and jury waiver.** Fixes where suits must be filed. Florida enforces mandatory forum clauses and distinguishes them from permissive ones. A pre-dispute jury trial waiver is enforceable if knowing and voluntary, and courts look at conspicuousness, bargaining power, and sophistication.

**Attorney fees.** Shifts fees to the loser or to one party. Under § 57.105(7), a clause that gives fees to one party is read to give them to the other if the other prevails. The "prevailing party" is the one who won on the significant issues. *Moritz v. Hoyt Enterprises, Inc.*, 604 So. 2d 807 (Fla. 1992).

**Assignment.** Without a clause, most contract rights are assignable, but duties cannot be delegated if the other party has a substantial interest in personal performance. A "no assignment" clause bars delegation and, unless it says an assignment is void, often does not invalidate an assignment of the right to payment. Change-of-control language matters if the business may be sold.

**Entire agreement (merger).** States that the writing is the complete deal. Triggers the parol evidence rule. Lesson 4. Does not bar fraud in the inducement in Florida, with limits.

**Notice.** Sets how and where formal notices go. A termination notice sent the wrong way can be ineffective.

**Also common:** severability; no-waiver, though conduct can still waive; survival; counterparts and electronic signatures; time is of the essence; independent contractor status; insurance requirements.

## How courts reason about it

Courts treat boilerplate as bargained-for between businesses, even when nobody bargained. The policy is predictability. If a court will not enforce a clear cap because it seems harsh, no cap can be trusted. So the typical opinion enforces the clause and then asks whether a narrow doctrine takes it out: ambiguity read against the drafter, a statute, public policy, fraud, or unconscionability.

A few recurring fights:

- **Indemnity for your own negligence.** Courts dislike making one party the insurer of the other's carelessness, so they demand explicit language. "Any and all claims" is often held not clear enough to cover the indemnitee's own fault. The clause must say "including claims arising from indemnitee's negligence" or words equally plain.
- **Force majeure and foreseeability.** Courts ask whether the event was within the clause and whether it actually prevented performance rather than making it more expensive. A supply chain delay that raises costs is not force majeure. A government order that bans the activity is.
- **Reciprocal fees.** Section 57.105(7) was passed to stop form drafters from giving themselves a one-way fee right. Courts apply it to the same type of action the clause covers. If the clause covers only "actions to collect amounts due," a defendant who wins a different kind of claim may not get fees.
- **Choice of law and public policy.** A Florida court will honor a Delaware choice-of-law clause for an LLC agreement, but it will apply Florida's non-compete statute, wage law, and FDUTPA where those statutes reach the parties.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on enforcement of commercial boilerplate. The Florida-specific points are collected here.

- § 57.105(7): reciprocal attorney fee provisions.
- § 725.06: limits on indemnity clauses in construction contracts (verify current text).
- § 542.335: restrictive covenants; CHOICE Act §§ 542.41 to 542.45 (verify numbering).
- § 668.50: electronic signatures and counterparts.
- Ch. 682: Revised Florida Arbitration Code.
- *Charles Poe Masonry*: indemnity for own negligence requires clear and unequivocal terms.
- *Moritz v. Hoyt Enterprises*: prevailing party test.
- Florida enforces termination-for-convenience and sole-discretion clauses without an implied good-faith override. Lesson 5.
- Florida courts apply contra proferentem to form contracts and insurance policies. Lesson 4.
- Jury waivers are enforced in Florida if knowing and voluntary (verify which party bears the burden; DCAs differ).

## Angles checklist

- Read the indemnity clause for who is covered, whose negligence, defense costs, and caps.
- Read the cap: what is excluded from it, and does it swallow the remedy?
- Who can terminate, when, with what notice, and what survives?
- What does force majeure list? Is there notice and mitigation language?
- Which law, which forum, jury or no jury, arbitration or court?
- Who gets fees? Does § 57.105(7) make it mutual?
- Can either side assign? What happens on a sale of the business?
- Is there a merger clause, and what oral promises does it cut off?
- Does the notice clause match how you actually communicate?
- Flip it: which of these clauses did you accept in a vendor's form that now binds you?

## Drill

A Gainesville IT services firm signs an MSA with a medical practice. Key terms: the firm indemnifies the practice for "any and all claims arising from the services"; the firm's liability is capped at fees paid in the prior 12 months; the practice may terminate for convenience on 30 days notice; fees go to "Provider in any action to enforce payment"; Florida law and Alachua County courts. A ransomware attack through a server the firm misconfigured exposes patient data. The practice incurs $200,000 (verify) in breach response costs, terminates the MSA immediately, and demands indemnity. Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Indemnity.** The breach response costs are the practice's own losses, not a third-party claim. Many indemnity clauses cover only third-party claims; "any and all claims" is ambiguous and will be read against whoever drafted it. If patients sue the practice, those are third-party claims squarely within the clause. Whether the clause covers the practice's own negligence, if any, is not at issue because the firm's misconfiguration is the alleged fault.
- **The cap.** Liability is capped at 12 months of fees. The practice will argue the indemnity obligation is carved out of the cap; the text as described does not say so, so the cap likely applies to the indemnity too. The practice may also argue gross negligence or that the cap is unconscionable, both uphill between businesses. The practice's recovery may be far less than $200,000.
- **Termination.** The practice terminated immediately, not on 30 days notice. Unless the MSA had a for-cause right it followed, the early termination is itself a breach, though damages may be small.
- **Fees.** The clause covers only payment actions. Under § 57.105(7) it is reciprocal, but this dispute is about indemnity, so neither side may have a fee right.
- **Regulatory overlay.** HIPAA and § 501.171 notice duties fall on the practice regardless of the contract. A business associate agreement, if any, may have its own indemnity and cap terms.
- **Likely outcome.** The practice recovers something under indemnity or breach, capped at 12 months of fees, in Alachua County under Florida law. Patient suits, if any, are covered by indemnity up to the same cap.
- **Facts that would change it.** An express carve-out of indemnity and data breaches from the cap would change everything. Cyber insurance on either side may be the real source of recovery.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A vendor's contract says "Customer shall indemnify Vendor for all claims." Does that cover claims caused by the vendor's own negligence in Florida?
2. A contract gives attorney fees to the landlord only. The tenant wins a suit over the landlord's failure to repair. Who pays fees?
3. A force majeure clause lists "fire, flood, hurricane, war, and labor strikes." A supplier's sole subcontractor goes bankrupt. Excused?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Probably not. Under *Charles Poe Masonry*, indemnity for the indemnitee's own negligence must be clear and unequivocal. "All claims" has repeatedly been held insufficient. The clause would need to say so expressly.
2. The tenant can recover fees from the landlord under § 57.105(7), if the suit is the kind of action the clause covers. A general "any action under this lease" clause would cover it; a narrow "action to collect rent" clause might not.
3. Almost certainly not. Bankruptcy of a subcontractor is not listed, there is no catch-all, and even with a catch-all it is a commercial risk of the supplier's own choosing. The supplier should have bargained for a broader clause or a second source.

</details>
