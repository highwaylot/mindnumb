# Partnerships and the accidental partnership

**Module:** 02 · **Lesson:** 8 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Nobody files anything to become a general partner. Two people who run a business together and split the money are partners under Florida law whether they know it or not, and each is personally liable for everything the other does in the business. The partnership is also the template for every other co-ownership form: the fiduciary duties, agency rules, and exit mechanics in LLC law all descend from it.

## The rule

Chapter 620, Fla. Stat., contains the Revised Uniform Partnership Act as adopted in Florida, §§ 620.8101 and following, plus the Revised Uniform Limited Partnership Act, §§ 620.1101 and following.

**Formation.** "The association of two or more persons to carry on as co-owners a business for profit forms a partnership, whether or not the persons intend to form a partnership," § 620.8202(1). No writing, filing, or name is required. The rules of construction in § 620.8202(3) do the work in a dispute:

- Joint ownership of property does not by itself establish a partnership, even if the co-owners share profits from it.
- Sharing gross returns does not by itself establish one.
- **A person who receives a share of the profits of a business is presumed to be a partner**, unless the profits were received in payment of a debt, as wages or compensation to an independent contractor, as rent, as an annuity or retirement benefit, as interest on a loan, or for the sale of goodwill or other property.

The party claiming a partnership bears the burden of proving it, but the profit-share presumption shifts the burden once profit sharing is shown.

**Liability.** All partners are jointly and severally liable for all partnership obligations, § 620.8306(1). A person admitted as a partner is not personally liable for obligations incurred before admission. A creditor must generally obtain a judgment against the partnership and exhaust its assets before levying on a partner's personal assets, § 620.8307(4) (verify).

**Agency.** Each partner is an agent of the partnership for the purpose of its business. An act of a partner in the ordinary course binds the partnership unless the partner lacked authority and the third party knew it, § 620.8301. A partnership may file a statement of partnership authority to limit or expand this, § 620.8303.

**Default rights among partners**, § 620.8401. Each partner shares equally in profits and bears losses in proportion to profit share. Each has equal rights in management. Ordinary matters are decided by a majority of partners; acts outside the ordinary course and amendments require unanimous consent. No partner is paid for services except in winding up.

**Fiduciary duties**, § 620.8404. The duty of loyalty: account for profits and benefits from the business or use of its property, refrain from adverse dealing, refrain from competing. The duty of care: refrain from grossly negligent or reckless conduct, intentional misconduct, or knowing violation of law. These are the model for § 605.04091.

**Partnership by estoppel**, § 620.8308. A person who represents himself, or consents to being represented, as a partner is liable to a third party who extended credit in reliance on the representation.

**Dissociation and dissolution.** A partner may dissociate at any time, § 620.8601. In a partnership at will, a partner's notice of withdrawal dissolves the partnership, § 620.8801(1), unless the remaining partners agree to continue. If the business continues, the dissociated partner is entitled to a buyout at the greater of liquidation value or going-concern value, § 620.8701. Lesson 9 covers the LLC counterparts.

**Limited liability versions.** A general partnership may register as a **limited liability partnership** under § 620.9001 and following by filing a statement of qualification; partners are then not liable for partnership obligations solely by reason of being partners, § 620.8306(3). A **limited partnership** has at least one general partner with full liability and limited partners who are not liable beyond their contributions, § 620.1303 (verify). Both are filed with the Department of State and carry annual fees (verify).

**Joint venture.** A partnership for a single transaction or limited purpose. Florida requires a community of interest, joint control, a joint proprietary interest in the subject matter, a right to share profits, and a duty to share losses. *Kislak v. Kreedian*, 95 So. 2d 510 (Fla. 1957). Partnership law applies to it.

## How courts reason about it

**Finding a partnership nobody signed.** Courts start with § 620.8202. The question is not what the parties called themselves but what they did: did they share profits, share control, hold themselves out together, contribute capital or labor to a common enterprise? The profit-share presumption is powerful because it flips the burden. A defendant who paid a helper "a cut of the profits" must prove the cut was wages. The facts that push toward partnership: equal say in decisions, both names on accounts or leases, joint filing of a tax return, the word "partner" in emails and on the website. The facts that push away: fixed pay, one person clearly in charge, a written contractor or employment agreement, and the helper having no exposure to losses. Courts treat sharing losses as the strongest single indicator of co-ownership, because employees do not absorb losses.

**Why the rule is strict.** Partnership liability protects outsiders. A supplier that dealt with two people extended credit to both, and the law will not let one say afterward that she was only an investor. The same policy explains partnership by estoppel: representation plus reliance equals liability, whatever the truth.

**Fiduciary duty between co-venturers.** *Meinhard v. Salmon* was a joint venture, not a formal partnership. Cardozo treated the co-adventurers as owing each other the loyalty of partners and held that Salmon could not take the renewal lease for himself. The reasoning applies to every accidental partnership: once the relationship exists, each partner must account for business opportunities and side profits. The partner who did not know he was a partner can still be sued for breaching duties he did not know he had, and can sue for breaches against him. *Guth v. Loft* gives the opportunity test courts borrow.

**Why an entity is the answer.** *Walkovszky v. Carlton* shows how strong the entity shield is. A partnership has none. Courts routinely see the sequence: two friends start, one signs a lease, the business fails, and the landlord sues the friend who never signed anything. Under § 620.8306 the landlord wins if a partnership existed.

**Conversion.** A partnership may convert to an LLC under §§ 605.1041 and following (verify). The converted entity is the same entity, so pre-conversion debts follow it and the partners remain liable for them.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the uniform act. Its text and section numbers map onto RUPA with the prefix 620.8.
- Florida's joint venture elements from *Kislak* are stated as five, including the duty to share losses, which some states omit. A plaintiff claiming a joint venture must plead all five.
- Florida has no general statute of frauds for partnership agreements; an oral agreement to form a partnership is enforceable, though one that cannot be performed within a year may run into § 725.01.
- An LLP must file an annual report and pay a fee per partner (verify amount), and loses LLP status if it fails to.
- The Florida Revised Uniform Limited Partnership Act of 2005 governs limited partnerships, §§ 620.1101 to 620.2205, with its own formation filing and $1,000 (verify) fee.

## Angles checklist

- Did two or more people share profits of a business? If so, which § 620.8202(3) exception applies, if any?
- Who had control? Who bore losses? Who signed what?
- Did anyone hold the other out as a partner to a creditor who relied?
- Which obligations did the partnership incur, and when did each partner join?
- Did any partner take a business opportunity or side profit?
- Has anyone given notice of withdrawal? Does that dissolve the partnership?
- What is the buyout value, and has the partnership been wound up?
- Can the exposure be fixed going forward by forming an LLC or registering as an LLP?

## Drill

Tomas runs a food truck in Gainesville. His friend Jess works it four nights a week. They agree she gets "half of whatever we clear" each week, and she puts $8,000 of her savings into a new generator. Jess posts on social media as "co-owner." Tomas signs a two-year commissary lease in his own name. Jess orders $15,000 of catering supplies on credit in the truck's name. Then they fall out and Tomas tells her not to come back. List the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Partnership.** Jess shares net profits, contributed capital, works the business, and holds herself out as co-owner. The § 620.8202(3) presumption applies and the wages exception is weak, since half of net profits is not a wage. A court would likely find a partnership at will.
- **The supply order.** As a partner, Jess bound the partnership in the ordinary course under § 620.8301. Tomas is jointly and severally liable for the $15,000 under § 620.8306, after the supplier exhausts partnership assets.
- **The lease.** Tomas signed personally and is liable on it. Whether it is a partnership obligation that Jess shares depends on whether it was entered in the ordinary course of the business; a commissary lease for the business likely is, so Jess may be liable for her share on contribution.
- **Partnership by estoppel.** Even if no partnership existed, the "co-owner" posts could make Jess liable to any creditor who relied on them, § 620.8308.
- **Expulsion.** Tomas cannot simply fire a partner. Telling her not to return is either an expulsion, which the default rules do not permit without unanimous agreement or a court order, or a dissociation by Tomas's own express will, which dissolves an at-will partnership. Either way Jess is entitled to a buyout under § 620.8701 or to a share on winding up, including credit for the generator.
- **Fiduciary duties.** Both owe each other loyalty and an accounting. If Tomas keeps running the business with Jess's generator, he must account for her share.
- **Open questions.** Any texts about ownership? Did the supplier know Jess as a partner? Who owns the generator?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A bakery pays its manager 10% of net profits as a bonus under a written employment agreement. Is the manager a partner?
2. Two partners in an at-will partnership have no written agreement. One wants out. What happens to the business?
3. A partnership registers as an LLP. A supplier who sold to it before registration is unpaid. Are the partners liable?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Profits received as wages or compensation for services fall within the § 620.8202(3) exception, and the written employment agreement makes the characterization clear. The presumption does not arise.
2. Notice of withdrawal dissolves the partnership under § 620.8801(1). The remaining partner may continue the business by agreement, in which case the departing partner is bought out at the greater of liquidation or going-concern value under § 620.8701. Otherwise the business is wound up and the net assets split per § 620.8401.
3. Yes, for that obligation. The LLP shield applies to obligations incurred while the partnership is an LLP. Pre-registration debts remain the partners' personal obligations under § 620.8306.

</details>
