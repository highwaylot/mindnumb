# PNR, Inc. v. Beacon Property Management, Inc., 842 So. 2d 773 (Fla. 2003)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · consumer protection · FDUTPA · trade or commerce · commercial leases
**Used in:** [`07-regulatory-compliance/03-fdutpa.md`](../07-regulatory-compliance/03-fdutpa.md), [`06-property-ip/01-commercial-leases.md`](../06-property-ip/01-commercial-leases.md), [`08-disputes-procedure/04-proposals-for-settlement-and-fees.md`](../08-disputes-procedure/04-proposals-for-settlement-and-fees.md)

## One-line takeaway

FDUTPA reaches a single unfair or deceptive act in a single transaction; the plaintiff need not show a pattern of conduct or that the defendant deals with the consuming public generally.

## Facts

PNR operated a restaurant in a shopping center. Beacon Property Management managed the center for the owner. PNR alleged that Beacon, in the course of the landlord-tenant relationship, engaged in deceptive and unfair conduct that damaged the restaurant, including misrepresentations and conduct connected with the lease and PNR's efforts to operate and sell the business (verify specific allegations). PNR sued under the Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act, among other claims.

## Procedural posture

The trial court entered judgment against PNR on the FDUTPA claim. The Fourth District Court of Appeal affirmed, reasoning that FDUTPA did not apply because the dispute was a single landlord-tenant dispute and Beacon's conduct did not involve the kind of trade or commerce aimed at the consuming public that the statute was meant to regulate. The Fourth District certified a question of great public importance to the Florida Supreme Court.

## Issue

Does FDUTPA apply to a single unfair or deceptive act arising out of a single transaction, such as a commercial lease dispute, when the defendant is not alleged to have engaged in a pattern of such conduct or to deal with the consuming public at large?

## Holding

Yes. The Florida Supreme Court held that FDUTPA's definition of "trade or commerce" is broad enough to cover a single transaction, that the statute does not require proof of a pattern or practice, and that it does not require that the defendant's conduct be directed at the consuming public generally. The court quashed the Fourth District's decision on the FDUTPA claim.

## Reasoning

Justice Lewis started with the statutory text. Section 501.204(1), Fla. Stat., declares unlawful "unfair methods of competition, unconscionable acts or practices, and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce." Section 501.203(8) defines trade or commerce as the advertising, soliciting, providing, offering, or distributing of any good or service, or any property, "whether tangible or intangible, or any other article, commodity, or thing of value, wherever situated." A lease of commercial space is property. Managing it is a service. The transaction fit the words.

The court rejected the Fourth District's reading that the statute requires a pattern or a consumer-facing business. Nothing in the text says "pattern." The act applies to "any" unfair act "in the conduct of any trade or commerce." The court noted that the Legislature in 2001 had amended § 501.211(2) to replace "consumer" with "person" in the damages provision, broadening who may sue and confirming that the statute is not limited to traditional consumer transactions. The statute's stated purposes in § 501.202 include protecting "the consuming public and legitimate business enterprises" from those who engage in unfair or deceptive acts.

The court was careful about scope. It did not hold that every breach of a lease is a FDUTPA violation. The plaintiff must still prove an act that is unfair or deceptive under the statute's standards, causation, and actual damages. It held only that the single-transaction nature of the dispute did not take it outside the statute.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Justice Wells dissented (verify the author and whether any justice joined). The dissent's concern was that reading the statute to reach ordinary commercial disputes between sophisticated parties turns every contract case into a statutory claim with attorney fees and that the Legislature had a consumer protection purpose in mind. The majority answered that the text controls and the Legislature could narrow it. The counterpoint has influence in practice: Florida courts frequently dismiss FDUTPA counts that merely restate a breach of contract without alleging a deceptive or unfair act.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

FDUTPA is now a routine add-on count in business-to-business litigation in Florida. A landlord, supplier, franchisor, contractor, or service provider can face it based on a single transaction. The stakes are actual damages plus a discretionary attorney fee award to the prevailing party under § 501.2105, Fla. Stat. The fee provision runs both ways, so a weak FDUTPA claim can cost the plaintiff. For a business defending, the questions are whether the conduct was deceptive or unfair under the statute's standards, whether it caused actual damages, and whether the claim is really a contract claim in disguise.

## Florida connection

This is Florida law. The elements of a FDUTPA damages claim are a deceptive act or unfair practice, causation, and actual damages. "Deceptive" means likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably in the circumstances. "Unfair" means a practice that offends established public policy and is immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous, or substantially injurious to consumers, a standard taken from the Federal Trade Commission, which § 501.204(2) directs courts to consider. Actual damages do not include consequential damages such as lost profits under most district court decisions (verify). The limitation period is four years, § 95.11(3), Fla. Stat. (verify subsection). After *PNR*, the fight in commercial cases is over whether the act was deceptive or unfair, not over whether FDUTPA applies at all.
