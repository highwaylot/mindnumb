# Jacob & Youngs, Inc. v. Kent, 230 N.Y. 239 (New York Court of Appeals 1921)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · substantial performance · conditions · damages · construction
**Used in:** [03-contracts/05-performance-conditions-breach.md](../03-contracts/05-performance-conditions-breach.md), [03-contracts/07-remedies.md](../03-contracts/07-remedies.md), [06-property-ip/01-commercial-leases.md](../06-property-ip/01-commercial-leases.md)

## One-line takeaway

A trivial and innocent deviation from a contract's specifications does not forfeit the right to payment; the measure of damages is the difference in value, not the cost of tearing out and replacing the work.

## Facts

Jacob & Youngs built a country house for George Kent for about $77,000. The contract specified that all wrought iron pipe be "well galvanized, lap welded pipe of the grade known as 'standard pipe' of Reading manufacture." A subcontractor installed pipe of the same grade and quality made by other manufacturers, including Cohoes, for much of the plumbing. The substitution was inadvertent. It was discovered after the house was nearly complete and the pipe was encased in the walls. The architect refused to issue the final certificate. Kent demanded that the builder tear out the walls and replace the pipe with Reading pipe. The builder refused. Kent withheld the final payment of $3,483.46.

## Procedural posture

The builder sued for the balance. At trial the builder offered evidence that the substituted pipe was the same in quality, appearance, market value, and cost as Reading pipe. The trial judge excluded the evidence and directed a verdict for Kent. The Appellate Division reversed and ordered a new trial. Kent appealed to the Court of Appeals.

## Issue

Where a builder has substantially performed but has innocently deviated from a specification in a way that is trivial in effect, may the owner withhold payment, and is the owner's damage measured by the cost of replacement or the difference in value?

## Holding

The builder could recover the balance. The deviation was trivial and innocent, performance was substantial, and the owner's remedy was an allowance for the difference in value between the pipe specified and the pipe installed, which on the evidence was nominal. Judgment was directed for the builder.

## Reasoning

Judge Cardozo, for a four-to-three majority, began by acknowledging that the parties could have made Reading pipe an express condition of payment. They did not use the language of condition. Where the contract does not clearly make a term a condition, the court must decide whether the omission is so significant that it should defeat the right to payment, or whether it is a breach that gives rise to damages but leaves the duty to pay in place.

That question turns on the purpose to be served, the desire to be gratified, the excuse for deviation, and the cruelty of enforced adherence. A deviation that is willful or that goes to the essence of the bargain is not excused. One that is trivial and innocent, in a contract of this size, is. The court was unwilling to read a specification of manufacturer as a condition when the owner got pipe of identical quality and could not tell the difference.

On damages, the rule is cost of completion unless that cost is grossly and unfairly out of proportion to the good to be attained. Here the cost of replacement would have required demolishing substantial parts of the house to achieve nothing of value. The measure was therefore the difference in value, which was nothing or nearly so.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Judge McLaughlin dissented, joined by two judges. The contract was explicit. Kent was entitled to what he contracted for, whatever his reasons, and the builder had no right to substitute. The builder had also failed to inspect and had not shown the omission was beyond its control. Whether Reading pipe was better or worse was irrelevant; a man may contract for what he wants. The dissent is the standard argument for freedom of contract and against courts rewriting specifications after the fact. The majority's answer was that the parties' probable intent, not the literal text, governs where the text is silent on consequences.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

For anyone who builds, installs, or delivers to specification, the case sets the default: small innocent deviations do not cost you the whole contract price, but they do cost you the difference in value, and willful deviations get no such protection. For anyone who hires such work, the case shows how to get the other result. Make the specification an express condition of payment. State that time or brand is of the essence. Require written approval of substitutions. Courts enforce clear conditions even when the result is harsh, so the drafting matters.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the doctrine of substantial performance in construction and other service contracts, and applies it to decide both whether a contractor may recover and what the owner may set off. For the damages measure, the Florida Supreme Court adopted the Restatement rule in *Grossman Holdings Ltd. v. Hourihan*, 414 So. 2d 1037 (Fla. 1982): the owner may recover the reasonable cost of completing the work in accordance with the contract, unless that would involve unreasonable economic waste, in which case the measure is the difference in value (verify citation). Florida courts also hold that a willful or bad-faith deviation defeats substantial performance (verify a citation before relying on it). Florida's construction lien law in chapter 713, Fla. Stat., and the mandatory pre-suit notice process for construction defect claims under chapter 558 add procedural layers that have no counterpart in this 1921 case.
