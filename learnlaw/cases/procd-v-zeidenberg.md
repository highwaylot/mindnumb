# ProCD, Inc. v. Zeidenberg, 86 F.3d 1447 (7th Cir. 1996)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · formation · shrinkwrap licenses · UCC · copyright preemption
**Used in:** [`03-contracts/09-online-and-electronic-contracts.md`](../03-contracts/09-online-and-electronic-contracts.md), [`03-contracts/01-formation.md`](../03-contracts/01-formation.md), [`03-contracts/08-ucc-sales-of-goods.md`](../03-contracts/08-ucc-sales-of-goods.md), [`06-property-ip/03-copyright.md`](../06-property-ip/03-copyright.md)

## One-line takeaway

Terms inside the box are enforceable when the outside gives notice that terms are inside, the buyer can read them, and the buyer can return the product instead of accepting; keeping the product after reading the terms is acceptance.

## Facts

ProCD compiled more than 3,000 telephone directories into a database called SelectPhone and sold it on CD-ROM. It charged consumers a low price and charged commercial users much more. To keep that price structure, every consumer package carried a license. The outside of the box said the software came with restrictions stated in an enclosed license. The license was printed in the manual, appeared on the screen each time the software ran, and limited use to non-commercial purposes.

Matthew Zeidenberg bought a consumer package in Madison, Wisconsin in 1994, formed a company called Silken Mountain Web Services, and offered the data on the internet for less than ProCD charged commercial users. He had read the license. He ignored it.

## Procedural posture

ProCD sued Zeidenberg and his company in the Western District of Wisconsin for breach of the license and for copyright infringement. The district court granted judgment for Zeidenberg. It held that the license was not part of the contract because its terms were not on the outside of the box when the sale was made, and that even if it were, the Copyright Act preempted a contract claim that tried to protect uncopyrightable facts. ProCD appealed to the Seventh Circuit.

## Issue

Is a license whose terms are inside the package, and which the buyer can reject by returning the product, an enforceable contract, and does federal copyright law preempt it?

## Holding

Yes, the license is enforceable, and no, it is not preempted. Judge Easterbrook held that shrinkwrap licenses are enforceable unless their terms are objectionable on grounds that apply to contracts generally. The vendor may invite acceptance by conduct, and a buyer who has the chance to read the terms and return the product, but instead keeps and uses it, accepts the terms. A contract right is not "equivalent" to a copyright right and so is not preempted.

## Reasoning

The court treated the transaction under UCC Article 2 and asked who was the master of the offer. Under § 2-204 a contract can be made in any manner sufficient to show agreement, and the vendor can set how acceptance happens. ProCD proposed a contract that the buyer would accept by using the software after having a chance to read the license. Section 2-606 gives a buyer the right to inspect goods and reject them. Zeidenberg inspected, read the license, and kept the product. That was acceptance.

Easterbrook pointed to everyday transactions where terms come after payment: insurance policies, airline tickets, warranties inside appliance boxes. Commerce would not work if every term had to be on the outside of the package. The notice on the box was enough to tell the buyer that terms followed.

The court also explained why the license mattered. Under *Feist Publications v. Rural Telephone*, the telephone data itself was not copyrightable. ProCD could not stop copying with copyright. Contract was the only tool that could protect it.

On preemption, the Copyright Act preempts state rights "equivalent" to copyright. A copyright is a right against the world. A contract binds only its parties. Zeidenberg's promise not to use the data commercially was not equivalent to a copyright and could be enforced.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The strongest counterpoint is the view taken in *Step-Saver Data Systems v. Wyse Technology*, 939 F.2d 91 (3d Cir. 1991), and *Klocek v. Gateway, Inc.*, 104 F. Supp. 2d 1332 (D. Kan. 2000): the contract forms when money changes hands at the counter, and later terms are proposals for addition under § 2-207 that do not bind a consumer without express assent. On preemption, critics argue that letting contracts lock up uncopyrightable facts undermines the policy of *Feist*. Courts have mostly followed *ProCD*, but the preemption point remains contested where the "contract" is a mass-market license nobody negotiates.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

This is the foundation for every software license, terms-of-service, and "by using this product you agree" arrangement. The lessons are concrete. Give notice before or at the sale that terms exist. Make the terms available to read. Give a real way to decline, such as a return or cancellation. On the other side, if you buy a product or sign up for a service and keep using it after seeing the terms, expect to be bound by them.

## Florida connection

Florida follows this approach. Florida's UCC has the same provisions, §§ 672.204 and 672.206, Fla. Stat., and the Florida Uniform Electronic Transaction Act, § 668.50, Fla. Stat., gives electronic records and signatures the same effect as paper. Florida courts enforce shrinkwrap and clickwrap terms where the user had reasonable notice and manifested assent, and they apply ordinary contract defenses to the terms themselves. Where notice is poor, Florida courts refuse enforcement. *Vitacost.com, Inc. v. McCants*, 210 So. 3d 761 (Fla. 4th DCA 2017) (verify citation) declined to compel arbitration where the link to the terms was not conspicuous and the customer was not required to assent.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Does the outcome change? Suppose the outside of the box said nothing about an enclosed license. Would Zeidenberg be bound?
2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What facts about the sale or the terms would have made the license unenforceable on ordinary contract grounds?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? You sell a hardware device with firmware governed by terms shown on first boot. A customer resells it after using it. Who is bound, and by what?
