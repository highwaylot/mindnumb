# How to read a statute

**Module:** 00 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Most of the law that touches a Florida business day to day is statutory: the LLC Act, the wage statute, the sales tax code, FDUTPA, the non-compete statute. Statutes are written to be precise and are therefore hard to read. Once you know the structure and the few rules courts use to interpret them, you can read a section yourself, know what you do not know, and spot when someone is quoting half of it.

## The rule

### Anatomy of a Florida Statutes chapter

Take chapter 605, the Florida Revised Limited Liability Company Act.

- **Short title** (§ 605.0101): the name you cite.
- **Definitions** (§ 605.0102): read this first, every time. Statutes define ordinary words in non-ordinary ways. "Person," "record," "transferee," "distribution," and "manager" each have a defined meaning in chapter 605, and the definition controls over the dictionary.
- **Scope and purpose** sections: what the chapter covers and sometimes what it does not.
- **Operative sections**: the rules. Grouped into "parts" (Part II formation, Part IV relations of members, Part V transferable interests, Part VII dissolution, and so on).
- **Default rules and mandatory rules**: chapter 605 is mostly defaults that an operating agreement can change. § 605.0105 lists what an operating agreement *cannot* do. That section is the key to the whole chapter.
- **Remedies and enforcement**: what happens on breach, who can sue, what a court can order.
- **Effective dates and transitional provisions**: usually in the session law, not the codified statute. Matters when events straddle an amendment.

### Section numbering

"§ 605.04091" reads as chapter 605, section 04091. The digits after the decimal are a section number, not a decimal fraction; § 605.0410 comes after § 605.04091 in the chapter, not before. Subsections nest: § 605.0105(3)(b) is subsection (3), paragraph (b). Florida uses (1), (a), 1., a. for the four levels.

### Words that carry weight

- **"Shall"** means mandatory. **"May"** means permissive. **"Must"** is Florida's modern word for mandatory in newer statutes.
- **"And"** versus **"or"**: conjunctive versus disjunctive. Elements joined by "and" must all be met; "or" means any one.
- **"Notwithstanding"**: this provision wins over whatever it names.
- **"Subject to"**: this provision loses to whatever it names.
- **"Except as otherwise provided in"**: go read the exception before you rely on the rule.
- **"Including" / "including but not limited to"**: the list that follows is examples, not a complete set. A bare list without "including" is usually exhaustive.
- **"Person"** usually includes entities. Check the definition.
- **"Knowingly," "willfully," "intentionally"**: mental-state words that change what has to be proven and often change the penalty.

### Canons of construction

These are the rules of thumb courts use to resolve ambiguity. Florida courts apply them and will tell you which one decided the case.

1. **Plain meaning.** If the text is clear, that ends it. Florida courts say this often and mean it. Legislative history is rarely consulted when the text is unambiguous.
2. **Read the whole statute.** A word is read in light of the sections around it. Courts avoid readings that make another section pointless.
3. **Specific over general.** A specific provision controls over a general one on the same subject.
4. **Later over earlier.** A later-enacted statute controls over an earlier inconsistent one.
5. ***Expressio unius.*** Listing some things implies exclusion of others not listed. If a statute lists three exceptions, there is no fourth.
6. ***Ejusdem generis.*** A general term at the end of a list is read to cover things of the same kind as the listed items. "Cars, trucks, motorcycles, and other vehicles" probably does not include boats.
7. ***Noscitur a sociis.*** A word is known by its companions. "Fraud, deceit, and misrepresentation" colors each term.
8. **Statutes in derogation of the common law are strictly construed.** If a statute changes a common-law rule, courts read the change narrowly.
9. **Remedial statutes are liberally construed.** Consumer protection statutes like FDUTPA get a broad reading to achieve their purpose. These two canons pull in opposite directions and courts choose between them.
10. **Avoid absurd results.** A reading that produces an absurdity is rejected even if the words allow it.
11. **Constitutional avoidance.** If one reading raises a constitutional problem and another does not, take the one that does not.
12. **Penal statutes are read in favor of the accused (lenity).** Matters for criminal and quasi-criminal provisions.

### Where the Legislature answers a case

A statute often exists because a court decided something the Legislature did not like. § 605.0503 (charging orders) answers *Olmstead*. HB 837 (2023) rewrote comparative fault and limitation periods after decades of tort decisions. When you read a statute, ask what it was reacting to.

## How courts reason about it

A Florida statutory-interpretation opinion usually runs in this order:

1. Quote the text.
2. Say whether the text is plain. If it is, apply it and stop. Florida Supreme Court opinions since about 2019 are emphatic that courts should not look past clear text to legislative intent.
3. If ambiguous, apply the canons, read the statute as a whole, and look at related statutes.
4. Only then consider legislative history (staff analyses, bill titles, committee reports), and with caution.
5. Explain why the reading chosen avoids absurdity or constitutional problems.

Watch for the move where a court says "the Legislature knows how to say X when it means X, and it did not say X here." That is *expressio unius* and comparison to a related statute. *Olmstead* is a textbook example: the limited partnership statute said "exclusive," the LLC statute did not, so the court inferred the difference was deliberate.

## Florida specifics

- **Where to read it.** Online Sunshine (`leg.state.fl.us`) has the current Florida Statutes and prior years back to 1997. Always check the year at the top. The "history" note at the end of each section lists every session law that touched it, with the chapter number (e.g., "ch. 2023-15"). Clicking through to the session law shows the effective date.
- **Session laws versus the codified statute.** A bill passes as a "chapter law" (e.g., ch. 2025-123, Laws of Florida). The Division of Law Revision then codifies it into the Florida Statutes. Effective dates and transitional rules live in the session law.
- **Florida Administrative Code** rules implement statutes and have the force of law within their delegated authority. Agencies like DBPR and the Department of Revenue act mostly through rules.
- **Statutory interpretation decisions to know:** *Holly v. Auld*, 450 So. 2d 217 (Fla. 1984) (plain meaning); *Conage v. United States*, 346 So. 3d 594 (Fla. 2022) (text controls, no resort to extrinsic sources when text is clear); *Ham v. Portfolio Recovery Associates*, 308 So. 3d 942 (Fla. 2020) (reading a statute as a whole).
- **Attorney fee statutes are strictly construed** in Florida because they depart from the American rule that each side pays its own fees. Any statute or contract clause you plan to rely on for fees, read every word.

## Angles checklist

When you read a section:

- What year's text am I reading? What does the history note say changed recently?
- What does the definitions section say about each key word?
- Is this a default rule or a mandatory one? Can a contract change it?
- What are the elements, and are they joined by "and" or "or"?
- Is there an exception, and does it swallow my situation?
- Who has a remedy, against whom, and what can a court order?
- What was the Legislature reacting to? Is there a case this overruled?
- Is there a federal statute on the same subject that could preempt?

## Check yourself

1. A statute says "a contract, agreement, or other arrangement." A court must decide if a verbal understanding counts. Which canon helps and how?
2. § 95.11(2)(b) gives five years for an action on a written contract. § 672.725 gives four years for a contract for sale of goods. A written contract for the sale of goods is breached. Which applies?
3. The statute says a notice "must be sent by certified mail." The notice was hand-delivered and admittedly received. Is it valid?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. *Ejusdem generis* and *noscitur a sociis*. "Other arrangement" is read in light of "contract" and "agreement," so it likely covers understandings of the same kind, including oral ones if the surrounding words are not limited to writings. Check the definitions section first; the statute may define "agreement."
2. Four years. Specific over general: § 672.725 is the specific statute for sales of goods. Also the UCC chapter was enacted later than the general limitations statute in its relevant form. Courts have reached this result.
3. Depends on whether the court reads "must" as a condition or as directory. Florida courts generally treat "must" and "shall" as mandatory, but some hold that where the purpose of the requirement (actual notice) was fully achieved, substantial compliance suffices. The honest answer is "it depends on the statute and the case law under it," and you should assume the worst and use certified mail.

</details>
