# Walkovszky v. Carlton, 18 N.Y.2d 414 (New York Court of Appeals 1966)

**Tags:** `[US]` · limited liability · veil piercing · undercapitalization · enterprise liability
**Used in:** [02-business-entities/05-limited-liability-and-veil-piercing.md](../02-business-entities/05-limited-liability-and-veil-piercing.md), [02-business-entities/01-choosing-an-entity.md](../02-business-entities/01-choosing-an-entity.md)

## One-line takeaway

Splitting a business into many thinly capitalized corporations is not, by itself, a reason to hold the owner personally liable; the plaintiff must allege the owner was running the business personally through a dummy entity.

## Facts

John Walkovszky was hit by a taxicab in New York City. The cab was owned by Seon Cab Corporation. Carlton was a stockholder of Seon and of nine other corporations, each of which owned two cabs and carried the minimum liability insurance the law required, $10,000 per cab. The ten corporations were operated as a single fleet with common garaging, financing, and management. The complaint alleged that the fragmentation was a device to defraud the public by making sure no corporation had enough assets to pay a serious injury claim.

## Procedural posture

Walkovszky sued Seon, the driver, Carlton, and the other corporations. Carlton moved to dismiss the complaint against him personally for failure to state a cause of action. The trial court granted the motion. The Appellate Division reversed by a divided vote and held the complaint sufficient. Carlton appealed to the Court of Appeals.

## Issue

Does a complaint alleging that a stockholder organized his taxi business as many minimally capitalized and minimally insured corporations state a claim against the stockholder personally?

## Holding

No. The complaint was dismissed as to Carlton, with leave to replead. To reach the stockholder, the plaintiff must allege that the stockholder was conducting the business in his individual capacity and using the corporation as a dummy for his personal affairs, shuttling funds in and out without regard to formality. Allegations that the corporations were part of a larger enterprise may support liability of the other corporations in the group, but not of the individual.

## Reasoning

Judge Fuld started from the premise that the law permits incorporation for the very purpose of escaping personal liability. Courts will disregard the corporate form to prevent fraud or achieve equity, and they do so on agency principles: when an owner uses control of the corporation to further his own business rather than the corporation's, he is liable for the corporation's acts as a principal is for an agent.

The opinion drew a sharp line between two theories. Under the first, enterprise liability, the plaintiff shows that the corporation is a fragment of a larger corporate combine. That can make the other corporations liable, because the combine is really the operating business. Under the second, the plaintiff shows that the corporation is a dummy for an individual who is in fact carrying on the business personally. Only the second reaches the individual's assets. The complaint pleaded the first but not the second.

On undercapitalization, the court was unsympathetic. The legislature had set the minimum insurance. If the minimum was too low to protect the public, the remedy lay with the legislature, not in holding every owner of a minimally insured cab company personally liable. The court also found no allegation that Carlton or his corporations had actually been stripped of assets or that the insurance was not in place.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Judge Keating dissented. In his view the corporations were deliberately undercapitalized to avoid responsibility for obvious risks of the business. A shareholder who provides capital so thin that the corporation plainly cannot meet the liabilities its activities will foreseeably create should not be allowed to hide behind the form. He would have let the case proceed. His position has influenced courts that treat gross undercapitalization as a strong factor, even if few adopt it as sufficient on its own.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Limited liability is the reason most people form an LLC. This case shows the shield holds even against unattractive structuring, as long as the entity is really an entity. It also shows what breaks it: treating the company's bank account as your own, ignoring the line between the company's business and your personal business, and leaving no records of the difference. Separate accounts, documented contributions and distributions, contracts signed in the company's name, and adequate insurance are the practical answers.

The enterprise liability point also matters. If you run several LLCs as one business with shared cash and no accounting between them, a creditor of one may reach the others even if it cannot reach you.

## Florida connection

Florida reaches a similar result by a stricter route. The Florida Supreme Court held in *Dania Jai-Alai Palace, Inc. v. Sykes*, 450 So. 2d 1114 (Fla. 1984), that the veil cannot be pierced absent a showing of improper conduct. Florida DCA decisions state a three-part test: domination so complete that the entity has no independent existence and is the alter ego of the owner, use of the entity for a fraudulent or improper purpose, and injury caused by that improper use. For LLCs, § 605.0304(2), Fla. Stat., provides that failure to observe formalities relating to the exercise of powers or management is not a ground for imposing personal liability on members or managers. Florida has recognized that one entity can be liable as the alter ego of another, but again only with the improper conduct showing. Florida courts cite *Walkovszky* occasionally as persuasive authority on the distinction between enterprise liability and individual liability (verify).
