# Premises liability

**Module:** 04 · **Lesson:** 2 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

If customers, vendors, or the public set foot on space you own or control, you owe them a duty of care that depends on why they are there. Slip-and-falls, parking lot assaults, and injuries to a contractor's workers are the bread and butter of Florida tort practice. The rules here decide what you must inspect, what you must warn about, what you must fix, and when a crime committed by a stranger becomes your liability. Florida has three statutes on point and all three favor the property owner more than the common law did.

## The rule

A possessor of land owes a duty that varies with the status of the entrant. The plaintiff must prove status, the corresponding duty, breach, causation, and damages.

1. **Invitees.** People on the premises for the possessor's business purposes, or members of the public on land open to the public. The possessor owes two duties: to use reasonable care to maintain the premises in a reasonably safe condition, which includes a duty to inspect; and to warn of concealed dangers that the possessor knows or should know about and the invitee cannot discover with ordinary care.
2. **Licensees.** Florida splits this category. **Invited licensees**, such as social guests, get the same duty as invitees under *Wood v. Camp*, 284 So. 2d 691 (Fla. 1973). **Uninvited licensees**, who enter for their own convenience with the possessor's tolerance, are owed only a duty to refrain from wanton negligence or willful misconduct and to warn of known dangers not open to ordinary observation.
3. **Trespassers.** Section 768.075, Fla. Stat., limits liability. For an undiscovered trespasser, the possessor must only avoid intentional misconduct. For a discovered trespasser, the possessor must avoid gross negligence or intentional misconduct and warn of known dangers not readily observable. The statute removes liability to trespassers who were intoxicated or committing a felony (verify details). Child trespassers drawn by an attractive nuisance are a common law exception.

**Transitory foreign substances.** In a slip-and-fall on a substance in a business establishment, § 768.0755 requires the plaintiff to prove the business had actual or constructive knowledge of the condition and should have remedied it. Constructive knowledge is shown by circumstantial evidence that the condition existed long enough that the business should have known of it in the exercise of ordinary care, or that it occurred with regularity and was therefore foreseeable. This statute replaced § 768.0710 in 2010 and reversed *Owens v. Publix Super Markets, Inc.*, 802 So. 2d 315 (Fla. 2001), which had shifted the burden to the store.

**Open and obvious dangers.** A danger that is open and obvious discharges the duty to warn, because the warning would tell the entrant nothing. It does not automatically discharge the duty to maintain the premises in a reasonably safe condition, if the possessor should anticipate harm despite the obviousness, for example where entrants must encounter the condition to use the premises. Obviousness also feeds comparative fault.

**Negligent security.** A business owes invitees a duty to protect against criminal acts of third parties when such acts are reasonably foreseeable, typically shown by prior similar crimes on or near the premises. Since 2023, two statutes govern. Section 768.0701 requires the fact-finder to consider the fault of all persons who contributed to the injury, including the criminal, so fault can be apportioned to the attacker. Section 768.0706 gives owners and operators of multifamily residential property a presumption against liability if they implement listed security measures: lighting, cameras, deadbolts, locked gates, a crime prevention through environmental design assessment, and employee training (verify list and deadlines).

**Who is the possessor.** Duty follows control. A landlord who gives up control of leased space generally is not liable for conditions inside it, but remains responsible for common areas and for dangers it knew of at lease signing. A business that hires an independent contractor is generally not liable for the contractor's negligence, with exceptions for inherently dangerous work and retained control.

## How courts reason about it

Premises cases are about knowledge and foreseeability. Courts return to the same questions.

- **What did the owner know, and when?** The *McCain* zone of risk is easy to find once an owner knows of a hazard. The fight is over constructive knowledge: how long was the puddle there, how often does this happen, were there footprints or cart tracks through it, had the floor been inspected. Section 768.0755 makes this the plaintiff's burden. Without evidence of time or regularity, the case fails at summary judgment. Florida's 2021 adoption of the federal summary judgment standard made that outcome more common.
- **Status is less rigid than it looks.** The categories matter, but *Wood v. Camp* shows Florida courts collapsing them where the invitation was real. A delivery driver, a customer's companion, and a contractor's employee are generally invitees. Status can change: a customer who wanders into a stockroom marked "employees only" may become an uninvited licensee or trespasser.
- **Open and obvious cuts both ways.** Defendants argue it ends the case. Plaintiffs argue it only ends the warning claim and that the owner still had to fix a hazard it knew people would walk through. Courts generally agree with plaintiffs on the framework, then let comparative fault do the work.
- **Crime foreseeability.** Pre-2023 cases asked whether prior similar incidents put the owner on notice. Courts looked at the type, frequency, and proximity of prior crimes, not just crime in the general area. After § 768.0701, even where duty and breach are shown, the jury can assign most of the fault to the criminal, which slashes the owner's share since Florida has no joint and several liability.
- **Control over contractors.** Courts ask who directed the details of the work. An owner who merely hires and inspects is usually not liable for a contractor's employee's injury. An owner who runs the job, supplies the equipment, or knows of a hidden hazard on the site can be.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the common law status categories with these departures and additions:

- *Wood v. Camp*: invited licensees get invitee-level care.
- § 768.075: trespasser liability limits, including exclusions for intoxicated trespassers and those committing felonies (verify).
- § 768.0755: plaintiff's burden to prove knowledge in transitory substance cases.
- §§ 768.0701 and 768.0706: negligent security after HB 837, effective March 24, 2023 (verify).
- Comparative fault under § 768.81, with the greater-than-50% (verify) bar for claims accruing after March 24, 2023 (verify).
- Limitation period: 2 years (verify) for claims accruing after March 24, 2023. § 95.11.
- Florida courts treat an independent contractor's employees as invitees of the owner but apply the control test before imposing liability for the contractor's work methods.
- A business owes a duty to render reasonable aid to an invitee it knows is injured or ill on the premises.

## Angles checklist

- Who possessed and controlled the spot where the injury happened? Owner, tenant, contractor?
- Why was the plaintiff there? Invitee, invited licensee, uninvited licensee, trespasser?
- What was the hazard? Was it concealed or open and obvious?
- What did the possessor know? Inspection logs, prior incidents, video, employee statements.
- For a substance: how long was it there, and does it happen regularly? § 768.0755.
- For a crime: prior similar incidents, lighting, security measures, § 768.0706 compliance.
- What was the plaintiff's own fault? Footwear, distraction, warnings ignored?
- Who else can be apportioned fault: a cleaning contractor, the criminal, a tenant?
- Insurance: is there a commercial general liability policy, and has notice been given?
- Flip it: does the lease or contractor agreement shift liability by indemnity? Module 03, lesson 10.

## Drill

A customer in a St. Petersburg grocery store slips on a grape in the produce aisle and breaks her wrist. Store video shows the grape on the floor for about 90 seconds before the fall. The store's policy calls for produce aisle sweeps every 30 minutes; the last sweep was 25 minutes before. The customer was looking at her phone. The fall occurred in 2025. Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Status and duty.** The customer is a business invitee. The store owes a duty to maintain the premises in a reasonably safe condition and to warn of concealed dangers.
- **Section 768.0755.** The plaintiff must prove actual or constructive knowledge. No employee saw the grape, so no actual knowledge. Constructive knowledge by length of time: 90 seconds is very short. Courts have found periods of several minutes insufficient. This prong likely fails.
- **Regularity.** Grapes on a produce floor may be a condition that occurs with regularity and is therefore foreseeable. The store's own 30-minute sweep policy is evidence it knew produce falls regularly. This is the plaintiff's best argument. Compliance with the policy, with a sweep 25 minutes earlier, is the store's answer: it exercised ordinary care.
- **Open and obvious.** A single grape is not open and obvious. No help to the store.
- **Comparative fault.** The customer was on her phone. A jury could assign her significant fault. If more than 50%, she recovers nothing. § 768.81(6).
- **Likely outcome.** The store has a strong summary judgment argument on constructive knowledge under the time prong, and a reasonable jury argument on regularity given its compliance with its own policy. If the case survives, comparative fault reduces recovery.
- **Facts that would change it.** Video showing the grape there for 15 minutes, or a missed sweep, changes the knowledge analysis. Prior falls in the same aisle support regularity. A warning cone or mat shows reasonable care.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A tenant's customer is assaulted in a shopping center parking lot. Who is the possessor for negligent security purposes?
2. A plumber's employee falls through an unmarked, rotted section of a warehouse floor the owner knew about. Is the owner liable?
3. A customer trips over a bright yellow parking bumper in daylight. Does the open-and-obvious doctrine end the case?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Usually the landlord, because common areas such as parking lots remain under the landlord's control. The tenant may share a duty if it undertook security or if the lease allocates control. Fault can also be apportioned to the attacker under § 768.0701.
2. Likely yes. The employee is an invitee, the hazard was concealed, and the owner knew of it. The owner's duty to warn of known hidden dangers runs to a contractor's employees even though the owner does not control the plumbing work itself.
3. No. It likely eliminates the failure-to-warn claim, but the plaintiff can still argue the bumper was placed where pedestrians had to cross and was therefore an unreasonably dangerous condition despite its visibility. Expect a heavy comparative fault reduction.

</details>
