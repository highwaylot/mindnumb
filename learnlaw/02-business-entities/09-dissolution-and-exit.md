# Dissolution and exit

**Module:** 02 · **Lesson:** 9 · **Time:** ~55 min
**Tags:** `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Every LLC ends, or every member does. A member retires, dies, divorces, goes bankrupt, or simply wants out. Chapter 605's default answers surprise people: a member can leave any time but gets no buyout; one member cannot dissolve the company by wishing it; and a court can order dissolution, but the other members can buy the petitioner out instead. The operating agreement is where owners write a better exit than the statute gives them.

## The rule

Three separate concepts, in order.

**Dissociation** is one member leaving. **Dissolution** is the company beginning to end. **Winding up** is settling its affairs after dissolution. A member can dissociate without the company dissolving, and the company can dissolve without any member dissociating.

### Dissociation

A member has the power to dissociate at any time, rightfully or wrongfully, by giving notice of express will, § 605.0601(1), Fla. Stat. Dissociation is wrongful if it breaches an express provision of the operating agreement or, in a company for a definite term or undertaking, occurs before the term ends by the member's withdrawal, expulsion, bankruptcy, or similar event, § 605.0601(2) (verify). A wrongfully dissociating member is liable for damages caused.

Events of dissociation, § 605.0602, include notice of express will; an event the operating agreement specifies; expulsion under the agreement; expulsion by unanimous consent of the other members in listed cases, such as transfer of the entire transferable interest; expulsion by court order for wrongful conduct that materially harms the company, willful and persistent breach of the agreement or fiduciary duties, or conduct making it not reasonably practicable to carry on with the member, § 605.0602(6) (verify); death; and bankruptcy or incapacity in a member-managed company (verify).

Effect, § 605.0603: the member's right to participate in management ends; the duties of loyalty and care end except as to matters arising before dissociation; and the member's transferable interest is held as a transferee. **There is no statutory buyout.** The former member receives distributions when and if the company makes them, and the net share on dissolution.

### Dissolution

An LLC dissolves on the first of these, § 605.0701: an event or circumstance the operating agreement specifies; the consent of all members, or as the operating agreement provides (verify); the passage of 90 consecutive days with no members; a judicial decree under § 605.0702; or administrative dissolution under § 605.0714. A dissolved LLC continues its existence but may carry on only activities appropriate to winding up, § 605.0709.

**Judicial dissolution**, § 605.0702. On a member's application, a court may dissolve the LLC if: its activities are unlawful; it is not reasonably practicable to carry on in conformity with the articles and operating agreement; those in control have acted or will act illegally or fraudulently; assets are being misapplied or wasted; or the managers or members are deadlocked, the members cannot break it, and irreparable injury is threatened or the business cannot be conducted to the members' advantage (verify grounds). The agreement cannot take this power from the court, § 605.0105(3).

**Election to purchase in lieu of dissolution**, § 605.0706. When a member petitions on grounds other than unlawfulness, the company or other members may elect to buy the petitioner's entire interest at fair value. The election stops the dissolution; if the parties cannot agree on price, the court sets fair value and terms.

**Administrative dissolution**, § 605.0714, for failure to file the annual report by the third Friday in September (verify), failure to maintain a registered agent, or similar defaults. Reinstatement under § 605.0715 restores the company retroactively.

### Winding up

The company collects its assets, discharges or provides for its liabilities, and distributes what remains, § 605.0709. The order, § 605.0710: first creditors, including members who are creditors; then members and transferees for the return of unreturned contributions; then members and transferees in the proportions in which they share distributions (verify). The company files **articles of dissolution**, § 605.0707, with a $25 (verify) fee, and may give notice to known claimants, § 605.0711, and publish notice to unknown claimants, § 605.0712, after which untimely claims are barred (verify period). Claims against a dissolved LLC may be enforced against members to the extent of assets distributed to them (verify).

### Exit by agreement

The operating agreement can provide what the statute does not: buy-sell triggers such as death, disability, divorce, bankruptcy, termination of employment, and deadlock; a valuation method, whether formula, appraisal, annually updated price, or a shotgun clause where one side names a price and the other chooses to buy or sell; payment terms; put and call rights; tag-along and drag-along rights on a sale; and release and non-competition on exit within § 542.335.

## How courts reason about it

**Deadlock and "not reasonably practicable."** Courts read § 605.0702 narrowly, because dissolution ends a going business. A 50/50 LLC where the members disagree about strategy but the business still functions usually does not qualify. One where the members have stopped speaking, cannot sign checks, and are suing each other does. The statute asks whether the company can carry on in conformity with its agreement, not whether the members are happy. Facts that push toward dissolution: operational paralysis, exclusion of a member, self-dealing by the member in control. Facts that push away: a functioning business, an unused deadlock mechanism in the agreement, a petitioner who is simply unhappy with her deal.

**The election to purchase as the real remedy.** Few judicial dissolution cases end in dissolution. The petition is leverage: it forces the other members either to let the company be sold or to buy the petitioner out at fair value set by a court, usually the proportionate share of going-concern value, with Florida law on minority and marketability discounts unsettled (verify). This is why minority members with no buyout in their agreement still have an exit, and why majority members prefer to write a buyout on terms they control.

**Fiduciary duties on the way out.** *Meinhard v. Salmon* is about a venture nearing its end: Salmon took the renewal for himself as the term expired. Courts apply the same scrutiny when a member leaves. A departing member who diverts customers, copies files, or lines up the company's next contract for a new business before giving notice breaches duties that still exist until dissociation. *Guth v. Loft* supplies the opportunity test. After dissociation the duties end, but conduct before it is fully actionable, and the operating agreement can add post-exit restrictions.

**Transferee status.** Once dissociated, the former member is a transferee, and *Olmstead* and § 605.0502 define that status: distributions only. Courts hold the remaining members to good faith in deciding distributions, but they do not force a buyout the statute did not provide. The reasoning is contractual: the member could have bargained for a buyout and did not.

**Winding up disputes.** Courts enforce the waterfall strictly. A member who takes assets ahead of creditors must return them. Members who lent money are paid as creditors before anyone gets capital back, which surprises the member who contributed more equity.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the uniform act's structure of dissociation without buyout, which is a change from Florida's pre-2014 LLC statute, ch. 608, under which a withdrawing member in some cases could demand fair value (verify). Agreements drafted under the old law may assume a right that no longer exists.
- Partnerships differ: a dissociated partner gets a statutory buyout, § 620.8701. Lesson 8.
- The election to purchase under § 605.0706 mirrors § 607.1436 for corporations.
- A member's death produces a transferee estate. The personal representative may exercise the decedent's rights for purposes of settling the estate, § 605.0504 (verify).
- Reinstatement after administrative dissolution requires filing all delinquent reports and paying all fees and the $400 (verify) late penalty for each missed year.

## Angles checklist

- Has anyone given notice of withdrawal? Is the dissociation wrongful under the agreement?
- Does the operating agreement provide a buyout, a valuation method, and payment terms? Was the procedure followed?
- Is the departing member a transferee now, and what distributions are being made?
- Did the departing member take customers, files, or opportunities before leaving?
- Is there a ground for judicial dissolution, and would the other members elect to purchase?
- Has the company been administratively dissolved? Reinstated?
- If dissolution is under way, is the distribution order being followed? Are creditors paid first?
- Are there post-exit restrictive covenants, and are they enforceable under § 542.335?

## Drill

A Tallahassee architecture LLC has two 50% members, Nora and Paul, with a written operating agreement that is silent on exit and deadlock. Nora announces she is leaving in 60 days, tells three of the firm's largest clients she will take their projects to her new firm, and copies the project files. Paul changes the locks and stops paying Nora her monthly draw. Nora demands to be bought out for half the firm's value. List the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Dissociation.** Nora's notice is a dissociation by express will under § 605.0601, effective when given or on the date she states. It is not wrongful, since the agreement has no term and no restriction. It does not dissolve the LLC.
- **No buyout.** The agreement is silent and ch. 605 provides none. On dissociation Nora becomes a transferee entitled to her share of distributions if and when made. Her demand for half the firm's value has no statutory basis.
- **Nora's conduct before leaving.** Until dissociation she owes the duty of loyalty. Soliciting clients for a competing firm and copying files while still a member is competing with the company and appropriating its opportunities, § 605.04091(2). *Meinhard* and *Guth* support a claim for damages and an accounting. Trade secret and computer misuse claims may apply; module 06.
- **Paul's conduct.** Locking out a current member and cutting off an agreed draw before dissociation is effective may breach the agreement and the good faith obligation. Whether the draw was a distribution or compensation matters.
- **Judicial dissolution.** Either member could petition under § 605.0702. A 50/50 split with the members at war and operations disrupted is a plausible deadlock and "not reasonably practicable" case. Paul could then elect to purchase Nora's interest at fair value under § 605.0706, which gives Nora the exit she wants at a court-set price, likely reduced by damages for her solicitation.
- **Open questions.** What does the agreement say about draws? Were the clients under contract with the LLC? What did Nora copy, and was it confidential?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A member of a four-member LLC dies. What does the estate get?
2. Can an operating agreement provide that no member may ever seek judicial dissolution?
3. On winding up, who is paid first: a member who loaned the company $50,000, or a member who contributed $200,000 of capital?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The decedent's transferable interest, held as a transferee: distributions when made and the net share on dissolution. The estate does not become a member unless the operating agreement or the other members admit it. Death is a dissociation event under § 605.0602.
2. No. Section 605.0105(3) bars varying the court's power to dissolve under § 605.0702. The agreement can add deadlock procedures and a buy-sell that make a petition unnecessary, and can require those steps first, but cannot eliminate the court's power.
3. The lender. Under § 605.0710 creditors, including members who are creditors, are paid before any member receives a return of contributions.

</details>
