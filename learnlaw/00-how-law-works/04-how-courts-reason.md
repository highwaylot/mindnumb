# How courts reason

**Module:** 00 · **Lesson:** 4 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

"The law is just interpretation" is half right. Interpretation is disciplined by a set of moves courts make in the open, and those moves are predictable. If you can recognize them, you can predict how a court is likely to treat a situation, see where the real uncertainty is, and understand why two lawyers can look at the same facts and both be right that "it depends."

## The rule

### 1. Precedent and stare decisis

*Stare decisis* means "to stand by things decided." A court follows its own earlier decisions and those of courts above it. This gives the law stability and makes it possible to plan.

The move: identify the earlier case's **holding**, decide whether the present facts are the same in the ways that mattered, and then:

- **Follow** it if the material facts match.
- **Distinguish** it if a material fact differs. "In *Smith* the employee had no customer contact; here she managed the top ten accounts." Distinguishing is the everyday work of lawyers and judges. Almost every argument about a case is an argument about which facts were material.
- **Extend** it by analogy to a new situation the earlier court did not face. "The reasoning that protects a multi-member LLC's other members applies equally to a limited partnership."
- **Limit** it to its facts, which is a polite way of refusing to extend it.
- **Overrule** it. Rare. A court overrules its own precedent only when it concludes the earlier case was wrong and the costs of keeping it outweigh the stability interest. The Florida Supreme Court has been more willing to do this since 2019 and has said the test is whether the precedent is "clearly erroneous."

Lower courts cannot overrule higher courts. A trial judge who thinks a DCA decision is wrong must follow it anyway and may say so, which can prompt review.

### 2. Rules versus standards

A **rule** is bright-line: a non-compete longer than two years is presumptively unreasonable. A **standard** is a judgment call: a restriction must be "reasonably necessary to protect a legitimate business interest." Statutes and cases use both, often together.

Rules are predictable and cheap to apply but produce harsh results at the edges. Standards fit the facts but make outcomes hard to predict. When you see a standard, expect a multi-factor test and expect the outcome to turn on the facts the court finds persuasive. When you see a rule, expect litigation about whether the rule applies at all.

### 3. Elements and burdens

Most claims break into **elements**, each of which the party bringing the claim must prove. Negligence: duty, breach, causation, damages. Fraud: a false statement of material fact, knowledge of falsity, intent to induce reliance, justifiable reliance, damages. Miss one element and the claim fails regardless of the others.

**Burden of proof** says who must prove what and how convincingly.

- **Preponderance of the evidence**: more likely than not. The default in civil cases.
- **Clear and convincing evidence**: substantially more likely. Florida requires this for punitive damages, fraud in some contexts, and to pierce certain protections.
- **Beyond a reasonable doubt**: criminal cases only.

The party asserting an **affirmative defense** (statute of limitations, waiver, estoppel, release, accord and satisfaction) bears the burden on it. Burdens shift in some doctrines: once a plaintiff shows a prima facie case of discrimination, the employer must articulate a legitimate reason, then the plaintiff must show pretext.

### 4. Standards of review on appeal

An appellate court does not retry the case. It reviews the trial court's decision under a standard that depends on what kind of decision it was.

- **De novo** (fresh): questions of law, contract interpretation, statutory interpretation, summary judgment. The appellate court owes no deference.
- **Abuse of discretion**: discretionary rulings such as evidentiary decisions, injunctions, sanctions, continuances. Reversed only if no reasonable judge would have ruled that way.
- **Competent substantial evidence** or **clearly erroneous**: findings of fact after a bench trial. The appellate court will not reweigh the evidence.

This is why winning at trial matters so much on fact-heavy claims and why legal questions get settled on appeal. It is also why a trial court's "findings of fact" section is drafted so carefully.

### 5. Analogy and policy

When no precedent or statute controls, courts reason by **analogy** to the closest cases and by **policy**: which rule produces better results for the kind of situation at hand. Common policy themes in business cases:

- **Freedom of contract** versus **protection of the weaker party**. Courts enforce commercial bargains between sophisticated parties and scrutinize consumer and employment contracts.
- **Predictability** versus **fairness in the individual case**.
- **Encouraging investment** (limited liability, business judgment rule) versus **preventing abuse** (veil piercing, fiduciary duties).
- **Access to courts** versus **discouraging frivolous suits** (fee shifting, proposals for settlement).
- **Floodgates**: a rule that would generate unlimited claims gets resisted.

When a court names a policy, it is telling you how it will decide the next case that fits the policy.

### 6. Textualism, purposivism, and where Florida sits

For statutes and contracts, **textualism** reads the words as a reasonable reader would understand them and stops there. **Purposivism** asks what the drafters were trying to achieve and reads the words to serve it. The current Florida Supreme Court and the U.S. Supreme Court lean strongly textualist. Expect a Florida court to hold you to the words of your contract and the words of the statute, not to what everybody "knew" was meant.

### 7. Equity

Alongside the common law runs **equity**, the body of remedies and principles that courts apply when money damages are inadequate or strict law produces injustice: injunctions, specific performance, rescission, reformation, constructive trusts, estoppel, unclean hands, laches. Equitable remedies are discretionary. A court can refuse an injunction to a party that behaved badly even if it is technically in the right.

## How courts reason about it

Here is the shape of a typical business opinion, with the moves labeled:

> The question is whether the restrictive covenant is enforceable. [issue] Section 542.335 requires the party seeking enforcement to plead and prove a legitimate business interest justifying the restriction. [rule, with burden] The employer identifies two: customer goodwill and confidential pricing data. [application begins] In *Proudfoot*, the Eleventh Circuit found goodwill sufficient where the employee had direct, ongoing relationships with clients she later solicited. [precedent] Here the employee was an internal analyst with no client contact. [distinguishing fact] Goodwill therefore cannot support the restriction. [sub-holding] As to pricing data, the employer produced no evidence the data was secret rather than generally known. [burden not met] The statute instructs us to construe restrictive covenants in favor of reasonable protection of legitimate business interests and forbids us to consider hardship to the employee. [statutory command, limiting the judge's discretion] But the statute still requires an interest to protect, and there is none proven. [textual limit] Reversed. [disposition]

Every sentence is one of the moves above. When you can label them on sight, you are reading like a lawyer.

## Florida specifics

- **Overruling precedent.** The Florida Supreme Court in *State v. Poole*, 297 So. 3d 487 (Fla. 2020), set out its current approach: it will recede from precedent it finds clearly erroneous, giving less weight than before to reliance interests. Expect more change in Florida common law than in the past.
- **DCA conflict.** When two DCAs disagree, trial courts in each district follow their own DCA, and the Florida Supreme Court can resolve the conflict. Until it does, Florida law genuinely differs by region.
- **Jury versus judge.** Florida preserves the civil jury right (art. I, § 22). Most business disputes can go to a jury on damages claims; equitable claims are tried to the judge. Contracts often waive jury trial, and Florida enforces knowing waivers.
- **Punitive damages** require clear and convincing evidence of intentional misconduct or gross negligence and a pre-suit showing (§ 768.72). Capped at three times compensatory or $500,000 in most cases (§ 768.73).

## Angles checklist

When predicting how a court will treat your situation:

- What is the controlling rule, and is it a bright line or a standard?
- What are the elements, and which one is weakest for each side?
- Who bears the burden, and at what level?
- Which existing case is closest, and what fact distinguishes it?
- What policy would a court name, and which way does it cut?
- If this went to appeal, what standard of review would apply to the key ruling?
- Is there an equitable defense (unclean hands, laches, estoppel) lurking?

## Drill

Two Florida DCA opinions, both about whether a text-message exchange formed a binding contract for a $12,000 equipment sale. In one, the texts said "deal, I'll send the paperwork," and the court found a contract. In the other, the texts said "sounds good, let's finalize next week," and the court found none. A client has texts that say "ok let's do it, send me the invoice." Reason it through using the moves above.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Rule:** A contract requires mutual assent to definite terms, judged objectively. Under the UCC (goods over $500) there must also be a signed writing; texts can be a "record" and a typed name can be a signature (§ 668.50, the Florida Uniform Electronic Transaction Act; § 672.201).
- **Closest case:** "Deal, I'll send the paperwork" is closer. "Ok let's do it" expresses present assent. "Send me the invoice" is like "I'll send the paperwork": a reference to documenting an agreement already reached, not a condition to it.
- **Distinguishing the other case:** "Let's finalize next week" defers agreement to a future act. The client's text does not.
- **Weak element:** Definiteness. Were price, quantity, and item agreed earlier in the thread? If the invoice was to set the price, there may be no contract yet.
- **Burden:** The party claiming the contract must prove it by a preponderance.
- **Standard of review:** Whether the texts formed a contract is often a question of law on undisputed texts (de novo), but intent can be a jury question if the surrounding facts are disputed.
- **Prediction:** Likely a contract if the earlier texts fixed the terms; likely not if the invoice was where terms were to be set. The facts you want: the full thread, prior dealings, and whether the invoice matched prior discussion.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A trial judge believes a 2015 Fourth DCA decision is wrong. The case is in the Fourth District. What must the judge do?
2. A contract dispute was decided on summary judgment. On appeal, what standard of review applies?
3. Why do courts resist "floodgates" arguments only sometimes?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Follow it. The judge may write that the decision seems wrong and invite the DCA to reconsider, but must apply it.
2. De novo. Summary judgment turns on whether there is a genuine dispute of material fact and whether the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, both legal questions.
3. Because the floodgates concern is a policy argument, not a rule. It carries weight when the proposed liability has no natural stopping point (pure emotional distress to bystanders, pure economic loss to everyone affected by an accident). It carries little weight when the class of plaintiffs is bounded by the rule's elements.

</details>
