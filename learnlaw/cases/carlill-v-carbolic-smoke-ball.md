# Carlill v. Carbolic Smoke Ball Co., [1893] 1 QB 256 (Court of Appeal, England 1892)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · offer · unilateral contract · advertising · consideration
**Used in:** [03-contracts/01-formation.md](../03-contracts/01-formation.md), [03-contracts/02-consideration-and-promissory-estoppel.md](../03-contracts/02-consideration-and-promissory-estoppel.md), [07-regulatory-compliance/05-marketing-telemarketing-advertising.md](../07-regulatory-compliance/05-marketing-telemarketing-advertising.md)

## One-line takeaway

An advertisement that promises a specific reward for a specific act is an offer to the world, accepted by anyone who performs the act, and the advertiser cannot escape by calling it puffery.

## Facts

The Carbolic Smoke Ball Company sold a device that released carbolic acid vapor when squeezed. During the 1891 influenza epidemic it advertised in London newspapers that it would pay £100 to any person who contracted influenza after using the ball three times daily for two weeks according to the printed directions. The advertisement added that £1,000 had been deposited with the Alliance Bank "showing our sincerity in the matter." Mrs. Louisa Carlill read the advertisement, bought a ball, used it as directed from November 1891 until January 1892, and then caught influenza. She claimed the £100. The company refused.

## Procedural posture

Mrs. Carlill sued in the Queen's Bench Division. Hawkins J. gave judgment for her. The company appealed to the Court of Appeal, arguing that the advertisement was mere puff, that there was no offer to any particular person, that no acceptance had been communicated, that the promise was too vague, and that there was no consideration.

## Issue

Did an advertisement promising a reward to anyone who used the product as directed and still fell ill create a binding contract with a member of the public who did so?

## Holding

Yes. The advertisement was an offer to the public, accepted by performance of the conditions, supported by consideration, and the company was bound to pay. The appeal was dismissed.

## Reasoning

Lindley, Bowen, and A. L. Smith LJJ each wrote. Their reasoning covered five points that American courts still use.

Puffery. An advertisement can be mere sales talk, but the deposit of £1,000 as a show of sincerity made this one a serious promise. A reasonable reader would take it as a commitment.

Offer to the world. An offer can be made to the public at large. It ripens into a contract with each person who performs the condition. Reward cases had already established this.

Communication of acceptance. Ordinarily acceptance must be notified. But an offeror may dispense with notice, and an offer that calls for an act rather than a promise implies that performing the act is enough. Nobody expects a person who finds a lost dog to write to the owner before returning it.

Vagueness. The company argued the promise had no time limit. The court read it reasonably: a person who used the ball as directed and caught influenza within a reasonable time after use was covered. Mrs. Carlill was within any reasonable reading.

Consideration. Mrs. Carlill's inconvenience in using the ball as directed was a detriment to her, and the company benefited from the sales the advertisement generated. Either sufficed.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The company's strongest argument was that no sensible reader expects a newspaper advertisement to be a binding contract with every reader, and that holding otherwise exposes advertisers to unlimited claims. The court's answer was that the company had written the terms, had chosen the deposit to persuade readers, and could not complain when readers believed it.

American courts adopted the case because it fit the objective theory of assent that was already taking hold here and because it gave a clean rule for reward offers and unilateral contracts. The Restatement (Second) of Contracts §§ 29 and 54 restate its holdings on offers to the public and on dispensing with notice of acceptance. The limit it recognized is also alive. In *Leonard v. Pepsico*, 88 F. Supp. 2d 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1999), a court held a commercial showing a Harrier jet for seven million Pepsi Points was obvious puffery that no reasonable person would take as an offer. The distinction is the same one Bowen LJ drew: specific terms and signs of sincerity make an offer; jokes and exaggeration do not.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Your marketing can be a contract. A guarantee, a bounty, a "we will match any price," a "free if not delivered in thirty minutes," or a promised reward for a referral is an offer that customers accept by doing what you asked. If you do not intend to be bound, do not promise a specific result for a specific act, or state the conditions and limits clearly. The case also teaches the formation side: a unilateral offer is accepted by performance, so you may be bound before anyone tells you they have accepted.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the rule. Florida courts recognize offers to the public and unilateral contracts accepted by performance, and apply the objective test from *Lucy v. Zehmer* to decide whether a promotion was serious (verify a Florida citation on reward offers before relying on one). Beyond contract, Florida regulates the advertising itself. The Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act, § 501.204, Fla. Stat., makes deceptive acts in trade or commerce unlawful, and a promise the business does not intend to keep can be a deceptive practice as well as a breach of contract. The Federal Trade Commission Act, 15 U.S.C. § 45, covers the same ground federally. A promotion that is a contract and a deceptive practice at once exposes a business to both damages and statutory fee shifting.
