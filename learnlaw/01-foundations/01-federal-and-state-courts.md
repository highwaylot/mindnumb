# Federal and state courts

**Module:** 01 · **Lesson:** 1 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

When a dispute turns into a lawsuit, the first practical question is which courthouse. The answer decides which judges, which procedural rules, which appellate court, and how fast the case moves. A Florida business that sells across state lines can be pulled into federal court or another state's court. Knowing the map lets you read a summons and ask a lawyer the right first question: can we move this somewhere better.

## The rule

The United States runs two court systems side by side. Each is a pyramid.

**The federal system.** Article III of the U.S. Constitution creates the Supreme Court and lets Congress create lower courts. Federal courts are courts of **limited jurisdiction**: they hear a case only if the Constitution and a statute allow it. The main doors are federal question jurisdiction, 28 U.S.C. § 1331, and diversity jurisdiction, 28 U.S.C. § 1332. Lesson 2 covers both.

- **U.S. District Courts** are the trial courts. Florida has three: the Northern District (Tallahassee, Pensacola, Gainesville, Panama City), the Middle District (Jacksonville, Orlando, Tampa, Fort Myers, Ocala), and the Southern District (Miami, Fort Lauderdale, West Palm Beach, Fort Pierce, Key West). Magistrate judges handle discovery and pretrial matters.
- **U.S. Courts of Appeals.** Florida, Georgia, and Alabama make up the **Eleventh Circuit**, which sits in Atlanta. Its published opinions bind every federal court in those states.
- **The U.S. Supreme Court** hears a small number of cases a year, almost all by discretionary review called certiorari. It is the final word on federal law. It is not the final word on Florida law; that belongs to the Florida Supreme Court.

**The Florida system.** Article V of the Florida Constitution sets it up. Florida courts are courts of **general jurisdiction**: they hear anything not taken away from them, including most federal claims. Congress has made a few areas exclusively federal, such as patents, copyright, and bankruptcy.

- **County courts.** One in each of the 67 counties. Civil claims up to $50,000 (verify), exclusive of interest, costs, and attorney fees. Claims up to $8,000 (verify) run under the Florida Small Claims Rules, which are simpler and let a business appear through an officer or employee. The $50,000 ceiling took effect January 1, 2023 (verify). County courts also hear most evictions.
- **Circuit courts.** Twenty judicial circuits. Civil claims over $50,000, all equity cases such as injunctions, specific performance, and dissolution of an LLC, probate, family, and felonies. § 26.012, Fla. Stat. Several circuits run a complex business litigation division (verify which).
- **District Courts of Appeal.** Six districts since January 1, 2023 (verify), when the Legislature created the Sixth DCA and redrew the map. They hear appeals from circuit courts and now from county courts too, which used to go to the circuit court, a change effective January 1, 2021 (verify). A DCA decision binds every Florida trial court unless the trial court's own DCA has ruled the other way. *Pardo v. State*, 596 So. 2d 665 (Fla. 1992).
- **The Florida Supreme Court.** Seven justices. Mostly discretionary review: conflicts between DCAs, questions a DCA certifies as of great public importance, and a short mandatory list such as death sentences and bond validations. Most Florida civil cases end at the DCA.

## How courts reason about it

Two cases set the frame for how the two systems relate.

*Marbury v. Madison* established that courts decide what the law is, and that a court must refuse to apply a statute that conflicts with the Constitution. That power, judicial review, is why a Florida court can strike a Florida statute under the Florida Constitution, and why a federal court can refuse to apply a state law that conflicts with federal law. For a business owner the lesson is that "the statute says so" is the beginning of the analysis, not the end.

*Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins* decided what law a federal court applies when it hears a state-law case through diversity. The answer is state substantive law, including state court decisions, not a federal judge's own view of the common law. So a federal judge in Tampa hearing a contract dispute between a Florida LLC and a Georgia supplier applies Florida contract law as the Florida Supreme Court would, and predicts the Florida answer where it is unsettled. Federal procedure still governs how the case runs.

The contested edges show up as **strategy about forum**. Lawyers prefer one system or the other for reasons that have little to do with the merits:

- **Speed and management.** Federal courts in Florida impose strict scheduling orders. State courts have been catching up: Florida's 2025 (verify) civil rule amendments added mandatory case management and initial disclosures modeled on the federal rules.
- **Pleading standard.** Federal complaints must state a plausible claim under *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544 (2007). Florida requires fact pleading of ultimate facts. The two have converged, but a motion to dismiss in federal court is often a sharper tool.
- **Appeals.** A loser in federal court goes to the Eleventh Circuit; in state court, to a DCA. If a DCA has already decided your issue favorably, you want state court.
- **Removal.** A defendant sued in state court can often move the case to federal court if a federal court could have heard it from the start. The plaintiff's lawyer often drafts the complaint to prevent that, for example by keeping a Florida defendant in the case or pleading only state claims.

Courts also police their own authority. A federal court will dismiss a case on its own motion if it lacks subject-matter jurisdiction, even after trial.

## Florida specifics

- Thresholds and the DCA map: fact sheet, and §§ 34.01, 26.012, and 35.01 and following, Fla. Stat.
- A corporation or LLC must appear through a licensed attorney in circuit court and in county court outside small claims. An owner cannot represent the company.
- Florida's rules of procedure are written by the Florida Supreme Court, not the Legislature.
- Florida follows the majority rule that state courts hear federal claims unless Congress made jurisdiction exclusive. A Florida court can hear a Fair Labor Standards Act wage claim or a federal trademark claim.
- The civil jury right is in art. I, § 22 of the Florida Constitution. It attaches to claims for money damages. Equitable claims such as injunctions and dissolution are decided by the judge. Florida civil juries are six people.

## Angles checklist

When a dispute is heading toward a courthouse:

- Is there a federal claim in it? Is there complete diversity and more than $75,000 (verify) at stake? If either, federal court is an option for someone.
- Which Florida county and which DCA? Has that DCA decided the key issue?
- Is the amount under $50,000, so county court? Under $8,000, so small claims?
- Is any claim equitable, which belongs in circuit court regardless of amount?
- Does a contract pick a forum or require arbitration?
- Who chooses first? The plaintiff picks; the defendant may remove or move to transfer.
- Will the company need counsel from day one? Outside small claims, yes.

## Drill

A Tampa LLC supplies restaurant equipment. A Georgia restaurant group owes it $120,000 on a written supply agreement and has stopped paying, claiming the equipment was defective. The agreement is silent on forum. The LLC's two members live in Hillsborough County. List every court that could hear the LLC's claim, and the reasons each side might prefer one over another.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Florida circuit court, Hillsborough County.** Over $50,000, so circuit court. Whether a Florida court has personal jurisdiction over the Georgia group, and whether venue lies in Hillsborough, are lesson 2 questions. The LLC likes a home forum and the Second DCA's precedent, if favorable.
- **U.S. District Court, Middle District of Florida, Tampa Division.** The LLC's citizenship is that of its members, both Floridians. If every owner of the Georgia group is a Georgia citizen, there is complete diversity, and $120,000 exceeds the threshold. The LLC could file there, or the Georgia group could remove. The LLC might prefer federal court for its fixed schedule; the Georgia group might prefer it to avoid a hometown county jury.
- **Georgia state or federal court.** Suing the group where it lives avoids any fight over personal jurisdiction, at the cost of litigating away from home.
- **Which law applies.** Under *Erie*, a federal court applies the state law chosen by the forum state's choice-of-law rules. With no governing-law clause, that is a real question.
- **Open questions.** Who owns the Georgia group, and where do they live? Is there a forum clause in the invoice terms? Where were delivery and payment to occur?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A Florida business is sued in Orange County circuit court by a Florida customer on a state-law claim for $200,000. Can the business remove the case to federal court?
2. Which court is the final authority on what § 605.0503, Fla. Stat., means: the Eleventh Circuit or the Florida Supreme Court?
3. Why can a Florida county court hear a federal wage claim but not a copyright claim?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Both parties are Florida citizens, so there is no diversity, and the claim arises under state law, so there is no federal question. Federal court is closed to this case no matter how much is at stake.
2. The Florida Supreme Court. The Eleventh Circuit interprets Florida statutes when it must, but its reading is a prediction of what Florida's courts would say, and Florida courts are not bound by it.
3. State courts have general jurisdiction and hear federal claims unless Congress made federal jurisdiction exclusive. Congress did that for copyright, patents, and bankruptcy in 28 U.S.C. §§ 1338 and 1334, but not for the Fair Labor Standards Act.

</details>
