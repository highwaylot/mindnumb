# At-will employment

**Module:** 05 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

"At-will" is the rule most owners think they understand and most misuse. It means you can end employment for any reason or no reason. It does not mean you can end it for any reason you like. The exceptions are statutory, numerous, and each one comes with fee shifting. Nearly every wrongful termination case in Florida is really a claim under one of the exceptions, so knowing the list is knowing where the risk is.

## The rule

In the absence of a contract for a definite term, employment is terminable at the will of either party, at any time, with or without cause and with or without notice. Florida has followed this rule since the nineteenth century, and it is the majority American rule.

A plaintiff who wants to escape at-will must show one of three things.

1. **A contract.** An agreement for a definite term, or one that limits the grounds for termination, such as "for cause only." Florida construes indefinite promises against the employee: a promise of "permanent" or "lifetime" employment, without more, is still at-will. *Hartley v. Ocean Reef Club, Inc.*, 476 So. 2d 1327 (Fla. 3d DCA 1985) (verify citation).
2. **A statute.** A specific law that forbids termination for a specific reason. The main ones for a Florida private employer:
   - **Florida Private Whistleblower Act**, §§ 448.101 to 448.105, Fla. Stat. Applies to employers with 10 or more employees (verify). Forbids retaliation against an employee who (a) disclosed or threatened to disclose to a government agency a violation of a law, rule, or regulation, after first giving the employer written notice and a chance to fix it; (b) provided information to or testified before an agency investigating the employer; or (c) objected to or refused to participate in an activity, policy, or practice that violates a law, rule, or regulation. Suit must be brought within two years (verify); remedies include reinstatement, back pay, and fees.
   - **Workers' compensation retaliation**, § 440.205. No discharge, threat, intimidation, or coercion because an employee filed or attempted to file a valid workers' comp claim.
   - **Discrimination and retaliation statutes**: Title VII, the ADA, the ADEA, the Florida Civil Rights Act (ch. 760), the Pregnant Workers Fairness Act, and others covered in lesson 7.
   - **FLSA retaliation**, 29 U.S.C. § 215(a)(3): no discharge for complaining about wages, including an oral complaint; and the parallel Florida minimum wage protection in § 448.110(5).
   - **Others**: jury duty (§ 40.271), military service (USERRA and § 250.482), FMLA leave for employers with 50 or more employees, concerted activity under the National Labor Relations Act (which covers non-union workplaces), and voting (§ 104.081).
3. **A public-policy tort.** Most states let an employee sue in tort for discharge that violates a clear public policy, even without a statute. Florida does not. See the Florida specifics.

The employee bears the burden of proving the exception. The employer never has to prove good cause unless a contract requires it.

## How courts reason about it

At-will is a default rule, and courts police the ways plaintiffs try to contract or plead around it.

**Implied contracts and handbooks.** The question is whether the employer made a promise definite enough to be enforced. Florida courts have mostly said no to handbooks. A policy manual that lists progressive discipline is a statement of general practice, not a contract, especially when the handbook contains an at-will disclaimer and reserves the right to change policies. *McConnell v. Eastern Air Lines, Inc.*, 499 So. 2d 68 (Fla. 3d DCA 1986) (verify citation). A contract is more likely where the handbook uses mandatory language, was signed, and was treated as binding. The lesson for drafting is obvious: a clear at-will statement, signed, on the first page, with no promise of specific procedure.

**Promissory estoppel.** An employee who moved across the country on a promise of a job sometimes recovers reliance damages, but not the lost job. Florida courts will not let estoppel create a term the parties never agreed on.

**The whistleblower prongs.** The Private Whistleblower Act generates most of the litigation. Two edges are contested. First, does the employee have to prove an actual violation of law, or is a good-faith, reasonable belief enough? The Fourth DCA said belief suffices in *Aery v. Wallace Lincoln-Mercury, LLC*, 118 So. 3d 904 (Fla. 4th DCA 2013), and the Second DCA required an actual violation in *Kearns v. Farmer Acquisition Co.*, 157 So. 3d 458 (Fla. 2d DCA 2015) (verify both citations). Second, the written-notice requirement applies only to the "disclosed or threatened to disclose" prong, not to the "objected to or refused to participate" prong, so plaintiffs plead the objection prong whenever they can.

**Causation and timing.** Retaliation cases turn on whether the protected act caused the firing. Courts look at temporal proximity, the employer's documented reasons, consistency with how others were treated, and whether the decision-maker knew of the protected activity. A firing two weeks after a complaint, with no prior documentation, survives summary judgment. The same firing after a year of documented warnings usually does not.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority at-will rule and departs from the majority on the public-policy exception. In *DeMarco v. Publix Super Markets, Inc.*, 384 So. 2d 1253 (Fla. 1980), the Florida Supreme Court declined to create a tort of wrongful discharge in violation of public policy, holding that any such change was for the Legislature. The Legislature answered, in part, with the Private Whistleblower Act in 1991 and § 440.205. So in Florida the question is never "was this firing against public policy?" It is "which statute, if any, covers this reason?"

Other Florida points:

- **Employer size thresholds** differ by statute: 10 for the Private Whistleblower Act, 15 for the Florida Civil Rights Act and Title VII, 20 for the ADEA, 50 for the FMLA (verify each).
- **Written notice** under § 448.102(1) must give the employer a reasonable opportunity to correct the practice before the employee discloses to an agency.
- **Fees.** The Private Whistleblower Act allows the court to award fees to the prevailing party, which includes a prevailing employer in some circumstances (§ 448.104) (verify).
- **No Florida statute** requires a reason for termination, a notice period, or severance. The federal WARN Act requires 60 days' notice of mass layoffs for employers with 100 or more employees; Florida has no mini-WARN.

## Angles checklist

- Is there any writing about the term of employment? Offer letter, handbook, email, text?
- Does the handbook have an at-will disclaimer? Was it signed?
- What is the stated reason for termination, and what was documented before the protected activity?
- Did the employee complain about anything, to anyone, in the months before? Wages, safety, discrimination, a legal violation, a workers' comp injury?
- How many employees does the employer have? Which statutes apply?
- Timing between any protected activity and the adverse action.
- Flip it: the employer's documented legitimate reason; comparators treated the same; the decision-maker's knowledge.
- Remedies: back pay, front pay or reinstatement, fees. Limitation periods: two years for the Private Whistleblower Act (verify); charge deadlines for discrimination claims.

## Drill

Harbor Logistics, LLC has 14 employees. Dana, a dispatcher, tells the owner in a meeting that the company's drivers are regularly exceeding federal hours-of-service limits and that she will not schedule them that way anymore. Two weeks later the owner fires her, citing "attitude." The handbook says employees will receive a verbal warning, then a written warning, before termination. Dana never received either. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **At-will baseline.** No definite-term contract is mentioned. Dana is at-will unless an exception applies.
- **Handbook.** The progressive discipline policy is probably not a contract under Florida law, especially if there is an at-will disclaimer. Facts that change it: mandatory language, a signed acknowledgment, no disclaimer, consistent enforcement.
- **Private Whistleblower Act.** Harbor has 14 employees, above the 10-employee threshold. Dana objected to and refused to participate in a practice that violates federal hours-of-service regulations. That is the § 448.102(3) prong, which needs no written notice. Firing two weeks later for "attitude" with no prior discipline is strong circumstantial evidence of causation. In the Second DCA, Dana would need to show an actual violation; in the Fourth, a good-faith belief. Either way she has facts.
- **No public-policy tort.** Under *DeMarco*, Dana cannot sue for wrongful discharge in violation of public policy. She needs the statute.
- **Federal angle.** The federal Surface Transportation Assistance Act, 49 U.S.C. § 31105, protects employees who refuse to violate motor carrier safety rules, with an OSHA complaint process. Possible parallel claim.
- **Remedies.** Reinstatement or front pay, back pay, fees under § 448.104. Two-year limitation period (verify).

</details>

## Check yourself

1. An employer with eight employees fires a worker for reporting a safety violation to OSHA. Does the Florida Private Whistleblower Act apply?
2. An offer letter promises "a long and rewarding career with us." Is the employee still at-will?
3. Why does the written-notice requirement matter less than it looks?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No; the Act covers employers with 10 or more employees (verify). The worker may have a federal claim under the OSH Act's anti-retaliation provision, which has no size threshold but requires a complaint to OSHA within 30 days.
2. Yes. Florida treats indefinite assurances as at-will. Only a definite term or an express limit on grounds for termination changes the default.
3. Because it applies only to the "disclosed or threatened to disclose" prong of § 448.102. An employee who objected to or refused to participate in the practice need not have given written notice, and most complaints can be framed that way.

</details>
