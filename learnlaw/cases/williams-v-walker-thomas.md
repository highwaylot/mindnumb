# Williams v. Walker-Thomas Furniture Co., 350 F.2d 445 (D.C. Cir. 1965)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · unconscionability · defenses · form contracts
**Used in:** [`03-contracts/06-defenses.md`](../03-contracts/06-defenses.md), [`03-contracts/09-online-and-electronic-contracts.md`](../03-contracts/09-online-and-electronic-contracts.md), [`03-contracts/10-common-business-clauses.md`](../03-contracts/10-common-business-clauses.md)

## One-line takeaway

A court may refuse to enforce a contract or clause that was unconscionable when made, meaning one side had no meaningful choice and the terms were unreasonably favorable to the other.

## Facts

Walker-Thomas was a retail furniture store in Washington, D.C. that sold household goods on installment. Between 1957 and 1962 Ora Williams, a mother of seven living on a monthly public assistance check, bought several items on credit. Each purchase was on a printed form with a "pro rata" or "add-on" clause. Every payment she made was spread across every item she had ever bought, in proportion to the balances. The effect was that no item was ever paid off until every item was paid off, and the store kept a security interest in all of them.

In April 1962 she bought a stereo set for $514.95. At that point she owed $164 on about $1,800 of earlier purchases. The store knew her income. She defaulted soon after. Walker-Thomas sued to repossess everything she had bought since 1957.

## Procedural posture

The D.C. Court of General Sessions entered judgment for the store. The D.C. Court of Appeals affirmed. It said it was troubled by the clause and thought the contract looked like sharp practice, but found no authority letting a court refuse to enforce a contract on that ground. Williams appealed to the U.S. Court of Appeals for the D.C. Circuit, which was then the top appellate court for local D.C. matters.

## Issue

Can a court refuse to enforce a contract, or a clause in it, because it was unconscionable when made?

## Holding

Yes. Judge J. Skelly Wright, writing for the majority, held that where unconscionability is present at the time the contract is made, the contract should not be enforced. Unconscionability has two parts: an absence of meaningful choice on the part of one party, together with contract terms unreasonably favorable to the other. The court remanded for findings on whether the add-on clause met that test.

## Reasoning

The court started with UCC § 2-302, which Congress had enacted for D.C. after these contracts were signed. The section did not govern, but the court read it as a statement of what the common law already allowed. Courts of equity had long refused to enforce bargains that no honest person would make and no fair person would accept.

The majority then gave the test content. Meaningful choice turns on the circumstances of the transaction: gross inequality of bargaining power, whether the weaker party had a reasonable opportunity to understand the terms, whether the terms were hidden in fine print or obscured by sales tactics. The court said a party who signs a contract normally is bound even without reading it, but that rule assumes a real choice. When there is little bargaining power and little real choice, consent is not meaningful.

The second part asks whether the terms are so one-sided as to be unconscionable under the circumstances existing when the contract was made. The test is not whether the terms look harsh in hindsight. It is whether they fall outside the commercial reasonableness of the trade.

The court stressed that the trial court had made no findings, so it sent the case back. It did not hold the clause void.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Judge Danaher dissented. He pointed out that Congress had just addressed the subject by statute and that courts should leave such policy questions to legislators. He also noted the business reality that stores selling on credit to low-income buyers take real risks, and that a rule making their contracts unenforceable might cut off credit to the very people the majority meant to protect.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

This case is the reason a one-sided form contract is not automatically enforceable. If your terms are drafted to be as favorable as possible, ask two questions. Did the other side have a real chance to see and understand the term, or was it buried? Is the term so lopsided that a judge would wince? A clause that fails both is at risk. Terms that most often get attacked are arbitration clauses with hidden costs, waivers of all remedies, fee-shifting in only one direction, and cross-collateral clauses like the one here. The fix is usually procedural: put the term where it is seen, use plain words, and give the other side a chance to decline.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the majority rule. Section 672.302, Fla. Stat. is Florida's enactment of UCC § 2-302 for sales of goods, and Florida courts apply the same defense to contracts generally. Florida requires both procedural and substantive unconscionability, but uses a sliding scale: a strong showing on one lowers what is needed on the other. *Basulto v. Hialeah Automotive*, 141 So. 3d 1145 (Fla. 2014). Procedural unconscionability looks at how the contract was made, including bargaining power, the chance to read and understand, and whether the term was offered take-it-or-leave-it. Substantive unconscionability looks at whether the terms are outrageous or oppressive. The defense is raised most often against arbitration clauses, and Florida courts enforce most of them while striking individual provisions that strip remedies. Unconscionability is an affirmative defense and the party resisting the contract carries the burden.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Does the outcome change? Suppose Williams had been a business buying office furniture on the same add-on terms. Would the court have reached the same result?
2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What facts could Walker-Thomas offer on remand to defend the clause as commercially reasonable?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? Think about a subscription agreement with an automatic renewal, a one-way fee-shifting clause, and a class waiver. Which would a Florida court look at first, and why?
