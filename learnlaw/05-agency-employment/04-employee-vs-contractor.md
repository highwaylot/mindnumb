# Employee vs contractor

**Module:** 05 · **Lesson:** 4 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Calling a worker a "1099 contractor" changes nothing if the law says the worker is an employee. The label decides who pays employment taxes, whether overtime is owed, whether workers' compensation and reemployment tax apply, whether the business answers for the worker's torts, and whether the worker can bring discrimination claims. Misclassification is the most expensive routine mistake a small business makes, because every agency applies its own test and bills separately.

## The rule

There is no single test. Four matter to a Florida business.

**1. The common-law right-to-control test (torts, agency, Florida reemployment tax, most Florida purposes).** The question is whether the hirer has the right to control not just the result but the manner and means of the work. In *Cantor v. Cochran*, 184 So. 2d 173 (Fla. 1966), the Florida Supreme Court adopted the factors of Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220: control over details; whether the worker runs a distinct business; whether the work is usually supervised; the skill required; who supplies tools and the place of work; length of the relationship; payment by time or by job; whether the work is part of the hirer's regular business; and what the parties believed. Control is the most important factor, and the right to control counts even if it is not exercised.

**2. The IRS common-law test (federal employment taxes).** The same idea, organized into three groups: behavioral control (instructions, training, evaluation), financial control (investment, unreimbursed expenses, opportunity for profit or loss, services offered to the market, method of payment), and the relationship of the parties (written contracts, benefits, permanency, whether the services are a key aspect of the business).

**3. The FLSA economic reality test (federal minimum wage and overtime).** Whether the worker is economically dependent on the hirer or in business for themselves. The Eleventh Circuit weighs: the nature and degree of control; the worker's opportunity for profit or loss depending on managerial skill; the worker's investment in equipment or materials; whether the service requires special skill; the permanency of the relationship; and whether the service is an integral part of the hirer's business. *Scantland v. Jeffry Knight, Inc.*, 721 F.3d 1308 (11th Cir. 2013) (verify citation). The Department of Labor's 2024 rule restated this test; in 2025 the Department said it would not apply the rule in its own enforcement while it reconsiders, and Eleventh Circuit precedent continues to govern in court (verify).

**4. The Florida workers' compensation definition, § 440.02(15)(d), Fla. Stat.** Outside the construction industry, a worker is an independent contractor if at least four of the listed criteria are met: maintains a separate business with its own work facility, truck, equipment, or materials; holds or has applied for a federal employer identification number, unless a sole proprietor not required to have one; receives compensation for services on a competitive-bid basis or completion of a task, as defined by contract; may realize a profit or suffer a loss; has continuing or recurring business liabilities; and the success or failure of the business depends on receipts versus expenditures (verify the list). If fewer than four are met, contractor status can still be shown on the usual control factors. In the **construction industry**, an "independent contractor" is treated as an employee for workers' comp purposes unless exempt (§ 440.02(15)(c)3) (verify).

The party asserting contractor status usually bears the burden, and for the FLSA, exemptions and exclusions are the employer's to prove.

## How courts reason about it

Every test is a list of factors with no arithmetic, so the reasoning is about weight and about what the factors are proxies for.

**The written contract is weak evidence.** Courts say it repeatedly: the parties' label is one factor and not a strong one, because the statutes protect workers who did not bargain for the label. A contract that calls the worker a contractor but then dictates hours, scripts, uniforms, and exclusivity is evidence of control, not of contractor status.

**Control of the result versus control of the means.** A client who specifies what it wants, by when, at what price, is controlling the result. A hirer who specifies how, where, in what order, with daily check-ins and the right to discipline, is controlling the means.

**In business for themselves.** The FLSA test asks a different question than the control test. A plumber with her own truck, insurance, and a dozen clients is in business for herself. A driver with no investment, one platform, and hourly pay is economically dependent even if nobody watches him. Courts ask about profit-and-loss opportunity that depends on skill and initiative rather than on working more hours.

**Integral to the business.** If the work is what the business sells, courts lean toward employee: a cleaning company's cleaners, a trucking company's drivers. If the work is peripheral, such as the consultant who maintains the website, a contractor finding is easier.

Some states adopted an "ABC test" that presumes employee status unless the hirer proves the worker is free from control, outside the usual course of the business, and customarily engaged in an independent trade. Florida has not.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority common-law control test from *Cantor* for torts, agency, and reemployment tax (§ 443.1216 uses the common-law rules). Florida has no ABC test. It departs in having a detailed statutory definition for workers' compensation, above, and in treating construction-industry "contractors" as employees.

Consequences of misclassification in Florida include:

- **Back wages.** Minimum wage and overtime under the FLSA for two years, three if willful, doubled as liquidated damages, plus fees. Florida minimum wage claims under § 448.110 run four years, five if willful (verify).
- **Federal employment taxes.** The employer's share of FICA, plus the employee's share and income tax withholding that was never taken, with reduced rates under 26 U.S.C. § 3509 if the failure was not intentional, and "Section 530" relief where the business had a reasonable basis and consistent treatment.
- **Workers' compensation.** Failure to secure coverage can bring a stop-work order and a penalty of twice the premium the employer would have paid over the preceding two years, with a minimum of $1,000 (§ 440.107) (verify). An injured misclassified worker can also sue in tort because the employer loses the exclusivity shield.
- **Reemployment tax.** Back tax on the first $7,000 of each worker's wages (verify), with interest and penalties, after a Department of Revenue audit, which is often triggered by a former contractor filing for reemployment benefits.
- **Everything else that depends on employee status:** discrimination statutes, FMLA, benefits, and respondeat superior.

## Angles checklist

- Who sets the hours, the place, the method, and the sequence of the work?
- Can the worker send a substitute or hire helpers? Does the worker have other clients?
- Who owns the tools, the vehicle, the software, the inventory?
- Is the worker paid by the hour, by the job, or by commission? Who bears the cost of mistakes?
- Is the work what the business sells, or support for it?
- Is the worker in the construction industry?
- Which agency is asking: IRS, DOL, Department of Revenue, Division of Workers' Compensation, a court?
- Exposure: back wages and liquidated damages, taxes and penalties, workers' comp penalties, tort liability, fee shifting.

## Drill

Palm Coast Cleaning, LLC has 12 "independent cleaners." Each signed a contractor agreement, is paid $18 per hour, wears a company shirt, uses company supplies, works a schedule set weekly by the office, and is told which homes to clean and in what order. Several work 50 hours a week. One is hurt lifting a sofa and files a workers' comp claim; the carrier says there is no policy because the company reported only two office employees. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Control test.** The office sets the schedule, the sequence, the supplies, and the uniform. Payment is hourly. The work is the company's core service. Every factor points to employee under *Cantor*.
- **Workers' comp.** Under § 440.02(15)(d), the cleaners meet almost none of the contractor criteria: no separate business, no FEIN, no profit-or-loss opportunity, hourly pay. They are employees. With 12 cleaners plus two office staff, the company is far above the four-employee threshold for non-construction coverage. The injured cleaner's claim is compensable; the company is uninsured and faces a stop-work order and a penalty of twice two years' premium (verify). Because it failed to secure coverage, the cleaner may also sue in tort.
- **FLSA overtime.** Under the economic reality test the cleaners are economically dependent. Those working 50 hours are owed time-and-a-half for 10 hours per week, for two or three years back, doubled, with fees.
- **Taxes.** Unpaid FICA and withholding; reemployment tax; new-hire reporting failures.
- **Likely outcome.** Reclassification across the board. Facts that would change it: cleaners who own their supplies, choose their jobs, serve other clients, and bid per job.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A worker signs an agreement acknowledging contractor status and asks to be paid on a 1099. Does that settle the question?
2. Why can the same worker be an employee for one purpose and a contractor for another?
3. A drywall installer with his own tools and truck works only for one general contractor. For Florida workers' comp, is he a contractor?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. The parties' agreement is one factor and a weak one. Agencies and courts look at the reality of control and economic dependence, because the statutes protect workers regardless of what they agreed to.
2. Because different statutes use different tests. The IRS and tort law use control; the FLSA uses economic reality; Florida workers' comp has its own statutory list. A worker can satisfy one and fail another.
3. Probably not. In the construction industry, § 440.02(15)(c)3 treats an independent contractor as an employee for workers' comp unless an exemption is held (verify). The general contractor must cover him or require proof of his own coverage or exemption.

</details>
