# IP ownership: work for hire and contractors

**Module:** 06 · **Lesson:** 6 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

You paid a freelancer $3,000 for your logo. You paid a developer $40,000 for your app. You wrote the first version of your product before you formed the LLC. In each case, the business may not own what it thinks it owns. Copyright and patent law vest ownership in the creator, and paying for the work does not move it. The fix is a signed assignment. Its absence surfaces at the worst moments: a sale, an investment, or a dispute with the contractor who built the thing.

## The rule

**Copyright vests in the author** (17 U.S.C. § 201(a)). The author is the person who created the work, with one exception.

**Work made for hire** (§ 101). The employer or commissioning party is the author, and owns the copyright from the start, in two situations only:

1. A work prepared by an **employee within the scope of employment**. No writing is needed. The employer owns it automatically.
2. A work **specially ordered or commissioned** from a non-employee, but only if (a) the parties signed a written agreement saying the work is made for hire, **and** (b) the work falls into one of nine statutory categories: a contribution to a collective work, part of a motion picture or other audiovisual work, a translation, a supplementary work, a compilation, an instructional text, a test, answer material for a test, or an atlas.

Software, logos, photographs, website copy, and most marketing content are **not** in the nine categories. A contractor agreement that calls them "work made for hire" does not make them so. The contractor owns them.

**Who is an "employee."** *Community for Creative Non-Violence v. Reid*, 490 U.S. 730 (1989), held that "employee" in § 101 means an employee under the general common law of agency, not merely someone the hiring party controls or someone who gets a W-2. The Court listed factors: control over manner and means; skill required; source of tools; location; duration; whether the hiring party can assign more projects; discretion over hours; method of payment; role in hiring assistants; whether the work is part of the hiring party's regular business; benefits; and tax treatment. No factor is decisive. A sculptor commissioned for one piece, working in his own studio with his own tools for a lump sum, was a contractor and owned the sculpture's copyright.

**Scope of employment.** Even for a true employee, only work within the scope is for hire: work of the kind the employee was hired to do, substantially within authorized time and space, to serve the employer. A bookkeeper who writes a novel at night owns the novel.

**Transfers must be in writing** (§ 204(a)). An assignment of copyright is valid only in a writing signed by the owner. An oral deal or an invoice is at best evidence of a nonexclusive license, which can be oral or implied.

**Implied license.** When a contractor creates a work at the client's request, is paid, and delivers it intending the client to use it, courts find an implied nonexclusive license for the intended use. The client may use the logo. It may not stop the contractor from reusing elements, register the copyright, or sell it to a buyer as an owned asset.

**Patents.** The inventor owns the invention (35 U.S.C. § 261 requires assignments to be in writing). An employer owns an employee's invention only if the employee was specifically hired to invent it or signed an assignment. Otherwise the employer gets at most a shop right to use the invention in its own business. Present-tense assignment language matters: "hereby assigns" transfers future inventions automatically when they arise, while "agrees to assign" creates only a promise, *Board of Trustees of Leland Stanford Junior University v. Roche Molecular Systems, Inc.*, 563 U.S. 776 (2011).

**Trademarks.** Owned by whoever controls the quality of the goods sold under the mark, which is the business, not the designer. But the logo as artwork has a separate copyright, which the designer owns absent assignment.

## How courts reason about it

**Reid factors and the W-2.** Courts lean heavily on benefits and tax treatment: a worker on payroll is almost always an employee; a worker paid by invoice almost always is not. A 1099 worker's output is not a work for hire unless it is in the nine categories and there is a signed writing.

**"Work for hire" language in contractor agreements.** Courts enforce the statute, not the label. If a for-hire clause is the only ownership language, the contractor owns the logo. Well-drafted agreements add "to the extent the work is not a work made for hire, Contractor hereby assigns all right, title, and interest." The backup assignment is what works.

**Scope of the implied license.** Courts ask what the parties contemplated when the work was made and paid for. A website built for one business carries a license for that business's site. Courts have refused to extend implied licenses to new products, to resale, or to use when the contractor was not paid in full, which is why unpaid contractors can demand that the site come down.

**Founders.** Work created before the entity existed belongs to the founder personally. Forming the LLC does not transfer it. Departing founders have walked away with the code or the brand because nothing was ever signed. Investors and acquirers check for this first.

**Joint works.** When two people contribute copyrightable expression intending to merge it into one work, each co-owns the whole and may license it without the other's consent, subject only to an accounting (§ 201(a)). This is how a contractor who "helped" ends up able to license the whole product.

## Florida specifics

Copyright and patent ownership are federal. Florida law governs the contract side: consideration for an assignment, enforceability of an NDA, and how an operating agreement handles contributed IP.

- **Invention assignment agreements.** Florida has no statute limiting employee invention assignments to work-related inventions, unlike California and several other states (verify). Ordinary contract defenses and § 542.335 still apply if the clause restrains competition.
- **Contributions to the LLC.** A member's contribution can be property, including IP (§ 605.0402) (verify). A founder's IP should be contributed by a written assignment listed in the operating agreement.
- **Right of publicity.** § 540.08 requires written consent to use a person's name or likeness commercially, which is why model releases matter for photos of employees and customers.

## Angles checklist

- Who created each asset: employee, contractor, founder, co-founder, agency? On payroll or by invoice?
- For contractors: is there a signed writing? Does it say "hereby assigns," or only "work made for hire"? Is the work in a statutory category?
- Was the contractor paid in full? Any open-source components, and under what license?
- For founders: was anything created before formation, and was it assigned to the entity in writing?
- Flip it: implied license as the business's fallback; joint authorship as the contractor's leverage; unpaid invoices as grounds to revoke the license.
- Remedies: declaratory judgment of ownership; a corrective assignment, which most contractors will sign for a modest fee if asked early.

## Drill

Three founders start a Miami software LLC. Before formation, Lena wrote the core engine. After formation, the LLC hires Raj as a W-2 developer and pays Studio K, an outside design firm, $8,000 for the logo and UI under an invoice that says "all work is work made for hire." A college friend, Dev, "helped with the recommendation algorithm" for free over a summer. The LLC now has a term sheet, and the investor's diligence list asks for the chain of title. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Lena's engine.** Created before the LLC existed, so Lena owns it personally. Needed: a written assignment from Lena to the LLC, ideally recited as a capital contribution in the operating agreement.
- **Raj's code.** W-2 employee writing software within the scope of employment. Work made for hire; the LLC owns it. Confirm his duties cover development and get an employment agreement with IP terms for the margins.
- **Studio K's logo and UI.** The invoice's "work made for hire" language is ineffective: a logo and UI designs are not in the nine categories. Studio K owns the copyright; the LLC has an implied license to use them. The LLC owns the trademark in the logo as used but not the copyright. Needed: a signed assignment from Studio K. Expect to pay something for it.
- **Dev's algorithm.** If Dev contributed copyrightable code intending it to merge with the product, he may be a joint author who co-owns the whole and can license it to anyone. Ideas alone give him nothing, but proving that is hard. Needed: an assignment or release from Dev.
- **Diligence outcome.** Without the Lena, Studio K, and Dev documents, the chain of title is broken; expect the investor to condition closing on fixing it.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A contractor agreement says the deliverable "shall be a work made for hire." The deliverable is a mobile app. Who owns the code?
2. An employee on salary writes code for the company's product. No agreement was ever signed. Who owns it?
3. Why does a business want both an assignment of the logo's copyright and ownership of the logo as a trademark?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The contractor, with an implied license to the client. Software is not one of the nine categories, so the for-hire label fails and there is no assignment language to fall back on.
2. The company, as a work made for hire, if the coding was within the scope of the job. No writing is required for the employee prong.
3. Because they are different rights with different owners by default. The trademark belongs to the business that uses the logo to identify its goods. The copyright in the artwork belongs to the designer until assigned. Without the assignment, the business cannot register the copyright, stop the designer from reusing the art, or deliver clean title to a buyer.

</details>
