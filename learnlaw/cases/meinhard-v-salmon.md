# Meinhard v. Salmon, 249 N.Y. 458 (New York Court of Appeals 1928)

**Tags:** `[US]` · fiduciary duty · duty of loyalty · joint venture · partnership · business opportunity
**Used in:** [02-business-entities/04-fiduciary-duties-and-business-judgment.md](../02-business-entities/04-fiduciary-duties-and-business-judgment.md), [02-business-entities/08-partnerships-and-the-accidental-partnership.md](../02-business-entities/08-partnerships-and-the-accidental-partnership.md), [02-business-entities/03-operating-agreements.md](../02-business-entities/03-operating-agreements.md)

## One-line takeaway

Co-venturers owe each other the highest loyalty, so a managing partner who receives a business opportunity because of the venture must disclose it and share it rather than take it for himself.

## Facts

In 1902 Walter Salmon leased the Hotel Bristol on Fifth Avenue in Manhattan from Louisa Gerry for twenty years, intending to convert it into shops and offices. He needed money. Morton Meinhard agreed to pay half the cost of reconstruction and operation. In return Meinhard would receive 40 percent of the net profits for the first five years and 50 percent after that. Losses were shared equally. Salmon alone managed the property. The arrangement was a joint venture, not a formal partnership.

In 1922, with less than four months left on the lease, Elbridge Gerry, who had acquired the reversion and owned the adjacent lots, approached Salmon with a larger plan. He offered a lease of the whole block, including the Bristol site, for up to eighty years, on condition that the lessee demolish the buildings and put up a new one. Salmon took the lease in the name of his own company, Midpoint Realty. He did not tell Meinhard. Meinhard learned of the new lease after it was signed and demanded a share.

## Procedural posture

Meinhard sued for a declaration that the new lease was held in trust for the joint venture. The referee found for Meinhard and awarded him a 25 percent interest. The Appellate Division increased it to 50 percent. Salmon appealed to the Court of Appeals.

## Issue

Did a managing co-venturer who was offered a new lease of the venture's property, during the term of the venture and because of his position in it, breach a fiduciary duty by taking the lease for himself without disclosing it to his co-venturer?

## Holding

Yes. The new lease was held in trust for the venture. Meinhard was entitled to half of it, with Salmon receiving one additional share so that he kept control. The judgment was modified accordingly and affirmed.

## Reasoning

Chief Judge Cardozo's opinion is the most quoted statement of fiduciary duty in American law. "Joint adventurers, like copartners, owe to one another, while the enterprise continues, the duty of the finest loyalty. Many forms of conduct permissible in a workaday world for those acting at arm's length, are forbidden to those bound by fiduciary ties. A trustee is held to something stricter than the morals of the market place. Not honesty alone, but the punctilio of an honor the most sensitive, is then the standard of behavior."

The facts that drove the result were these. The opportunity came to Salmon because he was the lessee in possession, which was a position he held for the venture. It arose during the term of the venture, which had not ended. Salmon was the managing partner, with exclusive control, which heightened his duty. And he said nothing. Had he disclosed the offer, Meinhard could have competed for the new lease or negotiated a share. Secrecy was the breach. Cardozo did not say Salmon was dishonest; he said the rule must be strict because loosening it invites abuse.

The remedy was tailored. Meinhard got an interest equal to Salmon's, but Salmon kept a one-share edge so that the person who had managed the enterprise for twenty years retained control.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Judge Andrews dissented, joined by two judges. He read the joint venture narrowly. It covered the 1902 lease and nothing more. When that lease expired, the venture ended. The new lease covered a different and much larger tract, a different building, and a term far beyond what the parties contemplated. Meinhard had no expectancy in it. Andrews would have treated Salmon as free to deal for himself once the venture's subject matter ran out. The dissent is the standard argument for defining the scope of a venture tightly in writing.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Two people who go into business together without a written agreement are, in Florida, probably partners or joint venturers, and they owe each other fiduciary duties whether they know it or not. This case shows what that means. If one of you is approached with a deal that relates to the business, you tell the other. The safe practice is a written operating or partnership agreement that defines the scope of the business, says what opportunities belong to it, and provides a procedure for presenting and waiving opportunities. Florida lets you narrow these duties by agreement within limits, but you must actually write it down.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the majority rule. Partners owe duties of loyalty and care under § 620.8404, Fla. Stat., and joint venturers are treated like partners. The Florida Supreme Court has long recognized joint ventures and the fiduciary duties within them; see *Kislak v. Kreedian*, 95 So. 2d 510 (Fla. 1957), on the elements of a joint venture (verify citation). For LLCs, § 605.04091, Fla. Stat., sets out the duty of loyalty, which includes accounting for any benefit derived from the appropriation of a company opportunity. Section 605.0105 lets the operating agreement identify specific activities that do not violate the duty of loyalty if not manifestly unreasonable, but it cannot eliminate the duty entirely. Florida courts cite *Meinhard* for the "punctilio" standard (verify).
