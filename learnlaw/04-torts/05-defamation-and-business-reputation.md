# Defamation and business reputation

**Module:** 04 · **Lesson:** 5 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

A one-star review says your company "stole from us." A competitor tells your customers you are about to file bankruptcy. You want to sue. The other side of the coin: your reference for a former employee or your reply to a review can get you sued. Defamation is where the First Amendment, Florida statute, and the common law of reputation meet, and a business has to know how to use it and how to avoid it.

## The rule

**Defamation** is a false statement of fact about the plaintiff, published to a third party, with the required degree of fault, that causes damage. Florida states the elements in *Jews for Jesus, Inc. v. Rapp*, 997 So. 2d 1098 (Fla. 2008): (1) publication; (2) falsity; (3) the actor acted with knowledge or reckless disregard of falsity on a matter concerning a public official or public figure, or at least negligently on a matter concerning a private person; (4) actual damages; and (5) the statement is defamatory. Written defamation is libel; spoken is slander.

**Fault.** Under *New York Times Co. v. Sullivan*, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), a public official, and under later cases a public figure, must prove **actual malice**: knowledge of falsity or reckless disregard for the truth, by clear and convincing evidence. A private plaintiff need prove only negligence to recover actual damages. *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323 (1974). A business can be a limited-purpose public figure if it has injected itself into a public controversy.

**Defamation per se.** Statements that impute a crime, a loathsome disease, conduct incompatible with the plaintiff's business, trade, or profession, or, for a woman historically, unchastity, are defamatory on their face. For private plaintiffs and non-media defendants, damages may be presumed. For media defendants, *Gertz* requires proof of actual damages unless actual malice is shown.

**Opinion.** Pure opinion, which discloses the facts on which it is based and draws a conclusion, is protected. A statement that implies undisclosed defamatory facts is not. *Milkovich v. Lorain Journal Co.*, 497 U.S. 1 (1990). Rhetorical hyperbole that no reasonable reader would take as fact is protected.

**Truth and privilege.** Substantial truth is a complete defense. Absolute privilege covers statements in judicial and legislative proceedings. Qualified privilege covers statements made in good faith on a subject in which the speaker has an interest or duty to a person with a corresponding interest, such as a reference or an internal report, lost on a showing of express malice.

**Business disparagement** (injurious falsehood or trade libel) covers false statements about a business's products or property, rather than its character, and requires proof of special damages.

**Procedure in Florida.** Limitation period: 2 years (verify), § 95.11(4). Before suing a newspaper, periodical, or broadcast station, the plaintiff must serve written notice at least 5 days (verify) before filing, specifying the statements. § 770.01, Fla. Stat. A full and fair retraction limits the plaintiff to actual damages. § 770.02. The single publication rule, § 770.07, starts the clock at first publication.

**Online platforms.** Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, 47 U.S.C. § 230, bars treating a website or platform as the publisher of content provided by its users. The reviewer can be sued; the review site generally cannot.

**Anti-SLAPP.** Section 768.295 prohibits suits brought primarily because the defendant exercised free speech rights on a public issue and provides expedited dismissal and attorney fees.

The plaintiff bears the burden on falsity and fault. The defendant bears the burden on privilege.

## How courts reason about it

Defamation opinions are constitutional balancing acts. *Sullivan* reasoned that debate on public issues must be uninhibited, robust, and wide open, and that some false statements are inevitable. If critics had to guarantee every fact, they would self-censor. So the Court required actual malice for public officials. *Gertz* drew the line: private individuals have less access to media to rebut falsehoods and have not assumed the risk of scrutiny, so states may let them recover on negligence.

Business cases turn on a few recurring questions.

- **Fact or opinion.** "Worst contractor I ever hired" is opinion. "He took my deposit and never showed up" is fact. Courts read the whole statement in context, including the forum. A review site invites opinion; readers expect venting. A statement framed as personal experience that asserts specific events is fact, and provable.
- **Is the plaintiff a public figure?** A small business is usually private. A business that advertises heavily, takes public positions, or is in a regulated field with public attention may be a limited-purpose public figure as to that controversy. The classification decides whether negligence or actual malice applies, and that often decides the case.
- **Substantial truth.** The defendant need not prove every detail. If the gist is true, minor inaccuracies do not make it false. A review that says the plaintiff was "sued for fraud three times" when it was twice is substantially true.
- **Employer references.** Courts protect good-faith references by qualified privilege. The privilege is lost where the employer knew the statement was false or acted with ill will.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule and the federal constitutional framework. Its own features:

- **Pre-suit notice** under § 770.01 applies to media defendants. Florida courts have split on whether it reaches non-media online speakers (verify current DCA positions).
- **Defamation by implication** is recognized. *Jews for Jesus v. Rapp*. Literally true statements arranged to imply a false fact can be actionable. The same case declined to recognize the tort of false light.
- **Employer immunity.** Section 768.095 immunizes an employer that discloses information about a former or current employee to a prospective employer unless the information was knowingly false or violated a civil right of the employee (verify).
- **Anti-SLAPP** under § 768.295 covers suits over free speech in connection with public issues, including online reviews on matters of public concern (verify scope).
- **Litigation privilege** is absolute in Florida for statements made in the course of judicial proceedings, including demand letters closely related to anticipated litigation (verify).
- Limitation period: 2 years (verify), § 95.11(4).
- Punitive damages are available on the § 768.72 showing, and for media defendants require actual malice.

## Angles checklist

- What exactly was said, where, and to whom? Screenshot it with the date.
- Is it a statement of fact or an opinion that discloses its basis?
- Is it false? Is the gist false, or only a detail?
- Is the plaintiff private, or a public figure as to this topic?
- Does a per se category apply?
- Any privilege: judicial proceeding, good-faith reference, fair report?
- Is the defendant a media outlet requiring § 770.01 notice? A platform protected by § 230?
- What are the actual damages? Lost customers, cancelled contracts?
- Is this a SLAPP risk, with fee exposure under § 768.295?
- Flip it: before you respond to a review or give a reference, is what you plan to say provably true?

## Drill

A Fort Myers pool contractor receives a Google review from a customer: "This company is a scam. They took my $8,000 deposit, did two days of work, and disappeared. I found out they are not even licensed. Avoid at all costs." The contractor is licensed, did stop work for three weeks over a permit dispute, then finished the job. The contractor wants to sue the customer and Google. Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Google.** Barred by 47 U.S.C. § 230. Google is not the publisher of the customer's content. The contractor can ask Google to remove the review under its policies, but there is no claim.
- **Statements of fact versus opinion.** "Scam" and "avoid at all costs" are opinion and hyperbole in a review context. "Took my $8,000 deposit, did two days of work, and disappeared" is a factual account, partly true: work did stop. "Not even licensed" is a pure statement of fact and false.
- **Falsity and substantial truth.** "Disappeared" is misleading because the contractor returned and finished, but a three-week stoppage after taking a deposit makes the gist arguably close enough that a jury could go either way. "Not licensed" is false and not a minor detail.
- **Per se.** Unlicensed contracting is a crime in Florida and a statement that a contractor is unlicensed imputes conduct incompatible with the trade. Defamation per se.
- **Fault.** The contractor is a private figure. Negligence suffices. The customer could have checked DBPR's license lookup. Negligence is provable.
- **Damages.** Per se status helps, but the contractor should gather evidence of lost jobs after the review. Against a non-media defendant, presumed damages may be available.
- **Anti-SLAPP.** The customer will argue the review is speech on a public issue. A consumer review of a contractor's licensing is a plausible matter of public concern. The contractor's claim is not meritless because the licensing statement is false, so the SLAPP motion likely fails, but the fee risk is real.
- **Likely outcome.** A viable claim limited to the licensing statement, against the customer only. Damages may be modest. The practical path is a demand to correct the review, then suit if refused.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the contractor's license had lapsed at the time, the statement is substantially true. If the contractor responds publicly with false statements about the customer, it faces a counterclaim.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A former manager asks for a reference. You say she "was let go for cause." She was. Any risk?
2. A blogger writes that your restaurant "gave me food poisoning." You can prove the health inspection that week was clean. Can you sue the blogger, and what must you do first?
3. A competitor tells a shared customer that your company "is being investigated by the state." It is not. Which claims?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Little. Truth is a complete defense, and § 768.095 immunizes good-faith employer disclosures to prospective employers. The risk arises only if the statement is false or you add knowingly false detail.
2. Yes, potentially, if the statement is false and the blogger was at least negligent. Whether a blogger is a "periodical" requiring § 770.01 notice is unsettled in Florida; serve the notice anyway to be safe. A clean inspection is evidence, not proof, that the customer was not sickened, so falsity will be contested.
3. Defamation per se, since it imputes criminal or regulatory misconduct incompatible with your business; tortious interference with the customer relationship if the customer reduced business; and possibly FDUTPA if the statement was part of the competitor's trade practices. The competitor's own statement defeats any competition privilege.

</details>
