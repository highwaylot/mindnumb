# Agency and authority

**Module:** 05 · **Lesson:** 1 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

An LLC cannot sign anything, buy anything, or promise anything. People do those things for it. Agency law decides when a person's act counts as the LLC's act, and when it counts as that person's own. It answers two questions that come up constantly: "Did my salesperson just commit us to that?" and "Did I just make myself personally liable by the way I signed?"

## The rule

An **agent** is a person who agrees to act on behalf of a **principal**, subject to the principal's control. The principal is bound by the agent's acts when the agent had **authority**. Authority comes in four forms.

1. **Actual express authority.** The principal told the agent, in words, to do the thing.
2. **Actual implied authority.** Authority the agent reasonably believes it has because of the express grant, the agent's position, or the principal's past acquiescence. A store manager hired to run a store has implied authority to order inventory.
3. **Apparent authority.** The principal, by words or conduct directed at the third party, led the third party reasonably to believe the agent was authorized. Apparent authority is created by the principal's manifestations, never by the agent's own claims. Florida courts state three elements: a representation by the principal, reasonable reliance by the third party, and a change of position in reliance. The party asserting apparent authority bears the burden.
4. **Ratification.** The principal, knowing the material facts, affirms an unauthorized act, by words or by accepting its benefits. Ratification relates back; the act is treated as authorized from the start.

Two further rules follow.

- **Undisclosed principal.** If the agent deals in its own name and the third party does not know a principal exists, the agent is personally liable on the contract. The principal is also liable once discovered. If the agent discloses that it acts for someone but not who, the agent is still personally liable unless the parties agreed otherwise.
- **The agent's own liability.** An agent acting within authority for a disclosed principal is not a party to the contract. An agent acting without authority is liable to the third party for breach of the implied warranty of authority.

The agent owes the principal fiduciary duties: loyalty, obedience, care, disclosure.

## How courts reason about it

Courts are deciding who should bear the loss when an agent went beyond what the principal wanted. Three questions do most of the work.

**What did the principal do?** Apparent authority turns on the principal's conduct, not the agent's. Giving someone the title "Vice President," listing them on the website as the contact for contracts, letting them sign past deals without objection, or handing them letterhead and a company email address are all manifestations. Courts look for a pattern the third party could reasonably have seen. A single unauthorized act the principal never knew about creates nothing.

**What did the third party know or have reason to know?** Reliance must be reasonable. A third party who knows the limits on the agent's authority, or who is dealing with a transaction so unusual that a reasonable person would check, cannot claim apparent authority. The larger and stranger the deal, the more a court expects the third party to ask for proof.

**Did the principal accept the benefit?** Ratification is the fallback. A principal who learns of an unauthorized contract and keeps the goods, cashes the check, or stays silent while the other side performs has ratified. Courts find ratification readily because the alternative lets the principal keep the upside and reject the downside.

Underneath, courts ask who was better placed to prevent the problem: the principal who handed out the trappings of authority, or the third party who ignored a red flag. Florida courts cite the Restatement (Third) of Agency (2006) as persuasive.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority common-law rules above. In *Mobil Oil Corp. v. Bransford*, 648 So. 2d 119 (Fla. 1995) (verify citation), the Florida Supreme Court held that a franchisee's use of the brand's signs and logos alone does not make the franchisor liable for the franchisee's acts without a further representation of control.

For LLCs, chapter 605 adds statutory rules.

- **Who is an agent of the LLC.** Under § 605.04074, Fla. Stat., in a **member-managed** LLC each member is an agent of the LLC for its activities and affairs, and a member's act for apparently carrying on the LLC's business in the ordinary course binds the LLC, unless the member had no authority and the person dealing with the member knew or had notice of that. In a **manager-managed** LLC, a member is not an agent solely by being a member; the managers hold that power. This is a Florida departure from the uniform act, which dropped statutory apparent authority. In Florida a member of a member-managed LLC can bind it in the ordinary course even if the operating agreement says otherwise, unless the third party knew.
- **Statement of authority.** Under § 605.0302, an LLC may file a statement of authority with the Department of State that grants or limits the authority of any position or person to enter transactions or transfer real property. A filed statement, recorded in the county for real property, gives constructive notice of the limits.
- **Personal liability of members and managers.** Under § 605.0304, a member or manager is not personally liable for an LLC's obligations solely by being a member or manager. That protection does not cover an obligation the person took on personally, which is where signature form matters.

**How to sign.** The standard form is the entity name, then the person's name and capacity:

> Sunrise Tile, LLC
> By: Maria Lopez, Manager

Signing "Maria Lopez" alone on a contract that does not name the LLC can make Maria the party. Florida courts look at the whole document: if the LLC is identified as the contracting party in the body and the signature block, a bare signature is usually read as the LLC's. If the document is ambiguous, parol evidence may come in and the agent can be held. A personal guaranty is a separate signature in an individual capacity; signing the contract "as manager" does not defeat a guaranty you signed as yourself.

## Angles checklist

- Who was the principal and who was the agent? Was the LLC member-managed or manager-managed at the time?
- What did the principal actually authorize, in writing or by course of dealing?
- What did the principal say or do toward the third party? Titles, cards, website, prior deals?
- Was the transaction in the ordinary course? How big and how unusual?
- Did the principal accept any benefit after learning of the deal?
- How was the document signed? Is the entity named in the body and the signature block?
- Flip it: can the third party sue the agent for breach of warranty of authority? Can the principal sue the agent for exceeding authority or for disloyalty?

## Drill

Coastal Fitness, LLC is a member-managed Florida LLC with three members. The operating agreement says any contract over $10,000 needs approval of two members. Tom, a member, signs a 36-month equipment lease for $48,000 with a vendor who has dealt only with Tom for two years on smaller orders. Tom signs "Tom Reyes" on the vendor's form, which lists "Coastal Fitness" as the lessee. The other members find out when the equipment arrives, use it for six weeks, then try to cancel. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Actual authority.** Tom lacked it; the operating agreement required two members above $10,000. That limit binds Tom as to the LLC but not necessarily as to the vendor.
- **Statutory agency.** Under § 605.04074, Tom's act in apparently carrying on the business in the ordinary course binds the LLC unless the vendor knew or had notice he lacked authority. Leasing gym equipment is ordinary course for a gym. Nothing suggests the vendor knew of the cap. Likely bound.
- **Apparent authority.** Two years of unobjected dealings with Tom are manifestations by the LLC. The deal is larger than before, which cuts slightly the other way, but it is not extraordinary. Reliance looks reasonable.
- **Ratification.** The members used the equipment for six weeks after learning of the lease. That is acceptance of the benefit with knowledge, and likely closes the question.
- **Tom's personal liability.** The form names "Coastal Fitness," not "Coastal Fitness, LLC," and Tom signed without a capacity. The document is probably read as the LLC's, but the vendor may argue Tom is personally liable and parol evidence could be admitted.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A manager of a manager-managed Florida LLC signs a contract the operating agreement forbids. The other side never saw the operating agreement. Is the LLC bound?
2. Can an agent's own statement, "I am authorized," create apparent authority?
3. A member signs a lease as "Jane Doe, Member, Oak Street Bakery, LLC," and then signs a separate "Personal Guaranty" as "Jane Doe." Which signature exposes her personally?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Probably yes. Managers of a manager-managed LLC hold the statutory agency power for ordinary-course acts, and a third party without knowledge or notice of the internal limit can rely. The LLC's remedy is against the manager. A filed statement of authority limiting the manager could change the result by giving notice.
2. No. Apparent authority flows from the principal's manifestations to the third party. The agent's own claims count only if the principal put the agent in a position where such claims would reasonably be believed, which is really a manifestation by the principal.
3. The guaranty. The lease signature is in a representative capacity and binds the LLC. The guaranty is signed individually and binds Jane.

</details>
