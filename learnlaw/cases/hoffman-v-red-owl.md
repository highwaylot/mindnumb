# Hoffman v. Red Owl Stores, Inc., 26 Wis. 2d 683, 133 N.W.2d 267 (Wisconsin Supreme Court 1965)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · promissory estoppel · reliance damages · negotiations · franchise
**Used in:** [03-contracts/02-consideration-and-promissory-estoppel.md](../03-contracts/02-consideration-and-promissory-estoppel.md), [03-contracts/03-statute-of-frauds-and-writings.md](../03-contracts/03-statute-of-frauds-and-writings.md), [03-contracts/07-remedies.md](../03-contracts/07-remedies.md)

## One-line takeaway

A promise that the promisor should expect to induce reliance, and that does induce it, can be enforced to the extent needed to avoid injustice even if the parties never reached a contract, with damages limited to the reliance loss.

## Facts

Joseph Hoffman owned a bakery in Wautoma, Wisconsin, and wanted a Red Owl grocery franchise. Over about two years, Red Owl's representative, Lukowitz, repeatedly assured him that $18,000 of capital would be enough. On Red Owl's advice Hoffman bought a small grocery store in Wautoma to gain experience, then sold it just before its profitable summer season because Red Owl said he would have his franchise soon. He paid $1,000 toward a lot in Chilton that Red Owl had selected. He sold his bakery building and business. He moved his family to Neenah and took a night job. Red Owl then raised the required capital to $24,100, then $26,000, then $34,000, and finally demanded that a $13,000 contribution from Hoffman's father-in-law be treated as a gift or as subordinated debt. Hoffman refused and the deal collapsed. No contract was ever signed and the essential terms of the franchise were never settled.

## Procedural posture

Hoffman and his wife sued Red Owl and Lukowitz. The jury found that Red Owl had made representations it should have expected Hoffman to rely on, that he relied on them, and awarded damages for the losses on the bakery, the grocery store, the Chilton lot, moving expenses, and other items. The trial court entered judgment on most of the award but ordered a new trial on the grocery store damages. Both sides appealed.

## Issue

Can a party who relied on promises made during negotiations recover when the negotiations never produced a contract because the essential terms were never agreed, and if so, what damages?

## Holding

Yes. Wisconsin adopted promissory estoppel as stated in Restatement of Contracts § 90 as an independent cause of action. The promise need not be definite enough to be a contract. Damages are measured by the reliance loss, not by the profits the promised business would have earned. The judgment was affirmed, with the grocery store damages to be retried under the correct measure.

## Reasoning

Justice Currie identified three questions under § 90. Was the promise one the promisor should reasonably expect to induce action or forbearance of a definite and substantial character? Did it induce such action? Can injustice be avoided only by enforcement? The first two were jury questions, answered for Hoffman. The third was for the court: Red Owl had kept Hoffman moving for two years on assurances it should have known he was acting on, then changed the terms.

The court rejected Red Owl's argument that promissory estoppel requires a promise with all the elements of an offer. Section 90 is not a substitute for consideration in an otherwise complete bargain. It is a separate basis of liability grounded in reliance.

On damages, the remedy is what justice requires, which in a reliance case is the out-of-pocket loss. Hoffman could recover the lot payment, moving costs, and the amount by which he sold the grocery store below its fair value. He could not recover profits from the store he never got.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. Red Owl's strongest argument was that nothing had been promised. The capital figures were estimates that changed as the project grew, the site changed, and Hoffman's financing changed. Businesses negotiate for months and most negotiations fail. If every assurance along the way creates liability, no one can negotiate freely. The case is often described as the high-water mark of promissory estoppel. Several states, including Florida, require a more definite promise.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

One lesson for each side of the table. If you are courting a franchisee, distributor, hire, or vendor, do not make assurances you will not stand behind, and do not let the other side spend money on your encouragement. Put "no agreement until a signed definitive contract" in writing early. If you are the one investing in reliance on a deal that is not signed, get the key promises in writing, keep a record of what you were told, and understand that in Florida your recovery for a failed negotiation is uncertain and, at best, limited to what you spent.

## Florida connection

Florida follows promissory estoppel but in a narrower form than this case. The Florida Supreme Court adopted the doctrine in *W.R. Grace & Co. v. Geodata Services, Inc.*, 547 So. 2d 919 (Fla. 1989), and held that the promise must be definite and of a substantial nature, that the reliance must be reasonable, and that the doctrine is not a vehicle for enforcing vague assurances. In *Grace* the court found the promise too indefinite. Florida also holds that promissory estoppel cannot be used to circumvent the statute of frauds, § 725.01, Fla. Stat.; see *Tanenbaum v. Biscayne Osteopathic Hospital, Inc.*, 190 So. 2d 777 (Fla. 1966). So a Florida court would likely treat Hoffman's shifting capital figures as too indefinite to support a claim, and a promise of a multi-year franchise or lease would face the writing requirement as well. Where the doctrine does apply, Florida limits recovery to reliance damages.
