# Civil procedure in outline

**Module:** 01 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Procedure is the sequence of a lawsuit: who files what, by when, and what happens if they do not. A missed deadline becomes a default. A defense not pleaded is waived. A claim not raised in the first case is lost. Knowing the sequence lets you read a court document and know what the next deadline is.

## The rule

The Florida Rules of Civil Procedure govern Florida circuit and county civil cases; the Federal Rules govern federal cases. They track each other closely. The stages, in order:

1. **Complaint.** Florida requires **fact pleading**: the ultimate facts showing entitlement to relief, Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.110(b). Fraud must be pleaded with particularity, Rule 1.120(b). Federal court requires a plausible claim, Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(a), *Bell Atlantic Corp. v. Twombly*, 550 U.S. 544 (2007). Filing stops the limitations clock.
2. **Service of process.** Individuals are served personally or at their usual abode, § 48.031, Fla. Stat. An LLC is served on its registered agent, and if that fails, on a member or manager and then by the further steps in § 48.062. Corporations: § 48.081. Service must be made within 120 days (verify) of filing in Florida, Rule 1.070(j), and 90 days (verify) in federal court, Fed. R. Civ. P. 4(m).
3. **Response.** Florida gives the defendant 20 days (verify) from service to answer or move, Rule 1.140(a); federal court, 21 days (verify), Fed. R. Civ. P. 12(a). A **motion to dismiss** argues the complaint fails on its face: lack of jurisdiction, improper venue, failure to state a claim, Rule 1.140(b). Jurisdiction and venue objections not raised in the first response are waived.
4. **Answer and affirmative defenses.** The answer admits or denies each allegation and pleads **affirmative defenses**, facts that defeat the claim even if the plaintiff proves it: limitations, statute of frauds, payment, release, waiver, estoppel, accord and satisfaction, Rule 1.110(d); Fed. R. Civ. P. 8(c). The defendant bears the burden of proving them. A defense not pleaded is generally waived.
5. **Default.** No response, and the clerk or court enters a default, Rule 1.500; Fed. R. Civ. P. 55. The allegations are taken as true and judgment follows. Setting it aside requires excusable neglect, a meritorious defense, and diligence.
6. **Counterclaims and third parties.** A **compulsory counterclaim** arises from the same transaction and must be brought in the same case or it is lost, Rule 1.170(a); Fed. R. Civ. P. 13(a). A third-party complaint brings in someone who may owe the defendant indemnity or contribution, Rule 1.180.
7. **Discovery.** Initial disclosures, interrogatories, requests for production, requests for admission, depositions, and subpoenas to third parties. The scope is anything relevant to a claim or defense and proportional to the needs of the case, Rule 1.280; Fed. R. Civ. P. 26. Florida adopted proportionality, initial disclosures, and mandatory case management effective January 1, 2025 (verify). Attorney-client communications are privileged. Parties must preserve evidence once litigation is reasonably anticipated.
8. **Summary judgment.** Judgment without trial if there is no genuine dispute of material fact. Florida adopted the federal standard effective May 1, 2021 (verify), Rule 1.510, as set out in *Celotex Corp. v. Catrett*, 477 U.S. 317 (1986), and *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby, Inc.*, 477 U.S. 242 (1986). The question is whether a reasonable jury could find for the non-moving party.
9. **Mediation.** Florida courts order mediation in most civil cases, ch. 44, Fla. Stat., and Rules 1.700 to 1.730.
10. **Trial.** A jury must be demanded in a pleading or within 10 days (verify) after the last pleading in Florida, Rule 1.430; 14 days (verify) in federal court, Fed. R. Civ. P. 38. Money claims get a jury on demand; equitable claims go to the judge. The plaintiff proves each element by a preponderance of the evidence.
11. **Judgment.** Post-trial motions under Rules 1.480 and 1.530 have short deadlines. The judgment is the enforceable document; interest, costs, and fees are added by later motion.
12. **Appeal.** Notice within 30 days (verify) of rendition, Fla. R. App. P. 9.110(b); Fed. R. App. P. 4(a). The deadline is jurisdictional. A money judgment is stayed on appeal only if the appellant posts a bond for the judgment plus two years' interest (verify), Fla. R. App. P. 9.310(b)(1).

Throughout, an entity must appear through counsel outside small claims.

## How courts reason about it

Procedural rules are rules, not standards, and courts enforce them as written far more often than they forgive. Deadlines and waivers are what make the system work for everyone else. Three places where the reasoning matters most:

**Motions to dismiss.** The court takes the complaint's facts as true and asks whether they state a claim. In Florida, ultimate facts, not conclusions, must fit every element. A complaint that says "defendant breached the contract" without saying what the contract required and what the defendant did loses. Dismissals are usually with leave to amend, so the real effect is delay and cost.

**Summary judgment.** Before 2021, Florida's standard from *Holl v. Talcott*, 191 So. 2d 40 (Fla. 1966), denied summary judgment if there was the "slightest doubt" about the facts, so nearly every case went to trial or settled under the threat of one. The federal standard asks whether the evidence would let a reasonable jury find for the non-movant. A movant who does not bear the burden at trial can win by showing the other side lacks evidence on an element; the non-movant must answer with record evidence, not argument. Discovery matters more, because the case can end on paper.

**Waiver.** The compulsory counterclaim rule is the sharpest trap. A defendant sued for an unpaid invoice who has a claim that the goods were defective must bring it in that case. If it waits and sues later, the claim is barred. The policy is efficiency: one transaction, one lawsuit.

Courts also punish **spoliation**. A business that deletes emails after it knows a suit is coming can face an adverse inference instruction or worse.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the federal model in most respects. The main differences: fact pleading; a 20-day response time; county-based venue; mediation in nearly every civil case.
- The 2021 summary judgment change and the 2025 amendments are the largest procedural shifts in a generation. Older Florida cases on the summary judgment standard are no longer good law.
- **Pre-suit requirements** often precede the complaint: a 21-day (verify) safe harbor before a § 57.105 sanctions motion; leave of court on a record showing before pleading punitive damages, § 768.72; administrative charges before discrimination suits; statutory notices in construction lien and landlord-tenant matters.
- **Proposals for settlement**, § 768.79 and Rule 1.442, run during the case and shift fees if the result misses the offer by 25% (verify). Module 08 covers them.
- **Service on an LLC** follows the order in § 48.062. A registered agent who cannot be found at the registered office exposes the company to substituted service it may never see.
- Small claims cases up to $8,000 (verify) use the Small Claims Rules: a simple statement of claim, a pretrial conference with mediation, limited discovery.

## Angles checklist

- What is the deadline on the document in hand, and what happens if it is missed?
- Has the entity been properly served? Is the registered agent reachable?
- What affirmative defenses must be in the answer? Limitations, statute of frauds, payment, release, waiver, arbitration?
- Is there a compulsory counterclaim? If so, it goes in now or never.
- Who else should be brought in: subcontractor, insurer, guarantor, other member?
- What documents and electronic records must be preserved today?
- Can the case be resolved on a motion to dismiss or at summary judgment, or is it a jury case?
- Has anyone demanded a jury? Is a proposal for settlement in play?

## Drill

An Orlando marketing LLC is served on March 1 with a circuit court complaint by a former client claiming $90,000 for breach of a services contract. The LLC believes the client never paid the last three invoices totaling $40,000 and that the client's own delays caused the problems. The owner puts the papers in a drawer, planning to call a lawyer "when things calm down." List the procedural issues and deadlines.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Response deadline.** Twenty days from service, so March 21. Doing nothing risks a default, with the $90,000 claim taken as proven. "I put it in a drawer" is a weak showing of excusable neglect.
- **Counsel.** The LLC cannot respond on its own. It needs a lawyer able to file within the 20 days or seek an extension before they run.
- **Compulsory counterclaim.** The $40,000 in unpaid invoices arises from the same contract. It must be pleaded as a counterclaim in this case or it is barred.
- **Affirmative defenses.** Prior breach by the client, failure of a condition, waiver, and any contractual limitation of liability must be pleaded in the answer. Any arbitration clause must be invoked now or it is waived.
- **Preservation.** The LLC must stop any automatic deletion of email and project files now.
- **Fees.** Does the contract have a prevailing-party fee clause? If so it is reciprocal under § 57.105(7). Consider a proposal for settlement once the facts are clear.
- **Open questions.** Who was served and how? Is there a signed contract with a fee, venue, or arbitration clause?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A defendant answers a complaint and, six months later, realizes the claim was filed after the limitations period ran. Can it raise the defense?
2. Under the 2021 Florida summary judgment standard, what must a non-moving party do to defeat a properly supported motion?
3. A business is sued for $12,000 on an unpaid invoice and has a $30,000 claim that the same goods were defective. Where must the $30,000 claim be brought?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Only with leave to amend. An unpleaded affirmative defense is waived, though courts often allow amendment before trial if the other side is not prejudiced. Plead every colorable defense in the first answer.
2. Come forward with record evidence, such as affidavits, deposition testimony, or documents, from which a reasonable jury could find in its favor on each disputed element. Argument, speculation, and a sliver of doubt are no longer enough.
3. As a compulsory counterclaim in the same case, because it arises from the same transaction. A counterclaim above the county court limit can move the case to circuit court.

</details>
