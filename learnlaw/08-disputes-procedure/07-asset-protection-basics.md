# Asset protection basics

**Module:** 08 · **Lesson:** 7 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[FL]` `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Every owner asks some version of "if the business gets sued, can they take my house." The answer is a set of doctrines that together describe what a creditor can and cannot reach. Florida is unusually protective of individuals. But limited liability has holes, exemptions have limits, and moving assets after a claim arises is a fraudulent transfer. This lesson explains the doctrines. It does not tell anyone how to arrange their affairs; that is planning advice from a lawyer who knows the facts.

## The rule

**What limited liability protects.** A member of an LLC is not personally liable for the LLC's debts solely by reason of being a member (§ 605.0304).

**What it does not protect.**

- **Personal guaranties.** A guaranty is a separate contract the LLC shield does not touch.
- **The member's own torts.** A member who personally commits negligence, fraud, or another tort is liable for it.
- **Veil piercing.** Under *Dania Jai-Alai*, a creditor may reach the owner where the entity was used for improper conduct and the owner dominated it such that it was a mere instrumentality.
- **Statutory personal liability.** Trust fund taxes (§ 213.29; 26 U.S.C. § 6672), unpaid wages under the FLSA where the owner is an "employer," securities sales under § 517.211, unlicensed contracting.

**Homestead (Fla. Const. art. X, § 4).** A Florida resident's homestead is exempt from forced sale by creditors, with no dollar limit, up to one-half acre inside a municipality or 160 acres outside. Three exceptions: taxes and assessments, mortgages and other liens for purchase or improvement of the property, and construction liens for work on it. Under *Havoco of America, Ltd. v. Hill*, 790 So. 2d 1018 (Fla. 2001), the exemption holds even where the debtor bought the homestead with non-exempt funds to defeat a creditor, unless the funds themselves were obtained by fraud. In bankruptcy, federal law caps the exemption at about $189,050 (verify) for property acquired within 1,215 days (verify) of filing (11 U.S.C. § 522(p)) and requires 730 days (verify) of Florida domicile.

**Tenancy by the entireties.** Property owned by a married couple as tenants by the entireties cannot be reached by a creditor of only one spouse. Under *Beal Bank, SSB v. Almand & Associates*, 780 So. 2d 45 (Fla. 2001), personal property, including bank accounts, held jointly by spouses is presumed entireties property if the six unities are present. The protection ends at divorce or the death of the non-debtor spouse, and does not apply to joint debts.

**Retirement accounts (§ 222.21).** ERISA plans, IRAs, Roth IRAs, and inherited IRAs are exempt from creditors' claims.

**Annuities and life insurance (§ 222.14).** The cash surrender value of life insurance on a Florida resident and the proceeds of annuity contracts are exempt from the owner's creditors.

**Wages (§ 222.11).** Head-of-family disposable earnings up to $750 (verify) per week fully exempt; above that, only with written consent. Deposited earnings keep the exemption for six months (verify) if traceable.

**LLC interests (§ 605.0503).** A creditor of a member gets a charging order on distributions, which is the exclusive remedy in a multi-member LLC. A single-member LLC interest can be foreclosed.

**Limits: fraudulent transfers (ch. 726).** A transfer made with intent to hinder, delay, or defraud creditors, or for less than reasonably equivalent value while insolvent, is voidable for four years (verify). Converting non-exempt property to exempt property with intent to defraud is itself voidable under § 222.30 (verify). The bankruptcy code adds a ten-year (verify) reach for transfers to self-settled trusts made to defraud (11 U.S.C. § 548(e)).

**Self-settled trusts.** Florida does not allow a person to shield assets from creditors by placing them in a trust for the person's own benefit (§ 736.0505). Whether a Florida court will honor another state's asset protection trust statute for a Florida debtor is doubtful (verify).

**Insurance.** The first line. Liability, umbrella, and professional coverage pay claims and provide a defense, which keeps the creditor from ever reaching the exemption analysis.

**Burden.** The debtor claiming an exemption bears the burden of showing it applies. The creditor challenging a transfer bears the burden on fraudulent intent, aided by the badges of fraud.

## How courts reason about it

- **Exemptions are construed liberally, transfers strictly.** Timing relative to the claim is the first fact every court examines.
- **Intent is inferred from badges.** Insider transferee, retained control, concealment, transfer after suit or threat of suit, transfer of substantially all assets, insolvency, lack of consideration. Three or four together usually suffice.
- **Homestead is nearly absolute, by design.** *Havoco* is the clearest statement: the constitution's text has three exceptions and courts will not add a fourth for bad intent. The equitable lien exception requires that the money used was itself obtained by fraud, not merely that the debtor owed money.
- **Entireties requires the unities and a marriage.** Courts look at whether the account or asset was acquired during the marriage, jointly, with equal rights of control and survivorship. A creditor with a judgment against both spouses reaches it fully.
- **Veil piercing stays rare.** Courts require improper conduct, not just a thin balance sheet or a single owner. Reverse piercing, where a creditor of the owner reaches the LLC's assets, is applied when the owner uses the LLC as a personal pocket.
- **Bankruptcy changes the rules.** Florida's exemptions apply in bankruptcy because Florida opted out of the federal list (§ 222.20), but the federal homestead cap, the domicile rule, and the ten-year trust lookback were enacted precisely to stop last-minute moves to Florida.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the majority of states in recognizing fraudulent transfer law under the Uniform Act and in exempting retirement accounts. It departs in its unlimited-value homestead, which only a few states share, and in its broad entireties presumption for personal property.
- Florida's refusal to recognize self-settled spendthrift trusts follows the traditional rule.
- The $4,000 wildcard exemption under § 222.25(4) is available only to a debtor who does not claim the homestead exemption.

## Angles checklist

- What is the claim, when did it arise, and what did the owner own and move before and after that date?
- Is the owner personally liable at all: guaranty, own tort, statute, piercing facts?
- Homestead: Florida resident, acreage, when acquired, how funded?
- Entireties: married, titled jointly, any joint debt?
- Retirement, insurance, annuities: properly titled and funded before the claim?
- LLC interests: single-member or multi-member, real co-members, charging order exposure?
- Transfers: any to insiders, for less than value, after a claim? Badges of fraud?
- Insurance: policies, limits, exclusions, notice?
- Flip it, as creditor: which of these walls is thin on these facts?
- Bankruptcy overlay: domicile period, homestead cap, trust lookback.

## Drill

Priya owns 100% of a landscaping LLC. A crew member on a riding mower severely injured a pedestrian last month; the LLC has a $1 million liability policy. Priya, who was not present, is worried. She owns a home with her husband purchased in 2019, has $400,000 in an IRA, $90,000 in a joint savings account with her husband opened in 2021, and a rental duplex in her own name. She is thinking about transferring the duplex to her husband and converting the LLC into a two-member LLC with her husband holding 1%. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Who is liable.** The LLC is liable for the crew member's negligence under respondeat superior. Priya is not personally liable solely as a member, and she committed no tort herself. Absent a piercing theory, her personal assets are not exposed. The policy provides a defense and $1 million.
- **Home.** Homestead, exempt without value limit if within acreage limits. Also likely entireties property, which is beyond the reach of a creditor of Priya alone.
- **IRA.** Exempt under § 222.21.
- **Joint savings.** Presumed entireties under *Beal Bank* if opened jointly during the marriage with no contrary designation. Beyond the reach of Priya's sole creditors.
- **Duplex.** Non-exempt, in her name alone. It is reachable only if Priya becomes personally liable. Transferring it to her husband now, after the injury and for no value, would be a fraudulent transfer under § 726.105 if a claim against her ever materializes, and it would hand a creditor the badges of fraud.
- **Adding a 1% member.** The charging order protection for multi-member LLCs is tested on whether the co-member's interest is real. A token spousal interest added after a claim arose invites a court to treat the LLC as single-member for § 605.0503 purposes and to view the change as a transfer in fraud of creditors.
- **Red herring.** The pedestrian's claim is against the LLC, not Priya. Her exposure is the LLC's assets and the policy. The panic is directed at the wrong defendant unless facts emerge that she personally was negligent in hiring or training.
- **Facts that would change it.** If Priya had personally been operating the mower, or had personally guaranteed something, or if the verdict exceeds the policy and the plaintiff finds piercing facts, the duplex is the reachable asset.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A debtor sells his boat and uses the proceeds to pay down the mortgage on his homestead the month after being sued. Can the creditor reach the equity?
2. A creditor has a judgment against both husband and wife. Does tenancy by the entireties protect their joint account?
3. Why is insurance called the first line of asset protection?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Generally no, under *Havoco*. The homestead exemption has three constitutional exceptions and bad intent is not one of them. The creditor could reach it only by showing the boat proceeds themselves were obtained by fraud, supporting an equitable lien.
2. No. Entireties protection applies only against a creditor of one spouse. A joint judgment reaches entireties property.
3. Because it pays the claim and funds the defense before any question of exemptions arises, and because it is the only form of protection that does not depend on the timing or intent of the owner's transfers.

</details>
