# Florida's 2023 tort reform and comparative fault

**Module:** 04 · **Lesson:** 7 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

On March 24, 2023, Florida changed the rules for nearly every negligence case a business will face. HB 837, chapter 2023-15, Laws of Florida, cut the time to sue in half, barred recovery by plaintiffs who are mostly at fault, changed how juries hear medical bills, rewrote insurance bad faith law, and gave property owners a shield against negligent security claims. Because the changes apply only to claims that accrued after the effective date, two sets of rules are running at once.

## The rule

**Comparative fault.** Section 768.81, Fla. Stat., governs negligence actions, which the statute defines to include civil actions for damages based on negligence, including professional malpractice and products liability, however the claim is labeled. The rule has three parts.

1. **Apportionment.** The fact-finder assigns a percentage of fault to each party and to any non-party the defendant has pleaded and proven contributed to the injury. *Fabre v. Marin*, 623 So. 2d 1182 (Fla. 1993). Each defendant pays only its own percentage. Joint and several liability was abolished in 2006. § 768.81(3).
2. **Reduction.** The plaintiff's damages are reduced by the plaintiff's own percentage of fault.
3. **The bar.** For causes of action accruing on or after March 24, 2023 (verify), a plaintiff found more than 50% (verify) at fault recovers nothing. § 768.81(6). This is modified comparative negligence. The bar does not apply to actions for medical negligence under chapter 766. Before HB 837, Florida used pure comparative negligence, adopted in *Hoffman v. Jones*, 280 So. 2d 431 (Fla. 1973), under which a 90% at-fault plaintiff still recovered 10%.

Section 768.81(4) excludes intentional torts from apportionment. An intentional tortfeasor is liable for the whole.

**Limitation period.** Negligence claims accruing on or after March 24, 2023 must be filed within 2 years (verify), down from 4 (verify). § 95.11(4)(a) (verify subsection). Claims that accrued before that date keep the 4-year period. A cause of action accrues when the last element occurs, usually the injury. § 95.031(1).

**Medical damages evidence.** Section 768.0427 limits what a jury hears about medical expenses. For bills already paid, only the amount actually paid is admissible. For unpaid bills, the admissible amount depends on whether the plaintiff has health insurance, and for uninsured plaintiffs is tied to a percentage of the Medicare rate (verify percentages). Treatment under a letter of protection must be disclosed, along with whether the plaintiff's lawyer referred the plaintiff to the provider. The point is to stop juries from seeing inflated billed charges nobody pays.

**Negligent security.** Section 768.0701 requires the fact-finder to consider the fault of all persons who contributed to the injury, including the criminal. Section 768.0706 creates a presumption against liability for owners and operators of multifamily residential property that implement listed security measures. Lesson 2.

**Insurance bad faith.** Section 624.155 was amended so that an insurer that tenders the policy limits or the amount demanded within 90 days (verify) after receiving actual notice of a claim with sufficient evidence to support it cannot be liable for bad faith. Mere negligence by the insurer is not bad faith. The claimant and the claimant's lawyer owe a duty of good faith in presenting the claim, and a jury may reduce damages for their failure. Procedures were added for multiple claimants against one policy.

**Attorney fees.** HB 837 repealed the one-way fee statutes that let an insured recover fees from its insurer in most coverage disputes, §§ 627.428 and 626.9373, replacing them with a narrower fee right in declaratory judgment actions after a total denial of coverage, § 86.121 (verify). It also amended § 57.104 to create a strong presumption that a lodestar fee is reasonable, limiting contingency fee multipliers (verify).

**Effective date and accrual.** The act took effect on becoming law, March 24, 2023. Section 30 of the act provides that it applies to causes of action accruing after the effective date, and that the amendments to § 95.11 apply to causes of action accruing after that date (verify text). Claims that accrued earlier are governed by prior law even if filed later.

## How courts reason about it

Under the old contributory negligence rule, any fault by the plaintiff barred recovery, which led juries to ignore plaintiff fault entirely. *Hoffman v. Jones* reasoned that all-or-nothing produced arbitrary results and that apportioning fault was fairer and more honest. The 2023 legislature took the view that a plaintiff mostly responsible for their own injury should not collect from someone less responsible, which is the majority rule nationally.

The practical effects, which courts and lawyers have been working out since:

- **The 50% line is now the whole case.** Defense strategy in every negligence case is to push the plaintiff's share over 50%. Plaintiff strategy is to keep it under. Evidence of the plaintiff's conduct that was once a damages issue is now a liability issue.
- **Fabre defendants matter more.** Fault assigned to a non-party reduces the defendant's share. But adding non-parties can also dilute the plaintiff's share and keep the plaintiff under 50%. The tactics are not obvious.
- **Accrual fights.** Courts have had to decide when claims accrued for injuries that developed over time, for claims involving delayed discovery, and for claims filed after March 24, 2023 over injuries before it. The general rule is that accrual is when the injury occurred, not when the suit was filed, so a 2022 injury filed in 2025 gets the 4-year period and pure comparative negligence.
- **Medical damages.** Section 768.0427 cut the headline numbers, since billed charges often ran several times what was paid. Litigation over its application to letters of protection and future expenses continues (verify).
- **Bad faith.** The 90-day safe harbor changed insurer behavior. Clear-liability claims above policy limits now get tendered quickly. Courts are defining what "sufficient evidence" means.
- **Constitutional challenges.** Retroactivity arguments have mostly failed because the act is prospective by its terms. Access-to-courts challenges under article I, section 21 of the Florida Constitution have been raised against the shortened limitation period and the 50% bar (verify status).

## Florida specifics

This lesson is entirely Florida law. The comparison to the majority rule: Florida now follows the majority on modified comparative negligence, and remains in the minority of states with no joint and several liability at all. Its 2-year negligence period is on the short end nationally.

Key sections, all in Florida Statutes: § 768.81 (comparative fault); § 95.11(4)(a) (verify) and § 95.031 (limitation and accrual); § 768.0427 (medical damages evidence); §§ 768.0701 and 768.0706 (negligent security); § 624.155 (bad faith); § 86.121 and § 57.104 (fees); § 768.72 (punitive damages, unchanged by HB 837).

## Angles checklist

- When did the injury occur? Before or after March 24, 2023?
- Which limitation period applies, and how much time is left?
- What is the plaintiff's own share of fault, and can it be pushed over or kept under 50%?
- Who else contributed, including non-parties and criminals, and can they be pleaded as Fabre defendants?
- Is this an intentional tort, where apportionment does not apply?
- Is it medical negligence, where the 50% bar does not apply?
- What are the paid medical amounts, as opposed to billed? Any letters of protection?
- Is there a liability policy, and has the claimant made a demand that starts the 90-day bad faith clock?
- Is a negligent security claim subject to § 768.0706's presumption?
- Flip it: as a defendant, has your insurer been notified so that it can evaluate a tender within 90 days?

## Drill

A Jacksonville warehouse's forklift operator strikes a visiting vendor's representative on March 10, 2023. The representative was walking in a marked no-pedestrian zone while looking at his phone. He has $150,000 (verify) in billed medical charges, of which his health insurer paid $52,000. He sues the warehouse on March 1, 2027. The warehouse's insurer received a demand for the $1,000,000 policy limits with full medical records on June 1, 2025 and did not respond. Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Accrual and limitation.** The injury occurred March 10, 2023, before the effective date. The claim accrued then. The 4-year period applies, so suit by March 10, 2027 is timely. Filing on March 1, 2027 works.
- **Comparative fault.** Because the claim accrued before March 24, 2023, pure comparative negligence applies. Even if the jury finds the representative 60% at fault for walking in a no-pedestrian zone while distracted, he recovers 40% of his damages. Had the accident happened two weeks later, the same finding would bar him entirely.
- **Fabre.** The warehouse may plead the vendor employer as a non-party if it failed to train or supervise the representative, shifting some fault away from the warehouse.
- **Medical damages.** Section 768.0427 is an evidence rule. Whether it applies to a pre-act claim tried after the act depends on whether the court treats it as procedural, applying to pending cases, or substantive. Courts have split (verify). If it applies, the jury hears $52,000 paid plus unpaid amounts measured under the statute, not $150,000 billed.
- **Bad faith.** The insurer received a demand with supporting records and did not tender within 90 days. Whether the amended § 624.155 or prior law applies to a pre-act claim must be verified, but either way, silence on a clear-liability demand is the conduct that exposes an insurer to an excess judgment.
- **Likely outcome.** The representative recovers a reduced but substantial award. The warehouse's liability is likely covered, and the insurer's handling may expose it beyond limits.
- **Facts that would change it.** Move the accident to April 2023: 2-year limitation, suit in 2027 is barred; even if timely, 60% fault bars recovery. Move the demand to after a prompt tender: no bad faith exposure.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A plaintiff injured in a January 2024 car crash is found 50% at fault. Does she recover?
2. A battery victim is found 70% at fault for starting the fight. Does § 768.81(6) bar her claim against the person who struck her?
3. A plaintiff's doctor billed $40,000 for treatment under a letter of protection and has not been paid. What does the jury hear?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Yes. The bar applies to a plaintiff "more than 50 percent" at fault. At exactly 50%, she recovers half of her damages.
2. No. Section 768.81(4) excludes intentional torts from comparative fault apportionment. The defendant who committed the battery is liable for the full damages, subject to any affirmative defense such as self-defense.
3. Under § 768.0427, the amount admissible for unpaid charges depends on whether the plaintiff has health insurance and, if not, is tied to the statutory Medicare-based benchmark rather than the billed $40,000. The jury also learns that treatment was under a letter of protection and whether the plaintiff's lawyer made the referral. Verify the current percentages.

</details>
