# Beal Bank, SSB v. Almand & Associates, 780 So. 2d 45 (Fla. 2001)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · asset protection · tenancy by the entireties · creditors · bank accounts · collecting a judgment
**Used in:** [`08-disputes-procedure/07-asset-protection-basics.md`](../08-disputes-procedure/07-asset-protection-basics.md), [`08-disputes-procedure/06-collecting-a-judgment.md`](../08-disputes-procedure/06-collecting-a-judgment.md), [`02-business-entities/05-limited-liability-and-veil-piercing.md`](../02-business-entities/05-limited-liability-and-veil-piercing.md)

## One-line takeaway

In Florida, personal property, including a bank account, titled in the names of both spouses is presumed to be held as tenants by the entireties, and so immune from the creditors of one spouse alone, unless the account documents expressly disclaim that form of ownership.

## Facts

Beal Bank held judgments against Almand & Associates and against several individuals who had guaranteed its debts. The individual debtors were married men. The bank sought to collect by garnishing bank accounts that each debtor held jointly with his wife. The wives were not debtors. The debtors argued the accounts were held as tenants by the entireties. The account signature cards showed joint ownership with right of survivorship but did not use the words "tenancy by the entireties."

## Procedural posture

The trial court ruled for the bank, holding that the debtors had not proved the accounts were entireties property. The Fifth District Court of Appeal affirmed, following cases that required a debtor to prove the spouses specifically intended to create a tenancy by the entireties, but certified a question of great public importance about the burden of proof. The Florida Supreme Court accepted jurisdiction.

## Issue

When spouses hold a bank account jointly, who bears the burden of proving whether it is held as tenants by the entireties, and what showing is required?

## Holding

The court held that a presumption of tenancy by the entireties arises when a married couple holds personal property, including a bank account, jointly, as long as the six unities are present and the account documents do not expressly disclaim entireties ownership. The presumption shifts the burden to the creditor to prove by a preponderance of the evidence that the spouses did not intend entireties ownership. The court quashed the Fifth District's decision.

## Reasoning

Justice Pariente began with the nature of the tenancy. It requires six unities: unity of possession (joint ownership and control), interest (identical interests), title (acquired in the same instrument), time (acquired at the same time), survivorship, and marriage. When all are present, the property belongs to the marital unit and a creditor of one spouse cannot reach it.

For real property, Florida had long presumed that a deed to a husband and wife created a tenancy by the entireties. For personal property, the cases had required the debtor to prove specific intent, with inconsistent results. The court saw no reason for the distinction. Spouses rarely know the legal label for how they hold an account, and banks rarely offer the choice in those words. Requiring proof of a specific intent that few people form meant the result turned on happenstance.

The court therefore extended the real property presumption to personal property. For bank accounts, if the signature card does not expressly disclaim entireties ownership and the account was opened by both spouses with the unities present, the account is presumed entireties property. The creditor can rebut the presumption by showing, for example, that the spouses treated the funds as separately owned or that the bank offered an entireties option that the spouses declined. The court said that as between the spouses and the bank itself, the account agreement controls.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Chief Justice Wells wrote separately, concurring in part and dissenting in part (verify). The concern was that a court-created presumption on a question with large consequences for creditors was a legislative task, and that the rule lets debtors shelter assets without any evidence of intent. The majority's answer was that the Legislature was free to change the rule. It did the opposite. Section 655.79, Fla. Stat., now codifies the presumption for deposit accounts (verify current text).

## Why it matters for a Florida business

For a business owner, this case cuts two ways. As a debtor, a married owner's jointly held accounts and property are substantially protected from a judgment against the owner alone, including a judgment on a personal guaranty, so long as the spouse is not also liable. That is why lenders ask both spouses to sign guaranties. As a creditor, a business trying to collect from a married individual must expect the entireties defense and must look for evidence rebutting it, such as a disclaimer on the signature card, a guaranty signed by both spouses, or a transfer into the joint account made to hinder creditors, which the fraudulent transfer act can unwind.

## Florida connection

This is Florida law and a core part of Florida's asset protection framework. Section 655.79(1), Fla. Stat., provides that a deposit account in the names of two persons who are married is considered held as tenants by the entireties unless otherwise specified in writing (verify text). Florida's homestead exemption, Fla. Const. art. X, § 4, and the head of family wage exemption, § 222.11, Fla. Stat., are the other main protections. The Uniform Fraudulent Transfer Act, ch. 726, Fla. Stat., lets a creditor reach property transferred into entireties form with intent to hinder creditors. Entireties protection ends on divorce or death of a spouse, and it does not protect against a joint creditor of both spouses or against federal tax liens.
