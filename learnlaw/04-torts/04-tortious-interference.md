# Tortious interference with contracts and business relationships

**Module:** 04 · **Lesson:** 4 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

A competitor hires away your key employee and your biggest customer follows. A former partner calls your clients and tells them you are going under. A supplier refuses to sell to you after a rival leans on it. Tortious interference is the claim that reaches a third party who deliberately disrupts your deals. It is also the claim competitors will throw at you when you win their customers. The doctrine draws a line between hard competition, which is lawful, and interference by improper means or without justification, which is not.

## The rule

Florida states the elements in *Tamiami Trail Tours, Inc. v. Cotton*, 463 So. 2d 1126 (Fla. 1985):

1. The existence of a business relationship, not necessarily evidenced by an enforceable contract, under which the plaintiff has legal rights;
2. The defendant's knowledge of the relationship;
3. An intentional and unjustified interference with the relationship by the defendant; and
4. Damage to the plaintiff as a result.

Where the relationship is an existing contract, the claim is interference with contract. Where it is an expectancy, such as a customer who regularly buys or a deal under negotiation, the claim is interference with an advantageous business relationship. The expectancy claim requires more: an identifiable relationship with a specific person, and an understanding between the parties that would have been completed had the defendant not interfered.

**Justification and privilege.** Interference is not actionable if it was justified. The Restatement (Second) of Torts § 767 lists factors: the nature of the defendant's conduct, its motive, the interests it sought to advance, the social interests in protecting those interests, the proximity of the conduct to the interference, and the relations between the parties. Section 768 states the **competition privilege**: one who causes a third person not to enter into or continue a prospective relationship with a competitor does not interfere improperly if the relationship concerns competition, the defendant does not use wrongful means, the action does not create an unlawful restraint of trade, and the purpose is at least in part to advance the defendant's competitive interest. The competition privilege does not protect inducing breach of an existing contract that is not terminable at will.

**Parties and their agents.** A party to a contract cannot tortiously interfere with its own contract; its remedy exposure is for breach. An officer, employee, or agent of a party acting within the scope of the agency is treated as the party and is privileged, unless the agent acted solely for personal gain or with malice unrelated to the principal's interest (verify Florida formulation).

**Remedies.** Compensatory damages including lost profits, punitive damages on the § 768.72 showing, and injunctive relief in some cases. Limitation: 4 years (verify), § 95.11(3).

The plaintiff bears the burden on the elements. Florida courts treat justification as something the plaintiff must negate as part of the "unjustified" element, though defendants plead privilege affirmatively in practice (verify).

## How courts reason about it

The hard cases are at two edges: how specific the relationship must be, and when competition becomes interference.

**Specificity.** *Ethan Allen, Inc. v. Georgetown Manor, Inc.*, 647 So. 2d 812 (Fla. 1994), is the Florida anchor. A furniture retailer lost its Ethan Allen dealership and claimed Ethan Allen interfered with its relationships with 89,000 past customers by announcing the end of the dealership. The Florida Supreme Court held that a relationship with the community at large, or with past customers who might return, is not a protected business relationship. The plaintiff must show an actual, identifiable understanding with specific persons that probably would have produced business. The policy is that businesses cannot own their customers. Without the limit, every lost sale would be a tort.

**Competition versus interference.** Courts ask what the defendant did and why. Offering better prices, better service, or a job to a competitor's at-will employee is competition. Lying to the customer about the competitor, threatening the customer, inducing breach of a non-terminable contract, or using stolen confidential information is interference by improper means. Motive matters too. A defendant who acted at least partly to advance its own business is more likely privileged than one who acted purely to injure.

**At-will relationships.** A contract terminable at will is still a relationship with legal rights, so inducing a customer or employee to leave can be interference. But the competition privilege applies with full force to at-will relationships, so the plaintiff must show improper means or improper motive.

**Who is a stranger.** Courts require that the defendant be a third party to the relationship. A franchisor that terminates a franchise, a lender that calls a loan, or a landlord that refuses to renew is exercising its own contractual rights, not interfering. Florida courts also protect a defendant that has a financial or supervisory interest in the relationship, for example a parent company directing a subsidiary, as long as it does not act with improper means (verify scope).

**Damages.** The plaintiff must prove the lost business was probable, not merely possible. Speculative claims of lost future customers fail for the same reason *Ethan Allen* failed on the relationship element.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule as expressed in the Restatement, and its Supreme Court has adopted the *Tamiami* elements and the *Ethan Allen* specificity requirement.

- The relationship need not be an enforceable contract. *Tamiami Trail Tours*.
- Past customers at large are not a protected relationship. *Ethan Allen*.
- The competition privilege under Restatement § 768 is applied by Florida courts, and Florida treats justification as part of the "unjustified interference" element.
- An agent of a party is privileged unless acting solely for personal interest or with malice (verify).
- Florida recognizes a qualified privilege for a defendant that interferes to protect its own legitimate financial interest in the relationship, and the privilege is lost if the defendant used improper means.
- **Interplay with restrictive covenants.** A competitor who hires an employee bound by a valid non-compete, knowing of it, can face an interference claim alongside the employee's breach claim. Section 542.335 is the frame for whether the covenant was enforceable. Module 05, lesson 6.
- **Interplay with trade secrets.** Interference accomplished by misappropriating trade secrets is improper means, and ch. 688 preempts other tort claims based solely on the misappropriation (verify § 688.008 scope).
- Limitation period: 4 years (verify), § 95.11(3).

## Angles checklist

- What exactly was the relationship: a contract, a pending deal, a regular customer? With whom, by name?
- Was the contract terminable at will?
- Did the defendant know of the relationship? How?
- What did the defendant actually do? Better offer, or lies, threats, or stolen information?
- What was the defendant's motive: its own business, or harming the plaintiff?
- Is the defendant a stranger to the relationship, or a party, an agent, or someone with its own interest in it?
- What business was actually lost, and can that be proven as probable?
- Is there a non-compete, NDA, or trade secret claim that frames the "improper means" question?
- Flip it: if you are the new employer or the winning competitor, what did your people say to the customer?

## Drill

A Tallahassee IT consulting firm's senior account manager, with no non-compete, leaves to join a competitor. In her last two weeks she downloads the firm's client list and pricing sheets. At the competitor, she emails the firm's ten largest clients, says the firm "is losing its best people and cannot support you anymore," and offers a 15% discount. Four clients, two of whom had annual contracts with 60-day termination rights, switch. Analyze the firm's claims.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Relationships.** Ten specific, identifiable clients, some under contract. This satisfies *Ethan Allen*. The two annual contracts are interference with contract; the others are interference with advantageous relationships.
- **Knowledge.** The manager obviously knew. Her knowledge is imputed to the competitor as her employer acting within the scope of her job.
- **Intentional and unjustified.** The competition privilege would cover a plain solicitation with a better price, even for at-will customers. It does not cover improper means. Two problems for the competitor: the false statement that the firm "cannot support you," which is a misrepresentation and possibly defamation, and the use of the downloaded client list and pricing, which is likely trade secret misappropriation under ch. 688. Either defeats the privilege.
- **Contract clients.** The 60-day termination right means the clients did not breach by leaving, but inducing termination through improper means is still actionable interference.
- **Damages.** Lost profits on four accounts, provable from the firm's records. Punitive damages possible given the deliberate falsehood.
- **Other claims.** Misappropriation of trade secrets against the manager and the competitor, which may preempt the interference claim to the extent it rests solely on the misappropriation. Breach of the duty of loyalty by the manager for downloading while still employed. Defamation or injurious falsehood for the "cannot support you" statement. FDUTPA against the competitor.
- **Likely outcome.** Strong claims against both the manager and the competitor. The competitor's defense is that it did not know about the downloaded materials; discovery into its onboarding will decide that.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the manager had used only her memory and said only "I have moved and would love to keep working with you," the competition privilege likely applies and the firm has no interference claim. A valid non-solicitation covenant would add a breach claim and make the competitor's knowing use of her an independent ground for interference.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A competitor offers your at-will customers a lower price and they switch. Tortious interference?
2. Your landlord refuses to renew your lease so it can lease to a competitor who will pay more. Interference?
3. A supplier's regional manager persuades the supplier to drop you as a distributor because he dislikes you personally. Can you sue the manager?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No, if the competitor used lawful means and acted to advance its own business. That is the competition privilege under Restatement § 768. It becomes interference if the competitor lied about you, used your confidential pricing, or induced breach of a contract that was not terminable at will.
2. No. The landlord is a party to the lease exercising its own rights at the end of the term. A party cannot interfere with its own contract, and declining to renew is not an unjustified act toward your relationship with anyone.
3. Possibly. An agent acting within the scope of employment is ordinarily privileged, but Florida case law removes the privilege where the agent acted solely out of personal animus or for personal gain rather than the principal's interest (verify formulation). You would need evidence that the decision served no business purpose of the supplier.

</details>
