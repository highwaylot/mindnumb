# Performance, conditions, and breach

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 5 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

The contract is signed. Now someone does not do what they promised, or does it late, or does most of it. Can you stop paying? Can you walk away? Get this wrong and the party who was wronged becomes the party in breach. The rules on conditions, substantial performance, material breach, and repudiation separate a justified refusal from an expensive mistake.

## The rule

**Breach** is a failure to perform a contractual duty when performance is due. Every breach gives the other side a damages claim. Only a **material** breach lets the other side suspend its own performance and, if the breach is not cured, terminate the contract.

**Conditions.** A condition is an event that must occur before a duty to perform arises. Express conditions use words like "if," "provided that," "on condition that," or "subject to." They are enforced strictly: close is not good enough. Constructive conditions are implied by courts to order the parties' performances. In a typical service contract, performance by the service provider is a constructive condition of the customer's duty to pay. Constructive conditions are satisfied by **substantial performance**.

**Substantial performance.** A party who has performed in all important respects, with only minor and unintentional deviations, has satisfied a constructive condition and is entitled to the contract price, less the other side's damages for the deviation. Willful or significant deviations do not qualify.

**Material breach.** Whether a breach is material turns on the factors in Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 241: how much the injured party is deprived of the benefit it expected; whether damages can adequately compensate; how much the breaching party will forfeit; the likelihood of cure; and whether the breach was in bad faith.

**Anticipatory repudiation.** A clear, unequivocal statement or act before performance is due that a party will not perform is a repudiation. The other side may treat it as an immediate breach and sue, or wait a reasonable time. A party with reasonable grounds for insecurity may demand adequate assurance in writing. For goods, § 672.609, Fla. Stat., codifies this. Failure to give assurance within a reasonable time, up to 30 days (verify), is a repudiation.

**The implied covenant of good faith and fair dealing.** Every contract carries an implied duty not to do anything that destroys the other party's right to receive the fruits of the bargain. It governs discretion the contract gives a party. It does not create new duties or override express terms.

The party suing for breach must prove its own performance, or that its performance was excused, as well as the other side's breach.

## How courts reason about it

Two cases set the tone and both are Cardozo's.

*Jacob & Youngs v. Kent*: the contract called for Reading pipe; the builder installed Cohoes pipe of equal quality. The owner refused to pay and demanded the walls be torn out. Cardozo held the builder had substantially performed and the owner's remedy was the difference in value, which was nothing. Courts will not impose forfeiture for a trivial deviation unless the contract makes the term an express condition. If the owner wanted Reading pipe or nothing, the contract had to say so.

*Wood v. Lucy, Lady Duff-Gordon*: an exclusive marketing agent made no express promise to do anything. Cardozo read in a promise to use reasonable efforts, because otherwise the exclusive grant made no sense. This is the root of the implied covenant of good faith and of "reasonable efforts" standards.

The hard cases involve timing and proportion.

- **Is it a condition or a promise?** Courts prefer to read doubtful language as a promise, because a promise gives damages while a condition gives forfeiture. Florida's *Peacock Construction Co. v. Modern Air Conditioning, Inc.*, 353 So. 2d 840 (Fla. 1977), is the classic example. A contractor's promise to pay a subcontractor "within 30 days after completion and payment by the owner" was read as setting a time for payment, not as making owner payment a condition of the sub ever being paid. If a contractor wants to shift the risk of owner non-payment to the sub, the clause must say so unambiguously.
- **Is the breach material?** A late delivery is not material unless time is of the essence or the delay defeats the purpose. A pattern of small breaches can become material. Courts watch for a party using a minor breach as an excuse to exit a deal it regrets.
- **Who breached first?** Under the prior breach doctrine, a party who committed the first material breach cannot enforce the contract against the other. But a party who treats a non-material breach as grounds to stop performing has itself committed the first material breach. This trap catches businesses that stop paying over a dispute about quality.
- **Was there a repudiation?** Expressions of doubt, requests to renegotiate, and statements that performance will be difficult are not repudiations. "We will not deliver" is.
- **Good faith.** Courts use the covenant to police discretion: a party with a right to approve, to set a price, or to terminate "for any reason" must not use it to deprive the other side of the bargain's core. But they refuse to use it to add duties the parties did not agree to.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on substantial performance, material breach, the § 241 factors, anticipatory repudiation, and the implied covenant.

- **The implied covenant cannot override express terms.** Florida courts hold the covenant attaches only to the performance of an express term and cannot be used to vary unambiguous language. *Insurance Concepts & Design, Inc. v. Healthplan Services, Inc.*, 785 So. 2d 1232 (Fla. 4th DCA 2001), is widely cited. A party exercising an express right to terminate on notice does not breach the covenant by doing so. The Eleventh Circuit applied the same rule in *Centurion Air Cargo, Inc. v. United Parcel Service Co.*, 420 F.3d 1146 (11th Cir. 2005).
- **Pay-when-paid clauses.** *Peacock* holds that a payment clause tied to owner payment is presumed to set timing, not a condition, unless the contract clearly shifts the risk. Construction contracts in Florida now use explicit "pay-if-paid" language to do that, and courts enforce it when it is unambiguous.
- **Time is of the essence** must be stated or clear from the circumstances. Without it, a reasonable delay is not a material breach.
- **Prior breach doctrine.** Florida courts routinely hold that a party who materially breaches first cannot recover for the other party's subsequent non-performance.

## Angles checklist

- What exactly did each side promise, and when was each performance due?
- Is the disputed term a condition or a promise? What words did the contract use?
- Has the other side substantially performed? What is the gap, and was it willful?
- Is the breach material under the § 241 factors? Can it be cured?
- Did anyone say or do something amounting to an unequivocal refusal?
- Who breached first? If you stop performing, are you the one in material breach?
- Is there a notice-and-cure clause? Did anyone follow it?
- Is the other side using discretion the contract gave it in a way that guts the deal?
- What damages flow, and is termination worth more than damages? See lesson 7.

## Drill

A Pensacola software firm agrees to build a custom inventory system for a wholesaler for $120,000 (verify), payable in three installments, final delivery June 30. The contract says "time is of the essence" and includes a 15-day cure period after written notice of any breach. The firm delivers on July 10 with two reporting features missing. The wholesaler uses the system, refuses to pay the final $40,000, and hires another vendor to build the missing features for $15,000. The firm sues. Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Late delivery.** Time was of the essence, so the ten-day delay is a breach that could be material. But the wholesaler accepted and used the system, which waives the right to terminate for lateness. It keeps a damages claim for delay losses if it can prove them.
- **Missing features.** Were they a small part of the whole? Two reports in a full inventory system looks like substantial performance under *Jacob & Youngs*, unless the reports were the point of the project. The remedy is the price less the cost to complete.
- **Cure period.** The contract required written notice and 15 days to cure. The wholesaler gave none and hired another vendor. Failing to follow a cure clause before terminating is itself a breach. This weakens the wholesaler badly.
- **Prior breach.** The firm breached first, by lateness and incomplete delivery. But if those breaches were not material, the wholesaler's refusal to pay $40,000 over a $15,000 gap is the first material breach.
- **Likely outcome.** The firm recovers $40,000 less $15,000 for completion and any provable delay damages. Attorney fees depend on the contract's fee clause and § 57.105(7) reciprocity.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the missing reports were the core deliverable, the breach is material and the wholesaler could have terminated, after giving notice and 15 days. If the wholesaler had given notice and the firm refused to cure, the wholesaler is on much stronger ground.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A supplier emails, "Given the price of steel, we are going to have real trouble delivering in March." Repudiation?
2. A franchise agreement lets the franchisor terminate "at its sole discretion on 30 days notice." The franchisor terminates to take over a profitable territory itself. Breach of the implied covenant in Florida?
3. A contractor finishes a kitchen with cabinets one shade different from the sample. The owner refuses to pay the final 20%. Who is in breach?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. It is an expression of difficulty, not an unequivocal refusal. The buyer can demand adequate assurance under § 672.609. If none comes within a reasonable time, that becomes a repudiation.
2. Probably not. Florida does not let the implied covenant override an express right. The franchisor exercised a bargained-for term. The result could differ if the franchisor's conduct breached some other express term or the agreement limited the grounds for termination.
3. Likely the owner. A minor cosmetic deviation is substantial performance. The owner owes the balance less the difference in value or a reasonable cost to correct, not a 20% holdback. If the contract made the exact cabinet an express condition, the result could change.

</details>
