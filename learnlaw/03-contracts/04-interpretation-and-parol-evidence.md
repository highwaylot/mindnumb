# Interpretation and the parol evidence rule

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 4 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Most contract fights are not about whether there was a deal. They are about what the deal means. A clause reads one way to you and another way to the customer. A salesperson promised something the signed order form does not mention. The rules of interpretation decide who wins those arguments, and the parol evidence rule decides whether the promise outside the paper even gets into evidence. Knowing them tells you what to put in the document and what to stop relying on.

## The rule

**Interpretation** asks what the words mean. Courts apply these principles roughly in this order.

1. **Plain meaning.** If the language is clear and unambiguous, the court enforces it as written. The court does not rewrite a bad bargain.
2. **The whole contract.** Every clause is read in light of the others. A reading that makes a clause meaningless is disfavored.
3. **Specific over general, negotiated over boilerplate.** A specific term controls a general one on the same subject. Handwritten or typed terms control printed ones.
4. **Ambiguity, then extrinsic evidence.** If a term is reasonably open to more than one meaning, the court looks outside the document: negotiations, the parties' course of performance under this contract, their course of dealing under past contracts, and trade usage.
5. **Contra proferentem.** If ambiguity remains, the term is read against the party who drafted it.

Whether a term is ambiguous is a question of law for the judge. What an ambiguous term means is a question of fact.

**The parol evidence rule** is a rule about which agreements count, not about which words mean what. When the parties have put their agreement in a final written form, evidence of earlier or contemporaneous agreements cannot be used to contradict it. If the writing is a complete integration, evidence of additional consistent terms is also barred. If it is a partial integration, consistent additional terms can be shown.

The rule does not bar evidence offered to:

- show the contract was never formed, or is void or voidable for fraud, duress, mistake, or illegality;
- explain an ambiguity;
- prove a condition precedent to the contract taking effect;
- prove a later modification;
- establish course of dealing or trade usage, which the UCC admits even against unambiguous terms unless carefully negated. See § 672.202, Fla. Stat.

A **merger clause** ("this is the entire agreement") is strong evidence that the writing is a complete integration.

The party relying on the writing's plain meaning has the easier burden. The party seeking to vary it must first show ambiguity or an exception.

## How courts reason about it

The fight is usually about whether a term is ambiguous, because that is the gate. Once the judge finds ambiguity, the case goes to a jury with all the emails and testimony. If the judge finds the term clear, summary judgment follows. Lawyers therefore argue hard at the gate.

Courts differ in how willing they are to find ambiguity. The strict view, sometimes called the "four corners" approach, allows extrinsic evidence only if ambiguity appears on the face of the document. The more liberal view, associated with Judge Traynor in California, lets a party offer extrinsic evidence to show that language that looks clear is actually reasonably susceptible to another meaning. Most states, including Florida, are closer to the strict view but will admit evidence to reveal a latent ambiguity, where words that seem clear turn out to fit two different things in the world. *Raffles v. Wichelhaus* is the classic. "Ex Peerless" was clear on paper. There were two ships named Peerless.

Courts return to a few concerns.

- **Reliability.** The signed writing was produced carefully, usually with both sides' review. Testimony about what was said in a meeting a year ago is less reliable and easy to fabricate. The parol evidence rule favors the paper.
- **Who drafted.** Contra proferentem is a tiebreaker, but courts apply it with real force against form contracts imposed on a party with no chance to negotiate. Insurers, franchisors, and software vendors get this treatment often. Between sophisticated parties who both had counsel, courts often decline to apply it.
- **Commercial sense.** Courts prefer a reading that makes commercial sense over one that produces an absurd result, but they will enforce a harsh result if the words are clear.
- **Fraud.** A merger clause does not bar a claim that a party was induced to sign by a lie. But some courts hold that if the writing expressly contradicts the oral representation, reliance on the oral statement was unreasonable. Florida's DCAs are not uniform on this (verify current state of the split).

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on plain meaning, whole-contract reading, contra proferentem, and the parol evidence rule.

- Florida courts state that where contract language is clear, the court cannot consider extrinsic evidence or the parties' intent, and must enforce the words as written. Ambiguity cannot be created by one party's assertion that it meant something else.
- Florida recognizes both patent ambiguity, visible on the face, and latent ambiguity, revealed by extrinsic facts. Extrinsic evidence is admissible to expose and resolve a latent ambiguity.
- **Fraud in the inducement survives a merger clause.** *Oceanic Villas, Inc. v. Godson*, 148 Fla. 454, 4 So. 2d 689 (1941), held that a party cannot contract away liability for its own fraud by a general merger clause. Later DCA cases limit this where the contract specifically disclaims the very representation at issue. Treat this as contested.
- **Course of dealing and trade usage** in sales of goods come in under §§ 671.303 and 672.202 (verify section numbering for Florida's revised Article 1).
- **Construction against the drafter** is applied firmly to insurance policies and to form contracts, and more gently between negotiating businesses.
- Florida courts treat "time is of the essence" as meaning what it says, and read dates without that language as allowing a reasonable time.
- A clause saying "modifications must be in writing" can be waived by a course of conduct in Florida (verify). Do not assume the clause protects you if you have been accepting oral changes.

## Angles checklist

- Is the disputed term clear on its face, or reasonably open to two readings?
- Read the whole document. Does another clause resolve it? Is there a definitions section?
- Who drafted it? Was it negotiated or a form?
- Is there a merger clause? Is the writing complete, or does it obviously leave things out?
- What is the outside evidence: emails, prior deals, industry custom, how the parties actually performed?
- Is the outside evidence offered to contradict the writing, or to explain it, or to prove fraud or a condition?
- Did the parties modify the deal by conduct after signing?
- Flip it: can the other side show a latent ambiguity you did not see? Did your sales team say things the contract denies?

## Drill

A Sarasota landscaping company signs a one-year maintenance agreement with an HOA for "weekly service of all common areas" at a fixed monthly price. The agreement has a merger clause. Before signing, the HOA manager emailed asking whether "common areas" included the retention pond banks, and the landscaper's owner replied "yes, we handle the ponds too." The signed contract does not mention ponds. Six months in, the landscaper stops cutting the pond banks and says they were never included. Who is right?

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Is "common areas" ambiguous?** Possibly. The term is undefined. The HOA's governing documents may define common areas to include the ponds, which would supply a plain meaning from a source the contract implicitly references. If the term has a clear meaning in HOA practice, that trade usage comes in too.
- **Does the parol evidence rule bar the email?** The email is a prior agreement about a term, and the merger clause signals complete integration. If "common areas" is unambiguous and excludes ponds, the email is barred as contradicting the writing. If the term is ambiguous, the email is admissible to explain what the parties meant by it. This is the whole case.
- **Course of performance.** The landscaper cut the ponds for six months. Under the whole-contract and course-of-performance principles, that conduct is strong evidence of what both sides understood "common areas" to mean. Florida courts admit course of performance to resolve ambiguity.
- **Contra proferentem.** If the landscaper drafted the form, the ambiguity is read against it.
- **Likely outcome.** The HOA probably wins. "Common areas" is undefined and reasonably includes the ponds; the email and six months of performance resolve the ambiguity in the HOA's favor; and the drafter loses ties.
- **Facts that would change it.** A definition in the contract excluding water features would be clear and the email would be barred. If the HOA drafted the agreement, contra proferentem flips. If the landscaper never cut the ponds from day one, course of performance would cut the other way.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A contract says disputes go to "the courts of Orange County." The buyer argues this means state courts only; the seller says the federal court in Orlando also qualifies. How does a court approach this?
2. A supplier's signed quote says "net 30." The buyer wants to testify that the salesperson agreed orally to net 60 the day before. Admissible?
3. Why does a merger clause not protect against a fraud claim in Florida?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. First, plain meaning and whole-contract reading. "Courts of" a county, versus "courts in" a county, has been read by many courts as meaning state courts only, since a federal court is "in" the county but not "of" it. If the court finds the phrase ambiguous, it looks to extrinsic evidence and then construes against the drafter.
2. Probably not. The quote is a final writing on payment terms, and "net 60" contradicts "net 30." Unless the buyer can show the quote was not meant as final, or a later modification, the parol evidence rule bars the testimony. For goods, course of dealing could be offered if the parties had always worked on net 60 before, but it cannot contradict an express term.
3. Because fraud goes to whether the contract was validly formed, and *Oceanic Villas* holds a party cannot immunize its own fraud by boilerplate. The exception narrows where the contract specifically contradicts the alleged misrepresentation, since reliance then looks unreasonable.

</details>
