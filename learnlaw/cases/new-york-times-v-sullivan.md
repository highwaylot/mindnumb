# New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964)

**Tags:** `[US]` · torts · defamation · First Amendment · actual malice · public officials
**Used in:** [`04-torts/05-defamation-and-business-reputation.md`](../04-torts/05-defamation-and-business-reputation.md), [`08-disputes-procedure/03-pre-suit-demand-letters-and-notice.md`](../08-disputes-procedure/03-pre-suit-demand-letters-and-notice.md)

## One-line takeaway

A public official cannot recover for defamation relating to official conduct without proving, by clear and convincing evidence, that the statement was made with actual malice, meaning knowledge of its falsity or reckless disregard of whether it was false.

## Facts

On March 29, 1960 the New York Times ran a full-page advertisement titled "Heed Their Rising Voices," placed by supporters of Martin Luther King Jr. It described a "wave of terror" against civil rights protesters in the South and criticized police conduct in Montgomery, Alabama. Some statements were wrong. They misstated the song the students sang, whether police had "ringed" the campus, and how many times King had been arrested.

L.B. Sullivan was the Montgomery city commissioner who supervised the police. The advertisement did not name him. He sued the Times and four Black Alabama ministers whose names appeared in the advertisement.

## Procedural posture

Sullivan sued for libel in Alabama state court. Under Alabama law the statements were libelous per se, falsity and malice were presumed, and the Times could avoid liability only by proving every statement true. The jury awarded $500,000. The Alabama Supreme Court affirmed. The United States Supreme Court granted certiorari and reversed.

## Issue

Does the First Amendment limit a state's power to award damages to a public official for a defamatory falsehood about his official conduct?

## Holding

Yes. Justice Brennan, for a unanimous Court, held that a public official may not recover damages for a defamatory falsehood relating to official conduct unless he proves the statement was made with "actual malice," that is, with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false or not. The proof must be of convincing clarity. Applying that standard to the record, the Court found the evidence insufficient as a matter of law.

## Reasoning

The Court began from "a profound national commitment to the principle that debate on public issues should be uninhibited, robust, and wide-open," including sharp attacks on government and public officials. Erroneous statement is inevitable in free debate and must be protected if free expression is to have the breathing space it needs. A rule that made a critic prove the truth of every factual assertion would lead to self-censorship.

The Court drew on the Sedition Act of 1798, judged unconstitutional in the court of history, and said a $500,000 libel judgment could do the same work of suppression.

Two further points. First, the Court held that it must independently review the record to make sure the constitutional standard was applied, rather than deferring to the jury. Second, it held that the advertisement's criticism of the police could not be turned into a personal attack on Sullivan merely because he supervised them.

The Times had not checked the advertisement against its own files. The Court held that failure to check was at most negligence, not reckless disregard.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. Justices Black, Douglas, and Goldberg concurred separately. They would have gone further and held that the First Amendment gives an absolute privilege to criticize the official conduct of public officials.

The later cases built the framework. *Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts*, 388 U.S. 130 (1967), extended actual malice to public figures. *Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc.*, 418 U.S. 323 (1974), held that a private figure need only prove fault, which states may set at negligence, but may not recover presumed or punitive damages without actual malice.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

A business is almost always a private figure, which means the standard for a defamation claim by or against it is lower than actual malice. But the framework still governs. Reviews, social media posts, and news coverage about a business's dealings with the public are often matters of public concern, so the business must prove falsity and at least negligence. If the business or its owner has become a limited public figure by stepping into a public controversy, actual malice applies. The same framework protects a business owner who criticizes a competitor or an official: false statements of fact remain actionable, opinion and true statements do not.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the federal framework, as it must. Florida states the elements of defamation as publication, falsity, fault amounting to at least negligence for a private person and actual malice for a public official or public figure, actual damages, and a defamatory statement. *Jews for Jesus, Inc. v. Rapp*, 997 So. 2d 1098 (Fla. 2008). Florida adds procedure. Section 770.01, Fla. Stat. requires a plaintiff, at least five days before suing a newspaper, periodical, or broadcast station over a publication or broadcast, to serve written notice specifying the statements alleged to be false and defamatory. The notice requirement applies to media defendants; Florida courts have divided over how far it reaches non-media speakers online (verify). Section 770.02 limits damages to actual damages when a timely retraction is published. The limitation period for defamation is two years. § 95.11(4), Fla. Stat. (verify subsection). Florida's anti-SLAPP statute, § 768.295, Fla. Stat., allows early dismissal and fees for suits brought primarily to chill free speech on public issues.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Does the outcome change? Suppose a Times employee had pulled the clippings, seen the errors, and run the advertisement anyway. Actual malice?
2. What would the losing party have needed to show? What evidence of the Times's state of mind would have satisfied the clear and convincing standard?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? A customer posts a one-star review with a false factual claim that your shop uses counterfeit parts. What must you prove, and what must you do before suing if the post was republished by a local news site?
