# Hadley v. Baxendale, 9 Exch. 341, 156 Eng. Rep. 145 (Court of Exchequer, England 1854)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · damages · consequential damages · foreseeability · limitation of liability
**Used in:** [03-contracts/07-remedies.md](../03-contracts/07-remedies.md), [03-contracts/08-ucc-sales-of-goods.md](../03-contracts/08-ucc-sales-of-goods.md), [03-contracts/10-common-business-clauses.md](../03-contracts/10-common-business-clauses.md)

## One-line takeaway

Damages for breach of contract are limited to losses that arise naturally from the breach or that both parties could reasonably have contemplated at the time of contracting as the probable result of a breach.

## Facts

The Hadleys ran a flour mill in Gloucester. The crankshaft of their steam engine broke, and the mill stopped. They needed to send the broken shaft to the engineers in Greenwich who would use it as a pattern for a new one. Their employee took it to the office of Pickford & Co., common carriers, of which Baxendale was the managing partner. The clerk said that if the shaft was delivered by noon it would be sent the next day. The Hadleys paid the freight. Through the carrier's neglect, delivery was delayed by several days. The mill stayed shut for those days and the Hadleys lost the profits they would have made.

The court's statement of facts said that the only circumstances communicated to the carrier were that the article was the broken shaft of a mill and that the plaintiffs were millers. The reporter's headnote says the clerk was told the mill was stopped, a discrepancy that commentators still debate.

## Procedural posture

The Hadleys sued the carrier for breach of contract and claimed lost profits. The trial judge left the question of damages to the jury, which awarded £25 beyond an amount the defendants had paid into court. The defendants moved in the Court of Exchequer for a new trial on the ground that the jury should not have been allowed to consider lost profits.

## Issue

May a party recover lost profits caused by a breach of contract when the other party, at the time of contracting, had no notice of the special circumstances that made those profits depend on performance?

## Holding

No. A new trial was ordered. Damages for breach are those that arise naturally, in the usual course of things, from the breach itself, or those that may reasonably be supposed to have been in the contemplation of both parties at the time they made the contract as the probable result of a breach. The loss of the mill's profits did not arise in the usual course of things from a delayed shipment, and the special circumstances were not communicated.

## Reasoning

Baron Alderson wrote for the court. He set out the two-branch rule above and then explained why the mill's profits fell outside both branches. In the great multitude of cases of millers sending broken shafts to third parties, the consequences of delay would not be a stopped mill. The miller might have a spare shaft. The engine might have other defects. A carrier who knew only that it was carrying a mill shaft for a miller could not be taken to have contemplated that delay would stop the mill. Had the special circumstances been communicated, the parties could have contracted for that risk and the carrier could have charged for it or refused.

The policy underneath the rule is allocation of risk at the time of contracting. A party should be liable for the losses it could have foreseen and priced, not for losses that depended on facts it did not know.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The Hadleys' strongest argument was factual: if the clerk was told the mill was stopped, the carrier had notice, and the loss was within the second branch. The court's narrower version of the facts controlled.

American courts adopted the rule almost immediately because it answered a question every commercial contract raises: how far does liability extend. It is now the law of every state through the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 351 and, for goods, UCC § 2-715(2), which allows consequential damages for losses the seller "had reason to know" of at contracting.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

This rule is why contracts contain limitation-of-liability clauses excluding consequential damages, and why they are worth fighting over. If you tell a vendor at signing that a late delivery will shut your line down, you have put them on notice and expanded their exposure; they may price accordingly. If you say nothing, your lost profits are probably unrecoverable. On the other side, when a customer tells you how important your performance is to their business, you are on notice and should either price for the risk or limit it in writing.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the rule. Florida courts allow consequential damages, including lost profits, only where they were within the contemplation of the parties at the time of contracting and can be proved with reasonable certainty. See *Hardwick Properties, Inc. v. Newbern*, 711 So. 2d 35 (Fla. 1st DCA 1998) (verify citation). For sales of goods, § 672.715(2), Fla. Stat., codifies the "reason to know" test, and § 672.719 permits parties to limit or exclude consequential damages unless the limitation is unconscionable. Lost profits for a new business are hard to recover in Florida because of the certainty requirement, though the rule has softened from a flat bar to a question of proof. A clause excluding consequential damages is generally enforced in Florida between commercial parties.

2. What would the Hadleys have needed to show to recover under the first branch of the rule?
