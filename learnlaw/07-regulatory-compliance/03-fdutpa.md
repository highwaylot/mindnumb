# FDUTPA: the Florida Deceptive and Unfair Trade Practices Act

**Module:** 07 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[FL]` `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

FDUTPA gets added to almost every Florida business dispute. It needs no contract, no proof of intent, and no proof of reliance. It carries attorney fees for the prevailing party. A business can bring it against another business, and can be hit with it by a competitor, a customer, or the Attorney General. Its elements, its damages ceiling, and its fee rule show both the threat and its limits.

## The rule

Section 501.204(1) makes unlawful "unfair methods of competition, unconscionable acts or practices, and unfair or deceptive acts or practices in the conduct of any trade or commerce." Section 501.204(2) tells courts to give "due consideration and great weight" to federal interpretations of § 5 of the FTC Act.

**Deceptive.** A representation, omission, or practice that is likely to mislead a consumer acting reasonably in the circumstances, to the consumer's detriment. The standard is objective.

**Unfair.** A practice that offends established public policy, or is immoral, unethical, oppressive, unscrupulous, or substantially injurious to consumers.

**Per se violations.** Section 501.203(3) defines a "violation" to include any rule of the FTC or Attorney General, and any state or federal statute or rule "which proscribes unfair methods of competition, or unfair, deceptive, or unconscionable acts or practices." So a violation of the Florida Telephone Solicitation Act or an FTC rule is a FDUTPA violation without separate proof of deception.

**Private claim (§ 501.211(2)).** Elements: (1) a deceptive or unfair act or practice, (2) causation, and (3) actual damages. "Actual damages" means the difference between the market value of what was promised and what was delivered. Consequential damages, lost profits from the plaintiff's own business, and personal injury are not recoverable. Injunctive or declaratory relief is available to "anyone aggrieved" under § 501.211(1) without damages.

**Who can sue.** Since 2001 the statute says "person," not "consumer." After *PNR, Inc. v. Beacon Property Management, Inc.*, 842 So. 2d 773 (Fla. 2003), a business harmed by another business's unfair act in trade or commerce has standing, and a single act suffices.

**Fees (§ 501.2105).** The prevailing party, after judgment, may recover reasonable attorney fees and costs. The award is discretionary and cuts both ways: a defendant who wins can recover from the plaintiff.

**Public enforcement.** The Attorney General and state attorneys enforce under §§ 501.207 and 501.2075, with civil penalties up to $10,000 (verify) per willful violation, more for victims who are seniors or disabled (§ 501.2077).

**Exemptions (§ 501.212).** Acts required or specifically permitted by federal or state law; claims for personal injury or death; and persons regulated by the Office of Financial Regulation for conduct within that regulation.

**Limitation.** Four years (verify) under § 95.11(3), as a liability created by statute.

**Burden.** Plaintiff, by a preponderance, on each element. Defendant on exemptions and the limitation defense.

## How courts reason about it

FDUTPA is broad on liability and narrow on damages. Most litigation happens at the seams.

- **Deception versus breach of contract.** Courts say a simple breach of contract is not a FDUTPA violation. If the only complaint is that the defendant did not perform, there is no deception. A promise the defendant intended to keep and then broke is contract. A promise made with no intention to perform, or a material fact hidden in the sale, moves to FDUTPA.
- **Puffery.** General claims of quality, "the best service in Tampa," are not actionable. Specific factual claims are.
- **Reliance.** Florida courts hold that reliance is not an element, following the objective "likely to mislead" standard. But causation still is. The plaintiff must connect the practice to the loss. Defendants argue that without reliance there is no causation; most Florida courts reject that but demand real proof of the causal link.
- **Actual damages ceiling.** This is the defendant's best friend. A buyer who paid $50,000 for a system worth $50,000 but suffered $200,000 in lost business has no FDUTPA damages. Courts police this strictly.
- **Business plaintiffs after PNR.** Courts accept business-versus-business FDUTPA claims but still require the act to occur in "trade or commerce" and to be unfair or deceptive to the public standard, not merely sharp dealing between sophisticated parties. Some decisions require that the practice would harm consumers or competition generally, not only the plaintiff (verify).
- **Fees cut both ways.** Because § 501.2105 is prevailing-party, plaintiffs who add a weak FDUTPA count to a contract case expose themselves to a fee award if the count fails. Courts weigh the merit of the claim, the parties' resources, and whether it was brought to harass (verify factors).
- **Relation to fraud.** Common-law fraud requires a knowingly false statement, intent to induce, justifiable reliance, and damages, and must be pleaded with particularity. FDUTPA drops intent and reliance. Whether it must be pleaded with particularity in federal court is contested (verify). It often survives where fraud is dismissed, but recovers less.

## Florida specifics

- Florida's act is a typical "little FTC Act" and follows the majority of states in borrowing federal interpretations. It departs from some states in extending private standing to businesses without a consumer-transaction requirement after *PNR*.
- Class actions under FDUTPA are common because individual reliance need not be proved.
- The economic loss rule does not bar a FDUTPA claim, because the claim is statutory (see *Tiara* and module 04).
- Price gouging during a declared emergency is a separate FDUTPA-adjacent offense under § 501.160, enforced by the Attorney General.

## Angles checklist

- What exactly was said or omitted, by whom, when, and in what document? Is it a verifiable fact or puffery?
- Would a reasonable consumer in that market be misled? Was the fact material?
- Is there a per se hook: a statute or FTC rule the conduct violated?
- What are the actual damages under the market-value measure? If zero, the count is a fee trap.
- Flip it: is the plaintiff's own count weak enough to create prevailing-party fee exposure for them?
- Exemptions under § 501.212: is the conduct required or permitted by another law?
- Limitation: four years from the violation.
- Public enforcement risk: pattern conduct, vulnerable victims, Attorney General interest.
- Companion claims: fraud, negligent misrepresentation, breach of warranty, tortious interference.

## Drill

Bayfront Fitness LLC sells annual memberships advertised as "cancel anytime, no fees." The membership contract, signed on a tablet, says cancellation requires 60 days' notice and a $150 fee. Members who try to cancel are charged the fee. A member sues. A competing gym also sues, saying Bayfront's ads took its customers. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Member's FDUTPA claim.** "Cancel anytime, no fees" is a specific factual representation, not puffery. The contract term contradicts it. A reasonable consumer would likely be misled. Causation: the member joined on the strength of the ad. Actual damages: the $150 fee is the cleanest measure; arguably the difference between a membership with free cancellation and one without.
- **Per se hook.** Health studios are regulated by FDACS under §§ 501.012 to 501.019, which require specific cancellation rights and registration. A violation of that statute is a per se FDUTPA violation under § 501.203(3).
- **Contract defense.** Bayfront will argue the signed contract controls and the parol evidence rule bars the ad. FDUTPA is not a contract claim; the deception occurred in the advertising, so the contract does not cure it.
- **Competitor's claim.** After *PNR*, the competing gym has standing if it suffered actual damages from the unfair practice in trade or commerce. Proving damages is the problem: lost memberships are consequential, not a market-value loss, and courts may reject them. Tortious interference under *Ethan Allen* fails for customers at large without identifiable relationships.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the ad said "cancel anytime, see terms" and the tablet flow conspicuously displayed the fee before signature, deception weakens. If no member paid a fee, actual damages vanish and the claim is injunctive only.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A supplier promised delivery by June 1 and delivered July 15. Is that a FDUTPA violation?
2. A plaintiff proves a deceptive ad and $80,000 in lost profits from relying on it, but the product was worth what she paid. What does she recover under FDUTPA?
3. Why might adding a FDUTPA count to a solid contract claim be a mistake?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. By itself, no. A broken promise is breach of contract. It becomes FDUTPA only if the promise was deceptive when made, such as a known inability to deliver, or part of a practice of misstating lead times.
2. Nothing in damages. Actual damages are the market-value difference, which is zero. She may obtain declaratory or injunctive relief and may still have fraud or contract claims for the lost profits.
3. Because § 501.2105 awards fees to the prevailing party on that count. If the FDUTPA count fails while the contract count succeeds, the plaintiff may owe the defendant's fees for defending it.

</details>
