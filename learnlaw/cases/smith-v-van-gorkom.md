# Smith v. Van Gorkom, 488 A.2d 858 (Delaware Supreme Court 1985)

**Tags:** `[US]` · corporations · duty of care · business judgment rule · directors · mergers
**Used in:** [02-business-entities/04-fiduciary-duties-and-business-judgment.md](../02-business-entities/04-fiduciary-duties-and-business-judgment.md), [02-business-entities/07-corporations-in-outline.md](../02-business-entities/07-corporations-in-outline.md), [02-business-entities/03-operating-agreements.md](../02-business-entities/03-operating-agreements.md)

## One-line takeaway

The business judgment rule protects only informed decisions; directors who approve a sale of the company in two hours without reading the agreement or obtaining any valuation are grossly negligent and personally liable.

## Facts

Trans Union was a public railcar leasing company with investment tax credits it could not use. Its CEO, Jerome Van Gorkom, was nearing retirement. Without board authority he approached Jay Pritzker, a takeover specialist, and proposed a cash-out merger at $55 per share. The stock traded around $38. The $55 figure came from a CFO calculation that a leveraged buyout could be financed at that price; it was not a valuation. Pritzker agreed and demanded a decision within three days.

Van Gorkom called a board meeting on one day's notice without telling most directors the subject. He gave a twenty-minute oral presentation. No written agreement was distributed and no valuation existed. The CFO said $55 was at the low end of a fair range but that his work was not a valuation. After about two hours the board approved the merger, subject to a short "market test" that later amendments made nearly meaningless. Shareholders approved the merger the next year.

## Procedural posture

Shareholders brought a class action in the Delaware Court of Chancery for rescission or damages. After trial the Chancellor found the directors had acted on an informed basis and were protected by the business judgment rule. The plaintiffs appealed.

## Issue

Were the directors protected by the business judgment rule when they approved the sale of the company on the basis of a short oral presentation, without reading the agreement or obtaining any independent valuation?

## Holding

No. The directors were grossly negligent in failing to inform themselves of all material information reasonably available, including Van Gorkom's role in setting the price and the company's intrinsic value. The shareholder vote did not cure the problem because the shareholders were not fully informed. The Chancellor was reversed and the case remanded for a determination of the fair value of the shares, with the directors liable for the amount by which fair value exceeded $55.

## Reasoning

Justice Horsey, for a three-to-two majority, began with the rule. The business judgment rule presumes that directors acted on an informed basis, in good faith, and in the honest belief that the action was in the company's best interest. The protection depends on the first element. Under Delaware law the standard for whether a decision was informed is gross negligence.

The directors failed that standard on the record. They did not know how the $55 figure was reached. They did not know that it was the product of a feasibility study rather than a valuation. They did not ask for a valuation or for time to get one. A premium over a market price that reflected unusable tax credits was not evidence of intrinsic value. They did not read the agreement they approved. Experience and a premium over market could not substitute for information, and the statute permitting reliance on officers' reports did not cover a twenty-minute oral pitch.

Later meetings and the market test did not cure the defect, and shareholder ratification failed because the proxy materials did not disclose the flaws in the process.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Justice McNeilly dissented. The directors were among the most experienced business people in the country, knew the company intimately, and had been following its tax credit problem for years. He called the majority's account a "comedy of errors" that did not match the record. Justice Christie also dissented, deferring to the Chancellor's findings.

The case settled for a reported $23.5 million, much of it beyond insurance (verify). In 1986 Delaware enacted § 102(b)(7) of its General Corporation Law, allowing a certificate of incorporation to eliminate directors' personal liability for money damages for breach of the duty of care, but not for breach of loyalty, bad faith, or improper personal benefit.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

For a board or a group of LLC managers, this case is the process checklist. Get the documents in advance and read them. Know where the price came from. Get an outside opinion when the decision is large. Minute the questions asked and answered. Business judgment protection is earned by process, not outcome. That is why exculpation and indemnification provisions appear in nearly every set of articles and operating agreements.

## Florida connection

Florida's rule is similar, but Florida provides exculpation by statute rather than by charter. Under § 607.0831, Fla. Stat., a director is not personally liable for monetary damages for a vote, decision, or failure to act unless the director breached his or her duties and the breach amounts to one of the listed categories, such as a criminal violation, an improper personal benefit, an unlawful distribution, or, in a derivative suit, conscious disregard for the corporation's best interest or willful misconduct (verify the current text). Standards of conduct are in § 607.0830, and articles may add optional provisions under § 607.0202 (verify which protections may be added). For LLCs, § 605.04091 limits the duty of care to refraining from grossly negligent or reckless conduct, willful misconduct, or a knowing violation of law, and § 605.0105 permits the operating agreement to alter the duty of care, and to limit or eliminate liability for breach of it, within the statute's listed limits (verify the subsection).
