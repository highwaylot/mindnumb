# Patents in outline

**Module:** 06 · **Lesson:** 5 · **Time:** ~40 min
**Tags:** `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Most small businesses will never need a patent, and many that pursue one waste the money. But every business that builds a product, improves a process, or receives a letter from a "patent licensing" company needs to understand the outline: what a patent is, what it takes to get one, the one-year clock that quietly kills rights, and the questions that tell you whether to bother. This lesson teaches enough to ask a patent attorney the right questions.

## The rule

Patents are exclusively federal, under Title 35 of the U.S. Code, granted by the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office. There is no state patent. A patent is a right to **exclude** others from making, using, selling, offering to sell, or importing the invention (35 U.S.C. § 271). It is not a right to practice the invention yourself; someone else's patent may cover part of it.

**Types.**

- **Utility patent**: a new and useful process, machine, article of manufacture, or composition of matter, or an improvement of one (§ 101). Term is 20 years from the filing date.
- **Design patent**: a new, original, and ornamental design for an article of manufacture (§ 171). Protects how it looks, not how it works. Term is 15 years from grant.

**Requirements for a utility patent.** The applicant must show, and the examiner tests:

1. **Patentable subject matter** (§ 101). Laws of nature, natural phenomena, and abstract ideas are excluded. *Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International*, 573 U.S. 208 (2014), made most software and business-method claims hard to patent unless they add an "inventive concept" beyond the abstract idea.
2. **Novelty** (§ 102). The invention must not have been patented, described in a printed publication, in public use, on sale, or otherwise available to the public anywhere before the filing date. Since the America Invents Act (2013), the United States is a **first-inventor-to-file** system: the first to file wins, not the first to invent.
3. **Non-obviousness** (§ 103). The invention must not have been obvious to a person of ordinary skill in the field at the time of filing, in light of the prior art. *KSR International Co. v. Teleflex Inc.*, 550 U.S. 398 (2007), rejected rigid tests and asks whether the combination would have been a predictable use of known elements.
4. **Written description, enablement, and definite claims** (§ 112). The application must describe the invention so that a skilled person can make and use it, and the claims must define its boundaries clearly.

**The one-year grace period** (§ 102(b)(1)). The inventor's own public disclosure, sale, or public use within one year before filing does not count as prior art against the inventor. After one year, it does, and the invention is unpatentable in the United States. Most other countries have no grace period; any public disclosure before filing destroys foreign rights.

**Provisional applications** (§ 111(b)). A provisional establishes a filing date for what it describes, is not examined, expires after 12 months, and must be followed by a non-provisional application claiming its date. It allows "patent pending" and costs a small fraction of a full application (verify current fee; small and micro entity discounts apply). A provisional that does not describe the invention adequately gives no priority for what it omits.

**Patent pending.** A truthful statement that an application is on file. It gives no enforceable rights until the patent issues. Falsely marking "patented" or "patent pending" is actionable under § 292.

**Ownership.** The inventor owns the patent rights unless assigned. An employer does not automatically own an employee's invention; it owns it if the employee was hired to invent it or signed an assignment, and otherwise may have only a "shop right" to use it. See lesson 6.

**Infringement and remedies.** Literal infringement or infringement under the doctrine of equivalents. Remedies are an injunction, which is no longer automatic after *eBay Inc. v. MercExchange, L.L.C.*, 547 U.S. 388 (2006); damages of at least a reasonable royalty, or lost profits; up to treble damages for willful infringement; and fees in exceptional cases (§§ 283 to 285).

## How courts reason about it

**Claims are the invention.** Courts and examiners read the claims, not the product. A broad claim is more valuable and easier to invalidate; a narrow one is easier to design around. Claim construction is a question of law for the judge.

**Obviousness is the main battleground.** Almost anything is a combination of known elements. The question is whether a skilled person would have had reason to combine them with a reasonable expectation of success. Commercial success, long-felt need, and copying can show non-obviousness if tied to the claimed features.

**The on-sale and public-use traps.** Courts treat a commercial offer for sale as triggering the clock even if confidential, *Helsinn Healthcare S.A. v. Teva Pharmaceuticals USA, Inc.*, 586 U.S. 123 (2019). Demonstrating a prototype at a trade show, posting it online, or selling a few units all start the one-year period.

**Eligibility for software.** After *Alice*, claims to a specific technical improvement in how a computer works survive; claims to a business practice "on a computer" do not.

**Demand letters from non-practicing entities.** Patent assertion entities send mass demand letters to small businesses for using ordinary technology. Florida's bad-faith patent assertion statute, § 501.993, Fla. Stat. (verify), makes bad-faith letters actionable under FDUTPA. Check whether the patent is expired, whether a vendor has indemnified you, and whether the claims plausibly read on what you do.

## Florida specifics

Patent law is entirely federal and Florida follows it. Florida's additions are peripheral: the bad-faith assertion statute, trade secret law (lesson 4) as the alternative for processes, and contract law for assignments and licenses. Federal courts in Florida hear infringement cases, with appeals to the Federal Circuit rather than the Eleventh Circuit (28 U.S.C. § 1295).

**When not to bother.** A patent is worth pursuing when the invention can be detected in a competitor's product, when it will still matter in three to five years, when the market is large enough to justify a six-figure enforcement budget, and when the business can describe the invention in claims a competitor cannot easily design around. It is usually not worth pursuing for software features with short cycles, for processes a competitor cannot see and trade secret law can protect indefinitely, for inventions disclosed more than a year ago, or when the budget stops at the application. A utility patent through issuance commonly costs well into five figures with attorney fees (verify), and examination takes one to three years.

## Angles checklist

- What exactly is new? Is it a product, a process, a design, or a business idea?
- When was it first publicly shown, sold, offered, or described? Is the one-year clock running, or expired?
- Who invented it, and what did they sign? Employee, contractor, founder?
- Is it software or a business method, and does it improve how a computer works?
- Can infringement be detected from outside? Would a trade secret protect it better?
- Is there a budget to prosecute and to enforce?
- Flip it: prior art search; invalidity; design-around; a received demand letter's expiration date, indemnity from vendors, and the Florida bad-faith statute.

## Drill

Two engineers who own a Tampa LLC built a sensor that cuts energy use in commercial refrigeration. They demonstrated it at a trade show 14 months ago, sold three units to a grocery chain 10 months ago, and posted a video 8 months ago. They have no agreements with each other about who owns the design, and a contractor wrote the firmware. They now want a patent and have received a letter from a Delaware company claiming its patent covers "remote monitoring of refrigeration equipment." Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **The one-year clock.** The trade show demonstration 14 months ago was a public use or disclosure more than one year before any filing. If the demonstration disclosed the claimed invention, U.S. rights are lost under § 102. If it showed only the housing and not the sensing method, method claims may survive. The sales 10 months ago and the video 8 months ago are inside the grace period but the clock is short. File a provisional immediately for whatever remains patentable. Foreign rights are gone.
- **Ownership.** The two engineers are joint inventors who each own an undivided interest absent assignment, and either can license without the other's consent. Nothing shows the LLC owns anything. The contractor owns the firmware's copyright, and if the contractor contributed to any claimed invention, is a co-inventor too. Assignments from all three to the LLC are needed before filing.
- **Eligibility.** A sensor that improves refrigeration control is a physical device with a technical effect, a good candidate. Claims to "monitoring energy data" alone risk *Alice*.
- **The demand letter.** Check the Delaware patent's expiration date, read its claims rather than the letter's summary, and ask whether the claims plausibly read on the sensor. Consider whether the letter is bad-faith assertion under § 501.993 (verify).
- **Alternative.** If the sensing method is invisible in the product, trade secret protection may fit better, with a design patent for the housing.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. Does "patent pending" let you sue someone who copies the product?
2. An employee invents a tool at home that improves the company's process. Who owns it?
3. Why can a confidential sale to one customer start the one-year clock?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. There is no enforceable right until the patent issues. At most, you may later recover a reasonable royalty for the period after the application published, if the issued claims are substantially identical (§ 154(d)).
2. The employee, unless hired to invent or bound by an assignment agreement. The company may have a shop right to use the tool in its own business without paying, but not to stop others. Lesson 6 covers the agreement that changes this.
3. Because § 102 treats an invention as "on sale" when it is the subject of a commercial offer and ready for patenting, and *Helsinn* held that a confidential sale counts. The grace period runs from that date.

</details>
