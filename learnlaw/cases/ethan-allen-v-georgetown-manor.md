# Ethan Allen, Inc. v. Georgetown Manor, Inc., 647 So. 2d 812 (Fla. 1994)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · torts · tortious interference · business relationships · customers
**Used in:** [`04-torts/04-tortious-interference.md`](../04-torts/04-tortious-interference.md), [`05-agency-employment/06-non-competes-and-restrictive-covenants.md`](../05-agency-employment/06-non-competes-and-restrictive-covenants.md)

## One-line takeaway

Tortious interference with a business relationship requires an identifiable relationship with specific customers, evidenced by an actual understanding that in all probability would have been completed; a business's relationship with its past customers at large is not enough.

## Facts

Georgetown Manor sold Ethan Allen furniture in South Florida for years as an Ethan Allen Gallery dealer. Over time it had sold to roughly 89,000 customers whose names it kept. Ethan Allen decided to change its distribution and told Georgetown it would no longer supply it. Georgetown decided to close its stores. Ethan Allen then ran newspaper advertisements announcing that Georgetown would no longer be an Ethan Allen dealer and pointing readers to new Ethan Allen outlets. Georgetown contended the advertisements were misleading, suggested it had already closed, and diverted its customers.

## Procedural posture

Georgetown sued Ethan Allen in Dade County circuit court on several theories, including tortious interference with its business relationships with its past customers. A jury found for Georgetown on the interference claim and awarded substantial damages. The Third District Court of Appeal affirmed in part. Ethan Allen sought review in the Florida Supreme Court, which accepted jurisdiction on conflict with decisions of other district courts.

## Issue

Can a business's general relationship with its past customers, not tied to any identifiable pending transaction, support a claim for tortious interference with a business relationship?

## Holding

No. The Florida Supreme Court held that a claim for tortious interference with a business relationship requires a relationship with identifiable customers, evidenced by an actual and identifiable understanding or agreement which in all probability would have been completed if the defendant had not interfered. The mere hope that past customers would return and buy again is not a business relationship the tort protects. The court quashed the award on the interference count.

## Reasoning

The court restated the elements of the tort, drawn from *Tamiami Trail Tours, Inc. v. Cotton*, 463 So. 2d 1126 (Fla. 1985): the existence of a business relationship, the defendant's knowledge of it, an intentional and unjustified interference with it, and damage to the plaintiff as a result. The relationship need not be a binding contract. It must, however, afford the plaintiff legal rights, which means something more than a speculative expectation.

Georgetown's claim rested on its list of 89,000 past customers and the assumption that some would have bought again. The court said that was a relationship with the community at large, not with any identifiable person. There was no evidence that any particular customer intended to buy, had an understanding with Georgetown, or was diverted by the advertisements. Without that, there was no relationship to interfere with and no way to measure damages except by guessing.

The court also noted that Georgetown's own decision to close contributed to the loss of future sales. Ethan Allen was entitled to tell the public that Georgetown would no longer carry its line. Competition for customers who are free to choose is not interference.

## Dissent or counterpoints

The strongest argument for Georgetown was that a retailer's customer base is its most valuable asset and that misleading advertisements aimed at that base cause real loss. The answer the court gave is that the loss, if any, had to be proved through specific relationships, and that other torts cover misleading statements. A false statement about a competitor's business can be defamation or injurious falsehood, and a deceptive advertisement can be a FDUTPA violation. The tort of interference is not a general remedy for lost goodwill.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

This case is the reason a departing employee's new employer, or a competitor who advertises aggressively, is usually not liable for tortious interference merely because your customers go elsewhere. To bring the claim you need to identify the customers, show a pending or ongoing relationship, show the defendant knew about it, and show the defendant did something beyond fair competition. The same rule protects you when you are the one competing. On the other side, if you are the plaintiff, the evidence that wins is specific: names, dates, pending orders, written or testified-to expectations, and proof that a particular customer left because of the defendant's conduct.

## Florida connection

This is Florida law and it is cited in nearly every Florida interference case. The rule works together with § 542.335(1)(b)3, Fla. Stat., which lists "substantial relationships with specific prospective or existing customers" as a legitimate business interest supporting a restrictive covenant. In both settings Florida law protects specific relationships, not customer lists in the abstract. Two further Florida limits matter. A party to a contract cannot interfere with its own contract, and an agent acting within the scope of its agency is treated as the principal. *Salit v. Ruden, McClosky*, 742 So. 2d 381 (Fla. 4th DCA 1999) (verify citation). And interference is privileged where the defendant acts to protect its own legitimate financial interest by proper means. *Ethan Allen* sets the threshold question: is there a relationship at all. A four-year limitation period applies (§ 95.11(3), Fla. Stat.) (verify subsection).
