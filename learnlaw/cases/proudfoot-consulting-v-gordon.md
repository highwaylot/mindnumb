# Proudfoot Consulting Co. v. Gordon, 576 F.3d 1223 (11th Cir. 2009)

**Tags:** `[FL]` `[US]` · employment · non-competes · injunctions · damages · § 542.335
**Used in:** [`05-agency-employment/06-non-competes-and-restrictive-covenants.md`](../05-agency-employment/06-non-competes-and-restrictive-covenants.md), [`06-property-ip/04-trade-secrets.md`](../06-property-ip/04-trade-secrets.md), [`01-foundations/04-remedies.md`](../01-foundations/04-remedies.md)

## One-line takeaway

Under Florida's § 542.335, an employer that proves a legitimate business interest and a breach gets an injunction without proving actual harm, but to recover damages it must still prove that the breach caused a specific loss.

## Facts

Proudfoot is a management consulting firm that sends teams into client businesses to improve operations. Edward Gordon worked for Proudfoot for several years, rising to a project management role in which he dealt directly with clients and learned Proudfoot's methods, pricing, and pipeline. His employment agreement contained a six-month post-employment non-compete, a non-solicitation covenant covering clients he had worked with, and a confidentiality clause. In 2007 he resigned and, within days, started work for Highland Group, a direct competitor, in a similar role. Proudfoot learned of it and sued.

## Procedural posture

Proudfoot sued Gordon in the Southern District of Florida for breach of the covenants. After a bench trial, the district court found the covenants enforceable under § 542.335, Fla. Stat., entered an injunction barring Gordon from working for Highland for six months running from the date of the order, and awarded Proudfoot damages measured by profits it claimed to have lost on a client engagement that went to Highland (verify details of the award). Gordon appealed to the Eleventh Circuit.

## Issue

Did the district court correctly apply § 542.335 in finding legitimate business interests, in entering the injunction, and in awarding lost-profit damages?

## Holding

The Eleventh Circuit affirmed the finding that the covenants were enforceable and affirmed the injunction. It vacated the damages award because Proudfoot had not proved that Gordon's breach caused the lost engagement (verify the precise disposition of the damages award). The court remanded on damages.

## Reasoning

The court walked through § 542.335 as a Florida court would. The statute requires a writing signed by the employee, a legitimate business interest, and a restraint reasonably necessary to protect it. Proudfoot proved three interests: confidential information about its methods and pricing, substantial relationships with specific clients Gordon had served, and the specialized training it gave him. Gordon argued his knowledge was general skill in consulting. The court found the record supported the district court's view that he carried Proudfoot-specific information a competitor could use.

On the injunction, the court relied on § 542.335(1)(j), which provides that violation of an enforceable restrictive covenant creates a presumption of irreparable injury. Proudfoot did not have to show it had actually lost business. It also relied on the statute's rule that a court may not consider the individualized economic hardship to the employee, § 542.335(1)(g)1, so Gordon's argument that the injunction would cost him his job did not count. The court approved running the six-month period from the date of the injunction rather than from the date Gordon left, because otherwise an employee could run out the clock during litigation.

On damages, the court applied ordinary Florida contract principles. Lost profits must be proved with reasonable certainty and must be caused by the breach. Proudfoot showed that Highland won an engagement Proudfoot had pursued, and that Gordon worked on it. It did not show that Proudfoot would have won the engagement but for Gordon's involvement. Without that link, the award could not stand.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. Gordon's strongest points were two. First, the information he carried was the kind any experienced consultant has, and treating it as a protectable interest turns every skilled employee's experience into the employer's property. Florida's statute, read with cases like *White v. Mederi Caretenders*, 226 So. 3d 774 (Fla. 2017), answers that the interest must be specific and proved, which the court found it was here. Second, running the restraint from the injunction date extended the covenant beyond its stated six months. Courts accept that extension because the alternative rewards breach.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

This case shows both halves of a non-compete fight. For the employer, the injunction is the real remedy. If the covenant is signed, the interest is real, and the breach is clear, Florida law delivers an injunction without proof of harm. Damages are a separate and harder case; they require evidence tying a specific lost client or contract to the employee's breach. For the employee, and for the new employer who hires one, the lesson is that Florida does not weigh the employee's hardship, and the six months can start when the court rules, not when the employee quit. The hiring business can also face a tortious interference claim if it knew of the covenant.

## Florida connection

The case applies Florida law and is persuasive, not binding, in Florida state courts, which reach the same results under § 542.335. The statute's presumptions on duration, 6 months or less presumed reasonable and more than 2 years presumed unreasonable for a former employee (verify), its presumption of irreparable injury, and its bar on considering employee hardship all appear in the opinion. The 2025 CHOICE Act, §§ 542.41 to 542.45, Fla. Stat. (verify numbering), goes further for covered high earners: a court must preliminarily enjoin a breach and the employee must show by clear and convincing evidence that the covenant should not be enforced. For an ordinary employee, *Proudfoot* and § 542.335 remain the framework. Tortious interference against the hiring employer follows *Ethan Allen, Inc. v. Georgetown Manor, Inc.*, 647 So. 2d 812 (Fla. 1994).
