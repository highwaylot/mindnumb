# Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins, 304 U.S. 64 (U.S. Supreme Court 1938)

**Tags:** `[US]` · federal courts · diversity jurisdiction · choice of law · sources of law
**Used in:** [00-how-law-works/01-sources-and-hierarchy.md](../00-how-law-works/01-sources-and-hierarchy.md), [01-foundations/01-federal-and-state-courts.md](../01-foundations/01-federal-and-state-courts.md), [01-foundations/02-jurisdiction-and-venue.md](../01-foundations/02-jurisdiction-and-venue.md)

## One-line takeaway

A federal court hearing a state-law claim applies the state's substantive law, including its court-made common law, because there is no federal general common law.

## Facts

Harry Tompkins, a Pennsylvania citizen, was walking at night along a well-worn footpath beside the Erie Railroad's tracks near Hughestown, Pennsylvania. A passing freight train struck him with something projecting from a car, which he said was an open door. He lost an arm. Erie was a New York corporation.

Under Pennsylvania court decisions, a person using a path along the tracks was a trespasser, and the railroad owed him only a duty to avoid wanton or willful injury. Under the "general law" that federal courts had applied since *Swift v. Tyson* (1842), the railroad owed ordinary care.

## Procedural posture

Tompkins sued Erie in the federal district court for the Southern District of New York, invoking diversity jurisdiction. Erie argued Pennsylvania law applied. The trial judge refused to apply it, holding that the question was one of general law on which federal courts were free to exercise independent judgment. The jury awarded Tompkins $30,000. The Second Circuit affirmed. The Supreme Court granted certiorari.

## Issue

Must a federal court sitting in diversity apply the unwritten, court-made law of the state, or may it apply its own view of the general common law?

## Holding

It must apply state law. "Except in matters governed by the Federal Constitution or by acts of Congress, the law to be applied in any case is the law of the state." Whether that law is declared by the legislature in a statute or by the state's highest court in a decision does not matter. *Swift v. Tyson* was overruled. The judgment was reversed and the case sent back for the court to apply Pennsylvania law.

## Reasoning

Justice Brandeis gave three reasons.

First, *Swift* rested on a misreading of the Rules of Decision Act. Historical research showed that "the laws of the several states" in that Act meant to include state court decisions, not only statutes.

Second, *Swift* had failed in practice. It did not produce the uniformity its supporters wanted, because state courts kept applying their own law. It produced the opposite: two bodies of law in the same state, and a litigant's rights depended on whether the opposing party happened to be from another state and could get into federal court. The Court pointed to a Kentucky taxi company that had reincorporated in Tennessee solely to get a federal forum and a more favorable rule. That was discrimination against citizens of the forum state and an invitation to forum shopping.

Third, and decisive, *Swift* was unconstitutional. Congress has no power to declare substantive rules of common law applicable in a state, whether in commercial law or in torts. The federal courts, exercising no power Congress lacks, could not do it either. "There is no federal general common law."

The Court left in place federal procedure in federal courts, and later cases drew the line between substance and procedure.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Justice Reed concurred in part. He would have reached the result by reading the Rules of Decision Act, without declaring *Swift* unconstitutional, since no party had argued the constitutional point. Justice Butler, joined by Justice McReynolds, dissented. He objected that the Court had decided a constitutional question neither party raised and would have affirmed on narrower grounds. Justice Cardozo did not participate.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

If your Florida LLC is sued by an out-of-state party in federal court in Tampa, Orlando, or Miami, the contract and tort rules will be Florida's. Removing a case to federal court changes the procedure and the judge, not the substantive rules. Lawyers still remove cases for the federal summary judgment practice, the jury pool, and the pace, but since Florida adopted the federal summary judgment standard in 2021 that gap has narrowed.

It also means a federal opinion applying Florida law is only a prediction of what the Florida Supreme Court would say. It can be wrong, and the Florida courts can later say so.

## Florida connection

Federal courts in Florida apply this rule daily. On a question of Florida law they are bound by the Florida Supreme Court. They follow the District Courts of Appeal unless there is persuasive indication that the Florida Supreme Court would rule differently. Where Florida law is unsettled, a federal appellate court may certify the question to the Florida Supreme Court under Fla. Const. art. V, § 3(b)(6) and Fla. R. App. P. 9.150. *Olmstead v. FTC*, briefed in this course, reached the Florida Supreme Court exactly that way. Florida has no statute on the point because none is needed. The rule governs the federal court, not the state.
