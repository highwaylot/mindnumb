# The economic loss rule and the line between contract and tort

**Module:** 04 · **Lesson:** 6 · **Time:** ~40 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

When a deal goes bad, the injured party wants to sue in tort as well as contract. Tort opens punitive damages and avoids the contract's caps and disclaimers. The economic loss rule is how courts say no: if your only loss is money and a contract governed the relationship, contract law is your remedy. Florida narrowed the rule sharply in 2013, but a related doctrine still blocks tort claims that are the contract claim in a different hat. Knowing the line tells you what claims you actually have, and what your limitation clause actually stops.

## The rule

**The economic loss rule** bars recovery in negligence for purely economic losses, meaning losses with no accompanying injury to person or to property other than the product itself.

In Florida after *Tiara Condominium Ass'n v. Marsh & McLennan Cos.*, 110 So. 3d 399 (Fla. 2013), the rule applies **only in products liability cases**. A buyer whose defective product damages only itself, causing lost value, repair cost, or lost profits, cannot sue the manufacturer in negligence or strict liability. The remedy is warranty under chapter 672. If the product damages other property or injures a person, tort is available for that harm.

**The independent tort doctrine** survives *Tiara* and does the work the contractual economic loss rule used to do. A party to a contract cannot sue the other party in tort for conduct that is merely a breach of the contract. To bring a tort claim, the plaintiff must show conduct that is independent of the breach: a duty that exists apart from the contract, or a wrong that caused harm distinct from the contract's non-performance. Fraud in the inducement, which precedes the contract, is independent. Negligent performance of the contract itself usually is not.

**Exceptions and non-applications** recognized before and after *Tiara*:

- Professional negligence by accountants, lawyers, engineers, and similar professionals, who owe duties independent of contract. *Moransais v. Heathman*, 744 So. 2d 973 (Fla. 1999).
- Fraudulent inducement and negligent misrepresentation that induced the contract. *HTP, Ltd. v. Lineas Aereas Costarricenses, S.A.*, 685 So. 2d 1238 (Fla. 1996).
- Statutory claims such as FDUTPA, which the legislature created regardless of contract.
- Claims by parties not in privity, where no contract allocates the risk.

The burden is on the plaintiff to plead a tort claim that is independent. The defendant raises the rule by motion to dismiss or for summary judgment.

## How courts reason about it

The rule exists to keep contract law from being swallowed by tort. If a buyer who bargained for a product with a disclaimer of consequential damages could sue in negligence and recover those damages anyway, the disclaimer is worthless, warranty law is pointless, and prices cannot reflect allocated risk. The U.S. Supreme Court said so in admiralty in *East River Steamship Corp. v. Transamerica Delaval Inc.*, 476 U.S. 858 (1986), and the Florida Supreme Court adopted the reasoning in *Florida Power & Light Co. v. Westinghouse Electric Corp.*, 510 So. 2d 899 (Fla. 1987).

Florida then expanded the rule beyond products. *AFM Corp. v. Southern Bell Telephone & Telegraph Co.*, 515 So. 2d 180 (Fla. 1987), applied it to bar a negligence claim between contracting parties over a botched Yellow Pages listing. For two decades the "contractual" economic loss rule barred most tort claims between contracting parties, with a growing list of exceptions until the rule became unpredictable.

*Tiara* ended the experiment. A condominium association sued its insurance broker for negligence and breach of fiduciary duty over advice about coverage limits. On a certified question from the Eleventh Circuit, the Florida Supreme Court held the rule applies only to products liability. Justice Pariente's concurrence, which has driven the law since, explained that this did not open the floodgates: a tort claim must still be independent of the contract breach. Lower courts follow the concurrence and dismiss negligence claims that merely allege careless performance of the contract.

So the current analysis has two steps.

1. Is this a products liability case where the only damage is to the product itself? If yes, no tort; warranty only.
2. If not, does the tort claim rest on a duty or a wrong independent of the contract? If it merely restates the breach, it is dismissed. If it alleges fraud before the contract, a professional duty, a statutory violation, or injury to a person or to other property, it proceeds.

Courts also police **"other property."** In *Casa Clara Condominium Ass'n v. Charley Toppino & Sons, Inc.*, 620 So. 2d 1244 (Fla. 1993), defective concrete in a building was held to have damaged only the product the owners bought, the building, so negligence was barred. Courts ask what the plaintiff purchased as a unit. A defective component that destroys the machine it was installed in usually damages only "the product."

## Florida specifics

Florida's position is now a minority one in its narrowness. Many states still apply a contractual economic loss rule between parties in privity. Florida confines the rule to products liability and uses the independent tort doctrine instead.

- *Tiara*, 110 So. 3d 399 (Fla. 2013): economic loss rule limited to products liability.
- *Casa Clara*, 620 So. 2d 1244 (Fla. 1993): the "product itself" includes the integrated whole the buyer purchased.
- *HTP*, 685 So. 2d 1238 (Fla. 1996): fraudulent inducement is independent of the contract.
- *Moransais*, 744 So. 2d 973 (Fla. 1999): professional negligence claims survive.
- The independent tort doctrine is applied across the Florida DCAs after *Tiara*, following the Pariente concurrence (verify for recent decisions).
- Negligent misrepresentation that induced the contract is treated like fraud: independent and not barred. Negligent misrepresentation during performance usually fails as duplicative.
- Contract limitation of liability clauses are enforced against contract claims and against negligence claims that duplicate them. They are not enforced against fraud.

## Angles checklist

- What was lost: money only, or injury to a person or to property other than what was bought?
- Is there a product? Did it damage anything beyond itself?
- Is there a contract between the parties? What does it say about remedies and caps?
- Does the tort claim allege anything the contract claim does not? A lie before signing, a professional duty, a statute?
- Was the misrepresentation made to induce the contract, or during performance?
- Is there a defendant outside the contract, such as a subcontractor or a manufacturer, who can be reached in tort?
- Flip it: if you are the defendant, which counts in the complaint simply restate the breach?

## Drill

A Daytona Beach bakery buys a $60,000 (verify) commercial oven from a dealer under a contract that disclaims consequential damages and limits remedies to repair or replacement. The oven's thermostat fails. Over three months of malfunction, the bakery loses $45,000 in spoiled product and sales, and one failure causes a small fire that destroys a $9,000 mixer next to the oven. The bakery sues the dealer and the manufacturer for breach of warranty, negligence, and strict liability. Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Economic loss rule, products case.** The oven is a product. Lost product and lost sales are purely economic losses from the product's failure to perform. Under *Tiara*, the rule still applies in products cases. The negligence and strict liability claims for those losses are barred against both the dealer and the manufacturer.
- **Other property.** The mixer is property other than the product. Tort claims for the $9,000 mixer proceed against both defendants, and the contract's remedy limitation does not reach the manufacturer in tort for that damage.
- **Warranty against the dealer.** The bakery's contract remedy. The repair-or-replace limitation is enforceable under § 672.719 unless it fails of its essential purpose. Three months of failed repairs is a strong argument that it did. If so, the bakery recovers general warranty damages. Whether the consequential damages exclusion also falls depends on how the court treats the two clauses; the majority treats them independently, so the $45,000 may remain excluded.
- **Warranty against the manufacturer.** Florida requires privity for implied warranty claims. *Kramer v. Piper Aircraft*. Unless the manufacturer issued an express warranty to the end buyer, the warranty claim against it fails.
- **Likely outcome.** Tort recovery for the mixer against both. Warranty recovery against the dealer for the oven's diminished value or price, likely without the $45,000 in consequential loss unless the exclusion is held unconscionable or linked to the failed remedy.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the fire had injured an employee, personal injury claims proceed in tort against both. If the dealer had lied about the oven's history to induce the sale, fraud in the inducement would be independent and would also escape the cap.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A client sues its bookkeeper, who had a written engagement letter, for negligently misclassifying expenses and causing a tax penalty. Barred?
2. A software vendor fails to deliver features promised in the contract. The customer sues for breach and for negligence. What happens to the negligence count?
3. Why did the Florida Supreme Court narrow the rule in *Tiara*?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. This is not a products case, so the economic loss rule does not apply after *Tiara*. A negligence claim against a professional for breach of a duty of care that exists independent of the engagement letter proceeds under *Moransais*, alongside any contract claim.
2. The negligence count is likely dismissed under the independent tort doctrine. Careless failure to perform the contract is a breach of contract, not a tort. The customer has the contract claim and its remedies.
3. Because the contractual version of the rule had become unpredictable, with exceptions swallowing it, and because contract law already has tools to keep tort claims out: the independent tort requirement, privity, and the enforcement of agreed remedies. The court confined the rule to the products setting where its rationale, protecting warranty law, is strongest.

</details>
