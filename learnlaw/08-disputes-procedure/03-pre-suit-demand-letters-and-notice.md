# Pre-suit demand letters and notice

**Module:** 08 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[FL]` `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Most disputes end with a letter, not a lawsuit. A good demand states the claim, the number, the deadline, and what happens next, and creates a record a judge will later read. Some claims cannot be filed until a statutory notice has been sent and a cure period has run. And once a dispute is foreseeable, a duty to preserve evidence attaches. This lesson covers what a demand does, which notices are mandatory, and two traps: the "paid in full" check and the letter that itself becomes a claim.

## The rule

**What a demand letter does.** No statute requires one for an ordinary contract claim. But it (1) fixes the date of breach and the amount for prejudgment interest, (2) satisfies any contractual notice-and-cure clause, (3) shows good faith for fee and sanctions purposes, (4) triggers an insurer's duty to respond if the recipient has coverage, and (5) often gets paid. It should not threaten criminal prosecution to collect a debt; that is extortion under § 836.05.

**Mandatory pre-suit notices in Florida.** Skip one and the suit is dismissed or abated.

- **Florida Minimum Wage Act, § 448.110(6).** Before suing, the employee must give the employer written notice identifying the minimum wage claimed, the actual or estimated hours, and the total amount. The employer has 15 calendar days (verify) to pay. The federal FLSA has no such requirement.
- **Construction liens, ch. 713.** A subcontractor or supplier not in privity with the owner must serve a Notice to Owner within 45 days (verify) of first furnishing (§ 713.06). A claim of lien must be recorded within 90 days (verify) of final furnishing. A contractor must give a final payment affidavit at least five days (verify) before suing to enforce the lien (§ 713.06(3)(d)). Suit to foreclose within one year (verify) of recording (§ 713.22). A willfully exaggerated lien is fraudulent under § 713.31.
- **Section 57.105 sanctions.** A motion for fees against a party or lawyer for a claim or defense with no basis in fact or law must be served on the other side 21 days (verify) before it is filed, giving them a chance to withdraw it (§ 57.105(4)).
- **Punitive damages, § 768.72.** A gate, not a notice: no punitive claim may be pleaded until the court grants leave on an evidentiary showing.
- **Discrimination, § 760.11.** A charge must be filed with the Florida Commission on Human Relations within 365 days (verify) of the act, or with the EEOC within 300 days (verify) for federal claims, before suit.
- **Associations.** Condominium disputes go to pre-suit mediation or arbitration under § 718.1255 (verify); homeowners' association disputes to pre-suit mediation under § 720.311.
- **Insurance.** A property insurance suit requires pre-suit notice under § 627.70152 at least 10 business days (verify) before filing (verify status after the 2022 and 2023 reforms). A bad-faith claim requires a civil remedy notice under § 624.155 with a 60-day (verify) cure. The one-way fee statute § 627.428 was repealed in 2023 (verify); § 86.121 now covers coverage declaratory actions (verify).
- **Landlord and tenant.** Residential three-day notice under § 83.56; nonresidential under § 83.20.

**Preserving evidence.** A duty to preserve relevant evidence arises when litigation is reasonably anticipated, not when the complaint arrives. A litigation hold is a written instruction to stop deleting and to collect. Florida does not recognize an independent tort of first-party spoliation (*Martino v. Wal-Mart Stores, Inc.*, 908 So. 2d 342 (Fla. 2005)); the remedy is sanctions, an adverse inference, and in severe cases striking pleadings. Fed. R. Civ. P. 37(e) governs electronic data in federal court.

**Accord and satisfaction by check, § 673.3111.** If a person in good faith tenders a check with a conspicuous statement that it is in full satisfaction of a disputed or unliquidated claim, and the claimant cashes it, the claim is discharged. Two protections for the business receiving such a check: it can designate in advance a person, office, or address where such tenders must be sent, and if the check went elsewhere the discharge fails; and it can repay the amount within 90 days (verify) and undo the discharge. Writing "under protest" on the check does not help.

**Burden.** The defendant raises a missed pre-suit requirement; the plaintiff shows compliance. The party claiming accord and satisfaction proves each element, including a bona fide dispute.

## How courts reason about it

- **Notice statutes are conditions precedent.** Courts dismiss without prejudice for non-compliance when the statute says "before filing." Where the period can still be satisfied, courts often abate rather than dismiss. Courts are strict on content: a minimum wage notice that omits the hours or amount is defective.
- **Substantial compliance versus strict compliance.** Lien law is strict because it creates a non-consensual encumbrance on someone's property. Contractual notice clauses are read for substantial compliance when the other side actually received the information, but a clause that specifies the method is enforced as written if the party insists.
- **Spoliation.** Courts ask when litigation became reasonably foreseeable, whether the party had control of the evidence, whether it was relevant, and whether the loss was negligent or intentional. An auto-delete policy that keeps running after a demand letter arrives is negligence. The adverse inference instruction tells the jury it may assume the lost evidence was unfavorable, which usually decides the case.
- **Accord and satisfaction.** Courts look for a real dispute at the time of tender, a conspicuous statement on the check or an accompanying writing, and cashing. A check marked "final payment" on an undisputed invoice is not an accord; there was nothing to compromise.
- **Demand letters as claims.** Statements in a demand letter made in good faith in anticipation of litigation are protected by at least a qualified privilege against defamation, and Florida's litigation privilege is absolute for statements in the proceeding itself (verify scope for pre-suit). A letter that threatens criminal charges, or is sent to the recipient's customers, loses the privilege and can be extortion or tortious interference. A collection letter to a consumer must comply with § 559.72, which applies to original creditors, and the federal FDCPA if sent by a collector.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the majority of states on the UCC accord and satisfaction rule in § 673.3111 and on the duty to preserve evidence. It departs in refusing a first-party spoliation tort.
- The § 448.110(6) notice has no federal counterpart; a plaintiff suing under both statutes must satisfy it for the Florida count.

## Case anchors

- [`cases/pnr-v-beacon-property-management.md`](../cases/pnr-v-beacon-property-management.md): a demand that misstates facts in trade or commerce can itself be an unfair practice.
- [`cases/ethan-allen-v-georgetown-manor.md`](../cases/ethan-allen-v-georgetown-manor.md): a letter sent to a competitor's customers can be tortious interference if it disrupts identifiable relationships.

## Angles checklist

- Does any statute require a pre-suit notice, and what must it contain? What is the cure period?
- Does the contract have a notice-and-cure clause, a method, an address?
- Has the demand fixed the date and amount for interest?
- Litigation hold: who received it, what systems, when did the duty start?
- Incoming checks: is there a designated address for disputed tenders? Who opens the mail and deposits?
- Is the letter going only to the adverse party, and does it threaten only civil suit?
- Flip it: did the other side send a defective notice? Is their claim barred or abated?

## Drill

Marisol owns a flooring company. A general contractor owes her $31,000 on a commercial job completed 70 days ago. She never sent a Notice to Owner. She emails the GC: "Pay in 5 days or I file a lien, call the owner, and report you to the state for fraud." The GC mails a check for $18,000 marked "full and final settlement of all claims," which her bookkeeper deposits. Marisol then deletes her old project emails to free up server space. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Lien rights.** As a subcontractor not in privity with the owner, Marisol needed to serve a Notice to Owner within 45 days of first furnishing. She did not, so her lien rights against the owner are likely lost. Her claim is a contract claim against the GC.
- **Threat.** "Report you to the state for fraud" to collect a debt is a threat of criminal exposure to gain advantage. That is extortion exposure under § 836.05. "Call the owner" risks tortious interference if she makes false statements.
- **Accord and satisfaction.** The check said "full and final settlement" conspicuously, the amount was disputed by the GC's conduct, and it was cashed. Under § 673.3111, the $31,000 claim may be discharged for $18,000. Unless she designated an address for disputed tenders, her remaining move is to repay the $18,000 within 90 days to undo the discharge, then sue for the full amount.
- **Spoliation.** Litigation was reasonably anticipated when she sent the demand. Deleting project emails afterward is at least negligent spoliation. Expect a sanctions motion and an adverse inference instruction.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the GC never disputed the amount before sending the check, there was no accord. If her invoices designated an address for disputed tenders and the check went elsewhere, the discharge fails.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. An employee emails her employer "you owe me $2,000 in unpaid wages" and sues under the Florida Minimum Wage Act the next week. What happens?
2. A business receives a "paid in full" check for a disputed invoice and writes "accepted under protest, balance still due" before depositing it. Is the balance preserved?
3. When does the duty to preserve evidence begin?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The Florida count is dismissed. The notice must identify the minimum wage claimed, hours, and amount, and the employer gets 15 days to pay. A bare email demanding $2,000 does not satisfy § 448.110(6). A federal FLSA count has no notice requirement.
2. No. Under § 673.3111 the UCC rule displaces the old "reservation of rights" approach. The protections are a designated address for such tenders or repayment within 90 days.
3. When litigation is reasonably anticipated, which can be a demand letter, a threatening email, an accident, or a known dispute, well before any complaint is filed.

</details>
