# Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad Co., 248 N.Y. 339, 162 N.E. 99 (N.Y. 1928)

**Tags:** `[US]` · torts · negligence · duty · proximate cause · foreseeability
**Used in:** [`04-torts/01-negligence.md`](../04-torts/01-negligence.md), [`04-torts/02-premises-liability.md`](../04-torts/02-premises-liability.md), [`04-torts/07-florida-2023-tort-reform-and-comparative-fault.md`](../04-torts/07-florida-2023-tort-reform-and-comparative-fault.md)

## One-line takeaway

A defendant is liable in negligence only to a plaintiff within the zone of foreseeable risk created by the defendant's conduct; carelessness toward one person is not a wrong to everyone it happens to injure.

## Facts

Helen Palsgraf was standing on a Long Island Railroad platform. A train bound elsewhere began to pull out. Two men ran to catch it. One, carrying a small package wrapped in newspaper, jumped aboard and seemed about to fall. A guard on the car reached out to pull him in and a guard on the platform pushed him from behind. The package fell to the rails and exploded. It contained fireworks. Nothing about it suggested that. The blast knocked over a set of scales at the other end of the platform, many feet away, and the scales struck Mrs. Palsgraf and injured her.

## Procedural posture

Palsgraf sued the railroad for negligence. A jury found for her. The Appellate Division affirmed by a 3 to 2 vote. The railroad appealed to the New York Court of Appeals, which reversed by a 4 to 3 vote and dismissed the complaint.

## Issue

Can a plaintiff recover in negligence when the defendant's conduct was careless toward someone else, but the risk of harm to the plaintiff was not foreseeable?

## Holding

No. Chief Judge Cardozo, for the majority, held that negligence is not a wrong in the abstract. It is a breach of a duty owed to a particular person. The guards may have been careless toward the man with the package. They were not careless toward Mrs. Palsgraf, because nothing in the situation gave notice that the package could harm anyone standing far away. "The risk reasonably to be perceived defines the duty to be obeyed." With no duty to her, there was no negligence as to her, and the question of proximate cause never arose.

## Reasoning

Cardozo framed the question as one of duty and relation. A plaintiff must show a wrong to herself, meaning a violation of her own right, not a wrong to someone else that happened to reach her. "Negligence in the air, so to speak, will not do." The guards had no reason to think the package was dangerous. That the package exploded did not turn ordinary conduct toward one passenger into a wrong toward another passenger standing out of any apparent danger.

The majority put the question of foreseeability at the front of the analysis, inside the element of duty, where it is decided by the judge. This has two effects. It lets courts cut off claims by unforeseeable plaintiffs before a jury hears them. And it treats the scope of liability as a question of the relationship between the parties rather than a question about the chain of events.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Judge Andrews dissented, joined by two others. He rejected the relational view. Everyone owes the world at large a duty to refrain from acts that unreasonably threaten the safety of others. When a defendant acts negligently and someone is hurt, the defendant has committed a wrong. The only question is proximate cause: whether the law will trace the consequences that far.

Andrews wrote: "What we do mean by the word 'proximate' is that, because of convenience, of public policy, of a rough sense of justice, the law arbitrarily declines to trace a series of events beyond a certain point." He offered the factors: was there a natural and continuous sequence between cause and effect, was the act a substantial factor in producing the result, was there a direct connection without too many intervening causes, was the result foreseeable in a general way, was the harm too remote in time and space. On these facts he would have left the jury's verdict alone. The explosion was close in time and space, and the injury flowed from it directly.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Negligence claims against a business are usually about customers, employees, and people on the premises. The question is who is inside the zone of risk your operation creates. A delivery driver's careless driving puts other drivers and pedestrians inside the zone. A locked loading dock does not put a trespasser's cousin inside it. When a claim arrives from someone outside the people your conduct could foreseeably reach, the duty argument is the first defense.

## Florida connection

Florida uses both halves of *Palsgraf*. In *McCain v. Florida Power Corp.*, 593 So. 2d 500 (Fla. 1992), the Florida Supreme Court explained that foreseeability plays two different roles. For duty, the question is whether the defendant's conduct created a foreseeable zone of risk; that is a question of law for the judge and tracks Cardozo. For proximate cause, the question is whether the specific injury was within the scope of that risk; that is a question of fact for the jury unless reasonable people could not differ, and it tracks Andrews. Florida's standard jury instruction defines legal cause as conduct that "directly and in natural and continuous sequence produces or contributes substantially to producing" the harm. Florida's 2023 tort reform did not change this framework; it shortened the limitation period to two years and adopted modified comparative fault (§ 768.81(6), Fla. Stat.).
