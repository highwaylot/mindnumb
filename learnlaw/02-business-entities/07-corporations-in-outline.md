# Corporations in outline

**Module:** 02 · **Lesson:** 7 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Most Florida small businesses are LLCs, but corporations are everywhere: the customer you sell to, the investor's preferred vehicle, the older family business, and the "S corp" your accountant mentions. The corporation runs on a fixed three-layer structure that the law enforces strictly. Knowing it lets you read a corporate counterparty's signature block, understand what an S election does and does not do, and recognize when your own LLC should have been a corporation.

## The rule

The Florida Business Corporation Act, ch. 607, Fla. Stat., governs. It was substantially revised effective January 1, 2020 (verify).

**Formation.** Articles of Incorporation filed with the Department of State, § 607.0202, stating the name, which must contain "corporation," "incorporated," "company," or an abbreviation, § 607.0401; the number of authorized shares; the principal office; the registered agent and acceptance; and the incorporator. Fee: $70 (verify). Annual report: $150 (verify), due January 1 to May 1, with a $400 (verify) late fee and administrative dissolution for non-filing. Bylaws, adopted by the incorporators or board, govern internal procedure and are not filed, § 607.0206.

**Three layers.**

1. **Shareholders** own the corporation and elect the directors, § 607.0803. They vote on fundamental changes: amending the articles, mergers, sale of substantially all assets, dissolution. They do not manage. Default voting is one share, one vote; a majority of shares is a quorum and a majority of votes cast decides, §§ 607.0725, 607.0726 (verify). Shareholders may act by written consent of the holders of the minimum votes needed, with notice to the others, unless the articles require otherwise, § 607.0704 (verify). Cumulative voting and preemptive rights exist only if the articles grant them.
2. **Directors** manage, or direct the management of, the business, § 607.0801. They act as a board, by majority at a meeting with a quorum, or by unanimous written consent, § 607.0821. Their standard of conduct is in § 607.0830: good faith, with the care of an ordinarily prudent person in a like position, in a manner reasonably believed to be in the corporation's best interests. Section 607.0831 limits personal liability to cases of criminal violation, improper personal benefit, unlawful distribution, conscious disregard of the corporation's interest, or recklessness or bad faith. Conflict-of-interest transactions are handled under § 607.0832 by disclosure and approval by disinterested directors or shareholders, or by proof of fairness.
3. **Officers** carry out the board's decisions and bind the corporation day to day. Titles and duties are set by the bylaws or the board, § 607.08401 (verify). One person may hold every office.

**Shareholder agreements.** In a closely held corporation, all shareholders may sign an agreement under § 607.0732 that eliminates or restricts the board, fixes who is a director or officer, governs distributions, sets voting arrangements, and provides for buyouts and deadlock. It must be in the articles or bylaws approved by all shareholders, or in a written agreement signed by all, and noted on share certificates. It is the corporate counterpart of the operating agreement.

**Shareholder rights.** Inspection of records on written demand, § 607.1602. Derivative suits on behalf of the corporation after demand, §§ 607.0741 to 607.0748 (verify). Appraisal rights to be bought out at fair value when the corporation takes certain fundamental actions over a dissenter's objection, §§ 607.1301 to 607.1340. Judicial dissolution under § 607.1430 for director or shareholder deadlock, waste of assets, or illegal or fraudulent conduct by those in control, with the corporation or other shareholders able to elect to purchase the petitioner's shares at fair value instead, § 607.1436.

**Tax.** A corporation is by default a **C corporation**: it pays federal corporate income tax and Florida's 5.5% (verify) corporate income tax on income above the first $50,000 (verify), and shareholders pay again on dividends. An **S corporation** has elected pass-through treatment on IRS Form 2553. Eligibility: 100 or fewer shareholders, all individuals, estates, or certain trusts, none nonresident aliens, and one class of stock. An S corporation generally pays no Florida corporate income tax. An LLC can make the same election, which is why "S corp" describes a tax status, not an entity type.

## How courts reason about it

Corporate law is older and more formal than LLC law, and courts enforce its structure strictly. Three themes.

**Directors decide, and courts defer to informed decisions.** The business judgment rule is at its strongest for a board. *Dodge v. Ford Motor Co.* set the frame: the court would not enjoin Ford's expansion plan because how to run the business was for the directors, but it ordered a dividend because Ford had announced a purpose that was not a business purpose. Courts read *Dodge* today less as a rule that corporations must maximize profit and more as a rule that directors must act for the corporation and its shareholders, with wide latitude on how. *Smith v. Van Gorkom* adds the condition: the deference is earned by an informed process. A board that approves a sale of the company in two hours on an oral presentation does not get it.

**Loyalty applies to officers as it does to directors.** *Guth v. Loft* involved a president, not just a director. Guth took the Pepsi opportunity using Loft's resources and lost it to Loft. The corporate opportunity doctrine asks whether the opportunity was in the corporation's line of business, whether it had an interest or expectancy, whether it could afford it, and whether taking it created a conflict. Florida courts apply the same factors.

**Form matters in a way it does not for LLCs.** Courts expect a corporation to have a board, hold meetings or act by consent, keep minutes, issue shares, and have officers sign in their capacities. A corporation that ignores this can still have its veil pierced only under the *Dania Jai-Alai* improper-conduct test, but the absence of records makes every other dispute harder: who owns what percentage, who was authorized to sign, whether the distribution was a dividend or a loan. Courts also enforce the statute's procedural requirements for fundamental actions. A merger approved without the required shareholder vote is voidable; a dissenting shareholder who was not given appraisal notice has a claim.

**Minority shareholders.** The majority elects the board, the board sets salaries and dividends, and the minority has no exit because closely held shares have no market. Courts respond through fiduciary duty, derivative suits, inspection rights, and judicial dissolution. Florida has no separate "oppression" ground for dissolution (verify), so a squeezed-out minority shareholder usually proceeds on fiduciary breach and waste, or negotiates under the threat of a dissolution petition that triggers the election to purchase under § 607.1436.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the Model Business Corporation Act, so its chapter 607 tracks most states' corporation statutes. Delaware law differs in places and is persuasive only.
- Florida has no separate statutory close corporation form. Closely held corporations use § 607.0732 shareholder agreements instead.
- Director exculpation is statutory in § 607.0831, rather than requiring an article provision as in Delaware after *Van Gorkom*.
- Professional corporations for licensed professions are under ch. 621, which limits ownership to licensed persons.
- Florida corporate income tax: ch. 220, Fla. Stat. The 5.5% rate and the $50,000 exemption are in the fact sheet. An S corporation files a Florida return only in limited cases (verify).
- Issuing shares is a securities offering under federal and Florida law. Module 07 covers exemptions.
- Veil piercing for corporations uses the *Dania Jai-Alai* test. Unlike § 605.0304(2) for LLCs, there is no statutory statement that formalities do not matter, but Florida courts have not pierced for formality failures alone.

## Angles checklist

- Is the counterparty a corporation, and did the signer hold an office with authority?
- Is there a shareholder agreement? Does it restrict transfers or fix who controls the board?
- Who are the directors of record on Sunbiz, and did the board approve the action in question?
- Was a fundamental action approved by the required shareholder vote? Were appraisal rights triggered?
- Is any director or officer on both sides of a transaction? Was it disclosed and approved?
- Is the corporation a C or S corporation, and does a proposed transfer of shares threaten S status?
- Is a minority shareholder being squeezed on salary or dividends?
- Are records, minutes, and share ledgers in order?

## Drill

Three siblings own a Pensacola marine supply corporation, 40/30/30. The eldest is president and sole director. He raises his salary, stops dividends, and signs a five-year lease of the company's warehouse to his own LLC at half market rent. The youngest sibling, who has not seen financial statements in three years, wants to sell her shares, but no buyer exists. List the corporate issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Board composition.** A sole director with 40% is lawful if the shareholders elected him, but the 60% held by the others can elect a different board at the next annual meeting, or by written consent under § 607.0704. The first question is why they have not.
- **Salary and dividends.** The director sets his own pay, a conflict under § 607.0832. With no disinterested approval, he must prove fairness. Withholding dividends while paying himself is the classic squeeze and supports a fiduciary claim.
- **The lease.** A self-dealing transaction at half market rent, not approved by disinterested shareholders. Voidable, and the director owes the difference. It is also potential waste supporting judicial dissolution under § 607.1430.
- **Inspection.** The youngest sibling has a statutory right to inspect financial records on written demand, § 607.1602. Three years of refusal supports a court order and fees.
- **Exit.** No appraisal right exists because no fundamental action has been taken. Her leverage is a dissolution petition, which lets the corporation or the other shareholders elect to buy her shares at fair value under § 607.1436, or a derivative suit for the lease and salary.
- **Open questions.** Is there a shareholder agreement? Have annual meetings been held? Is the corporation an S corporation, so that disproportionate distributions could threaten its status?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A corporation's vice president signs a contract to sell the company's only building. Is the corporation bound?
2. What does electing S corporation status change about a Florida corporation's liability shield?
3. Why might a startup that expects venture investment choose a C corporation over an LLC?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Probably not without board and shareholder approval. Sale of substantially all assets is a fundamental action requiring a shareholder vote, § 607.1202 (verify). An officer's apparent authority covers ordinary business, not the sale of the enterprise. The buyer should have required a board resolution.
2. Nothing. S status is a federal tax election. The shield under ch. 607 is the same for C and S corporations. What changes is pass-through taxation and the eligibility limits on who can hold shares.
3. Investors want preferred stock, which S corporations cannot issue, and many funds cannot hold S corporation shares or LLC interests. C corporation stock may also qualify for the federal qualified small business stock exclusion. The cost is double taxation on distributed profits, which a growth company does not plan to distribute.

</details>
