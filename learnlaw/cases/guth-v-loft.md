# Guth v. Loft, Inc., 5 A.2d 503 (Delaware Supreme Court 1939)

**Tags:** `[US]` · corporations · duty of loyalty · corporate opportunity · constructive trust
**Used in:** [02-business-entities/04-fiduciary-duties-and-business-judgment.md](../02-business-entities/04-fiduciary-duties-and-business-judgment.md), [02-business-entities/03-operating-agreements.md](../02-business-entities/03-operating-agreements.md), [02-business-entities/07-corporations-in-outline.md](../02-business-entities/07-corporations-in-outline.md)

## One-line takeaway

An officer or director who takes for himself a business opportunity that the corporation could afford, that is in its line of business, and in which it has an interest or expectancy, holds the resulting gains in trust for the corporation.

## Facts

Loft, Inc. ran a chain of candy and soda-fountain stores in the New York area. Charles Guth was its president and a director. Loft bought Coca-Cola syrup for its fountains, and Guth was annoyed by Coca-Cola's refusal to give Loft a jobber's discount. In 1931 Pepsi-Cola's trademark and secret formula were available out of the bankruptcy of the National Pepsi-Cola Company. Guth, with a syrup maker named Megargel, acquired them through a new corporation, Pepsi-Cola Company, in which Guth took a majority of the stock. He did not offer the opportunity to Loft's board. He then used Loft's money, credit, plant, employees, and executives to develop and make Pepsi syrup, and switched Loft's stores from Coca-Cola to Pepsi. Pepsi became highly valuable.

## Procedural posture

Loft sued Guth in the Delaware Court of Chancery, asking that Guth's Pepsi stock be impressed with a constructive trust in Loft's favor and that Guth account for profits. The Chancellor found for Loft. Guth appealed to the Delaware Supreme Court.

## Issue

Did Guth breach his duty of loyalty to Loft by acquiring Pepsi-Cola for himself and developing it with Loft's resources, so that equity should treat the Pepsi stock as held for Loft?

## Holding

Yes. The decree was affirmed. The Pepsi opportunity belonged to Loft, Guth had seized it in breach of his fiduciary duty, and the stock and its profits were held in constructive trust for Loft.

## Reasoning

The court stated the general rule first. Corporate officers and directors may not use their position of trust to further their private interests. The rule demands "undivided and unselfish loyalty." The court then gave the test that bears the case's name. If a business opportunity is presented to an officer or director, the corporation is financially able to undertake it, it is in the corporation's line of business and of practical advantage to it, the corporation has an interest or reasonable expectancy in it, and taking it personally would bring the fiduciary's self-interest into conflict with the corporation's, then the law will not permit him to take it. Conversely, if the opportunity comes to him personally, the corporation has no interest or expectancy, and he does not use corporate resources, he may take it, so long as he does so in good faith.

Applied to the facts, every element pointed one way. Loft had a cola problem and a large syrup business, so Pepsi was squarely within its line. Loft could have afforded the purchase. The opportunity came to Guth through his position and his frustration with Coca-Cola on Loft's behalf. And he did not merely take the opportunity; he built it with Loft's cash, staff, and plant. Guth's argument that the offer came to him personally was rejected because, even if it had, his use of Loft's resources and his conflict of interest brought it within the rule. The court also noted that Guth bore the burden of proving the fairness of his conduct and had not carried it.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The strongest argument for Guth was the one he made: the opportunity was offered to him personally, Loft was a candy retailer rather than a syrup manufacturer, and Loft's board could have objected at any time. Later Delaware cases softened the test somewhat. In *Broz v. Cellular Information Systems* (1996) the court made clear that a fiduciary need not always present an opportunity formally, and that financial inability of the corporation and the absence of corporate interest can defeat the claim.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

This case defines what a manager or active owner may and may not do on the side. If a deal arrives that relates to your company's business, the safe course is to present it to the other owners in writing and get a documented waiver before taking it. The risk is not just damages. The remedy is a constructive trust: the company takes the new business, including its growth. The practical fix is an operating agreement that defines the company's line of business, states whether members may pursue outside ventures, and sets out a procedure for presenting opportunities.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the majority rule. For LLCs the duty is codified. Section 605.04091(2)(a), Fla. Stat., requires a member of a member-managed LLC, or a manager, to account to the company for any benefit derived from the use of company property or from the appropriation of a company opportunity. Under § 605.0105, the operating agreement may identify specific types or categories of activities that do not violate the duty of loyalty if not manifestly unreasonable, which is how outside ventures are usually carved out (verify the subsection). For corporations, § 607.0832 governs director conflict-of-interest transactions and provides a safe harbor through disinterested approval or fairness (verify the section as renumbered in the 2020 revision). Federal courts applying Florida law have used the *Guth* line-of-business test; see *Farber v. Servan Land Co.*, 662 F.2d 371 (5th Cir. Unit B 1981) (verify citation).
