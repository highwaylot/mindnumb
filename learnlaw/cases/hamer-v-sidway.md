# Hamer v. Sidway, 124 N.Y. 538 (New York Court of Appeals 1891)

**Tags:** `[US]` · contracts · consideration · forbearance · gratuitous promises
**Used in:** [03-contracts/02-consideration-and-promissory-estoppel.md](../03-contracts/02-consideration-and-promissory-estoppel.md), [03-contracts/01-formation.md](../03-contracts/01-formation.md)

## One-line takeaway

Giving up a legal right at the promisor's request is consideration, whether or not the forbearance benefits the promisor and whether or not it is good for the promisee.

## Facts

In 1869, at a family celebration, William E. Story Sr. promised his nephew, William E. Story II, then fifteen, that if the nephew refrained from drinking liquor, using tobacco, swearing, and playing cards or billiards for money until he turned twenty-one, the uncle would pay him $5,000. The nephew agreed and kept the bargain. When he turned twenty-one in 1875 he wrote to his uncle to say he had earned the money. The uncle wrote back that the nephew had, that the money was his, and that the uncle would hold it with interest until the nephew was older and could use it wisely. The nephew agreed to that. The uncle died in 1887 without paying. The nephew had meanwhile assigned the claim to his wife, who assigned it to Louisa Hamer.

## Procedural posture

Hamer sued Franklin Sidway, the executor of the uncle's estate, on the promise. The trial court found for Hamer. The General Term of the Supreme Court reversed, reasoning that the nephew had given nothing of value because refraining from those habits benefited him and cost the uncle nothing. Hamer appealed to the Court of Appeals.

## Issue

Is a promisee's forbearance from lawful conduct, at the promisor's request, sufficient consideration for the promise even though the forbearance was good for the promisee and gave the promisor no tangible benefit?

## Holding

Yes. The nephew's abandonment of his legal right to drink, smoke, swear, and gamble, for a period of years at his uncle's request, was consideration. The judgment of the General Term was reversed and the trial court's judgment for Hamer reinstated.

## Reasoning

Judge Parker rejected the idea that the court must weigh the exchange. Consideration is a right, interest, profit, or benefit accruing to one party, or some forbearance, detriment, loss, or responsibility given, suffered, or undertaken by the other. It is enough that something is promised, done, forborne, or suffered by the promisee as consideration for the promise. Courts will not ask whether the thing that was given up was actually of benefit to the party who gave it up, or whether it was of any substantial value to the promisor. The nephew had a legal right to drink and smoke. He gave that right up for several years because his uncle asked him to. That restriction of his lawful freedom was a detriment, and it was enough.

The court also dealt with the statute of limitations. The uncle's 1875 letter, accepted by the nephew, converted the obligation into one in which the uncle held the money for the nephew with interest. The claim therefore did not accrue, and the limitation period did not start, until the uncle's death and the estate's refusal.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The executor's best argument, which persuaded the General Term, was that the promise was a gift dressed up as a bargain. The uncle wanted his nephew to live well; he got nothing for himself; the nephew lost nothing he valued. Many family promises are like this, and courts generally refuse to enforce them. The Court of Appeals' answer was that the test is legal detriment, not economic loss, and that the uncle plainly asked for the forbearance in exchange for the money. A court that weighed the adequacy of consideration would be rewriting bargains.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

The rule that courts do not weigh consideration cuts in your favor and against you. In your favor: a small or nominal exchange will usually support a promise, which is why releases, option agreements, and confidentiality agreements recite "ten dollars and other good and valuable consideration." Against you: a promise you make in exchange for someone's forbearance, such as a vendor's agreement not to sue if you promise to pay next quarter, is enforceable even if the forbearance cost them nothing you can see. The case is also the clearest statement of what consideration is not. A promise to pay for something already done, or a promise with nothing asked in return, is a gift, and gifts are not enforced as contracts. Promissory estoppel, covered in *Hoffman v. Red Owl*, is the limited exception.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the majority rule. Florida courts hold that a promise, no matter how slight, is consideration if the promisor agrees to do or refrain from something he or she is not already obligated to do, and they do not inquire into the adequacy of consideration absent fraud or unconscionability. See *Cintas Corp. No. 2 v. Schwalier*, 901 So. 2d 307 (Fla. 1st DCA 2005) (verify citation). Florida also follows the pre-existing duty rule: doing what you were already bound to do is not consideration for a new promise, which matters in contract modifications. Under the UCC as adopted in Florida, § 672.209, Fla. Stat., a modification of a contract for the sale of goods needs no consideration to be binding, which is the main statutory departure. Florida's statute of limitations for a written promise is five years and for an oral one four years under § 95.11, Fla. Stat. (verify), so a long-deferred family promise like this one would face a limitations defense here as well.
