# Olmstead v. FTC, 44 So. 3d 76 (Florida Supreme Court 2010)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · LLC · charging orders · single-member LLC · judgment collection · asset protection
**Used in:** [02-business-entities/06-charging-orders-and-transfers.md](../02-business-entities/06-charging-orders-and-transfers.md), [08-disputes-procedure/06-collecting-a-judgment.md](../08-disputes-procedure/06-collecting-a-judgment.md), [08-disputes-procedure/07-asset-protection-basics.md](../08-disputes-procedure/07-asset-protection-basics.md)

## One-line takeaway

Under Florida's old LLC statute a charging order was not the exclusive remedy against a single-member LLC, so a court could order the member to surrender the whole interest; the Legislature then codified a narrower version of that result in § 605.0503.

## Facts

Shaun Olmstead and Julie Connell ran an advance-fee credit card scheme. The Federal Trade Commission sued them in federal court in Florida and obtained a judgment for more than $10 million. Each defendant was the sole member of several Florida single-member LLCs. The district court ordered them to endorse and surrender all of their right, title, and interest in those LLCs to a receiver so the assets could be applied to the judgment.

## Procedural posture

The defendants appealed to the Eleventh Circuit, arguing that under Florida's then-current LLC Act, § 608.433(4), Fla. Stat., a charging order was a judgment creditor's exclusive remedy against a member's interest, so the district court could not order surrender of the interests. The Eleventh Circuit found no controlling Florida authority and certified the question to the Florida Supreme Court. The Florida Supreme Court rephrased the question as whether, under Florida law, a court may order a judgment debtor to surrender all right, title, and interest in a single-member LLC to satisfy an outstanding judgment.

## Issue

Was the charging order remedy in § 608.433(4) the exclusive remedy of a judgment creditor against the membership interest of a single-member Florida LLC?

## Holding

No. A court may order a judgment debtor to surrender all right, title, and interest in the debtor's single-member LLC to satisfy a judgment. The statute's charging order provision did not state that it was exclusive, and the general execution statute, § 56.061, Fla. Stat., reaches corporate stock and other personal property, which includes an LLC membership interest.

## Reasoning

Justice Canady wrote for the majority. The reasoning moved in three steps.

First, an LLC interest is personal property subject to the ordinary rules of execution. Section 56.061 lists stock and other property as subject to levy. Nothing exempted LLC interests.

Second, the charging order provision did not displace that rule. The Court compared the LLC Act with Florida's partnership statutes, which said expressly that a charging order was the exclusive remedy. The LLC Act had no such language. The Court declined to read exclusivity into a statute that did not say it.

Third, the usual reason for the charging order limitation did not apply. A charging order protects the other members of an entity from being forced into business with a stranger. It does that by giving the creditor only the debtor's economic rights and leaving the management rights with the member. The transfer restriction in § 608.432 required the consent of all other members before an assignee could become a member. In a single-member LLC there are no other members to protect, and the sole member's consent is the only consent needed. A creditor who takes the whole interest therefore takes everything, including management rights, without impairing anyone else's bargain.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Justice Lewis dissented, joined by two justices (verify). He argued that the majority had rewritten the statute. The Legislature had chosen the charging order as the remedy for every LLC and had not distinguished single-member LLCs. The majority's reasoning also, in his view, threatened multi-member LLCs because its textual argument about the absence of "exclusive" applied equally to them. He would have answered the certified question in the affirmative and left the policy choice to the Legislature.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Many owners form a single-member LLC in the belief that it shields the business from their personal creditors. This case and the statute that followed it show the limit. The LLC protects the owner from the company's debts. It does not reliably protect the company from the owner's debts when the owner is the only member. A personal judgment creditor can, in the right conditions, take the entire interest and with it control of the business.

For multi-member LLCs the charging order remains the creditor's only route against the member's interest. Whether a second member is a real member with a real economic interest, or a token added to defeat creditors, is exactly the kind of fact a court will examine.

## Florida connection

The Legislature responded in 2011 by amending § 608.433, and the rule now lives in § 605.0503, Fla. Stat., of the Florida Revised LLC Act. For a multi-member LLC, a charging order is the sole and exclusive remedy by which a judgment creditor may satisfy a judgment from the debtor's interest. For a single-member LLC, the statute keeps the *Olmstead* result in a narrower form: if a judgment creditor shows that distributions under a charging order will not satisfy the judgment within a reasonable time, the court may order foreclosure of the interest, and the purchaser at the sale becomes the member and obtains the debtor's full rights. The statute also says that it does not limit remedies available under other law with respect to the debtor's own fraudulent transfers. Read § 605.0503 in full before relying on any summary; it has several subsections and the single-member rule has conditions.
