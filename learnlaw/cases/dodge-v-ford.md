# Dodge v. Ford Motor Co., 204 Mich. 459 (Michigan Supreme Court 1919)

**Tags:** `[US]` · corporations · shareholder primacy · dividends · business judgment rule · minority shareholders
**Used in:** [02-business-entities/04-fiduciary-duties-and-business-judgment.md](../02-business-entities/04-fiduciary-duties-and-business-judgment.md), [02-business-entities/07-corporations-in-outline.md](../02-business-entities/07-corporations-in-outline.md), [02-business-entities/03-operating-agreements.md](../02-business-entities/03-operating-agreements.md)

## One-line takeaway

A corporation is run for the profit of its shareholders, so directors may not withhold dividends from a huge surplus to pursue other ends, but courts will not second-guess their business decisions about expansion.

## Facts

By 1916 Ford Motor Company was enormously profitable. It had a surplus of about $112 million and cash of over $50 million. Henry Ford held 58 percent of the stock. John and Horace Dodge held 10 percent and had been receiving large special dividends, which they were using to build their own car company. Ford announced that the company would stop paying special dividends, would cut the price of the Model T from $440 to $360, and would spend the money on a vast expansion, including the River Rouge plant and a smelter. He said publicly that the company was making too much money, that his ambition was to employ more men and spread the benefits of the industrial system, and that shareholders should be content with a modest return.

## Procedural posture

The Dodge brothers sued in the Wayne County Circuit Court to compel a dividend and to enjoin the expansion. The trial court ordered a special dividend of about $19 million and enjoined construction of the smelter and related projects. Ford appealed to the Michigan Supreme Court.

## Issue

May directors refuse to declare a dividend out of a large accumulated surplus in order to reduce prices and expand the business for the benefit of employees and the public, and may a court enjoin the expansion itself?

## Holding

The dividend order was affirmed. The refusal to pay a dividend was arbitrary in light of the surplus and Ford's stated reasons. The injunction against expansion was reversed. Whether to build the plant was a business decision for the directors, and the court would not substitute its judgment for theirs.

## Reasoning

Justice Ostrander wrote for the court. The famous passage states that a business corporation is organized and carried on primarily for the profit of the stockholders, that the directors' powers are to be used to that end, and that the directors' discretion extends to the choice of means to attain it but not to a change of the end itself. Directors may not reduce profits or withhold them in order to benefit others at the shareholders' expense.

On the dividend, the court took Ford at his word. He had testified that the company could not use the surplus in the business and that the purpose of the price cut was to spread benefits rather than to make money. Given cash on hand far in excess of any planned expenditure, the refusal to distribute any of it was not a good-faith business judgment. The court ordered distribution of about half the available cash, leaving ample funds for expansion.

On the expansion, the court applied what is now called the business judgment rule. The judges were not business experts. Expanding capacity, integrating production, and lowering prices to increase volume were legitimate business strategies that might increase profit in the long run. The court would not enjoin them.

## Dissent or counterpoints

There was no dissent. The counterpoints are historical and modern. Historically, Ford's testimony was probably posturing. He was trying to squeeze out the Dodges, who were using Ford dividends to fund a competitor, and he bought them out soon after. Modern commentators argue that the shareholder primacy passage is dicta, that the actual holding is narrow, and that most states now allow directors to consider other constituencies. Delaware and many other courts nevertheless cite the case for the proposition that the board's duty runs to the shareholders. The dividend holding is also unusual. Courts almost never order a dividend, and the case is remembered more for its language than for its remedy.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Two lessons. First, minority owners have very limited power to force distributions. In an LLC or a closely held corporation, the only reliable protection is a written agreement that says when distributions must be made, for example to cover tax on pass-through income. Second, the majority's discretion is wide but not unlimited. An owner who openly says the company is withholding distributions to punish or starve a minority owner is handing that owner a lawsuit.

## Florida connection

Florida follows the majority rule on both points. Directors of a Florida corporation must act in good faith and in a manner reasonably believed to be in the best interests of the corporation under § 607.0830, Fla. Stat. Distributions are governed by § 607.06401, which limits them to amounts that leave the corporation solvent; it does not require them. For LLCs, § 605.0404, Fla. Stat., provides that a member has no right to a distribution before dissolution except as the LLC decides or the operating agreement provides, and § 605.0405 limits distributions that would leave the LLC unable to pay its debts. Florida courts apply the business judgment rule to corporate decisions, and Florida recognizes claims by minority shareholders in closely held companies for oppression through the judicial dissolution remedy in § 607.1430 and the LLC counterpart in § 605.0702 (verify both). The duty runs to the entity and its owners, not to employees or the public.

## Test yourself

1. Change one fact. Ford testified that the company needed every dollar of surplus to fund the River Rouge plant and that the price cut was expected to increase total profit. Does the outcome change?
2. What would the Dodges have needed to show to win the injunction against the expansion?
3. Where would this rule bite in a small business? Think about a two-member LLC where the managing member pays himself a large salary and never declares a distribution.
