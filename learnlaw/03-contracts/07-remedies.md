# Contract remedies

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 7 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

A breach is only worth what you can recover. The law of remedies decides whether a lost contract is worth the profit you expected, only the money you spent, or nothing provable. It decides whether a liquidated damages clause will hold, whether a cap in your vendor's form binds you, and whether you can force performance rather than payment. Thinking about remedies first is how lawyers decide whether a case is worth filing.

## The rule

Contract damages compensate. They do not punish. The injured party must prove the amount with reasonable certainty.

**Three measures.**

1. **Expectation.** The default. Enough money to put the injured party where it would have been had the contract been performed: the value of the promised performance, minus what the injured party saved by not having to perform, plus incidental and consequential losses.
2. **Reliance.** Money spent in preparing to perform or in performing, when expectation cannot be proven or the plaintiff chooses it. It puts the plaintiff back where it started.
3. **Restitution.** The value of the benefit the plaintiff conferred on the breaching party.

**Limits on damages.**

- **Foreseeability.** Consequential damages are recoverable only if, at the time of contracting, the breaching party had reason to foresee them as a probable result of breach. This is *Hadley v. Baxendale*. Losses that arise naturally from the breach are always foreseeable. Special losses must have been communicated.
- **Certainty.** Lost profits must be proven with reasonable certainty, not speculation. The amount need not be exact; the fact of loss must be established.
- **Mitigation.** The injured party cannot recover losses it could have avoided with reasonable effort. The burden of proving failure to mitigate is on the breaching party.
- **Causation.** The loss must have been caused by the breach.

**Agreed remedies.**

- **Liquidated damages.** A clause fixing damages in advance is enforceable if damages were difficult to estimate at contracting and the amount is a reasonable forecast, not a penalty. A penalty is void.
- **Limitation of liability and exclusion of consequential damages.** Generally enforceable between businesses unless unconscionable or the limited remedy fails of its essential purpose. UCC § 672.719, Fla. Stat.
- **Attorney fees.** Not recoverable without a contract clause or statute. Florida makes one-sided fee clauses reciprocal. § 57.105(7).

**Equitable remedies.**

- **Specific performance.** An order to perform, available when damages are inadequate because the subject matter is unique. Land is presumed unique. Personal services contracts are never specifically enforced.
- **Injunction.** An order not to do something, such as not to compete or disclose.
- **Rescission and restitution.** Unwinding the contract where there is a defense to formation.

Punitive damages are not available for breach of contract. They require an independent tort.

## How courts reason about it

*Hadley v. Baxendale* is the organizing case. A mill's crankshaft broke. The carrier delayed delivering it for repair. The mill lost days of profit. The court held the carrier was not liable for the lost profits because it had no reason to know the mill was shut down waiting on the shaft. The policy is about pricing risk. A carrier who knows a shipment is critical can charge more or decline. One who does not know cannot be made the insurer of the shipper's business.

Courts apply the same thinking across remedies.

- **Lost profits and the new business.** Older cases barred lost profits for a new business as speculative. Most courts, including Florida, now allow them if the plaintiff offers a reliable method of proof, such as comparable businesses or a track record elsewhere.
- **Cost of completion versus diminution in value.** When a builder performs defectively, the measure is normally the cost to fix it. Where fixing it would cost far more than the value it adds, courts award the loss in value instead, as in *Jacob & Youngs*.
- **Liquidated damages.** Courts look at the clause from the moment of contracting. Was the harm hard to measure? Was the sum a reasonable forecast? A clause that sets the same sum for a trivial breach and a total one looks like a penalty. So does a clause that lets the non-breaching party choose between the liquidated sum and actual damages, because that is a floor, not an estimate.
- **Mitigation.** An employer who fires an employee without cause owes the salary for the term, less what the employee earned or reasonably could have earned elsewhere. A seller whose buyer cancels must try to resell.
- **Limitation clauses.** Courts enforce them between sophisticated parties but strike them when the limited remedy leaves the buyer with nothing, such as a repair-or-replace remedy on a product the seller cannot fix, or when the clause would excuse fraud.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on expectation damages, *Hadley* foreseeability, mitigation, and the bar on punitive damages for breach of contract.

- **Liquidated damages.** *Lefemine v. Baron*, 573 So. 2d 326 (Fla. 1991), sets the test: damages must not have been readily ascertainable at contracting, and the sum must not be grossly disproportionate to the damages that might reasonably be expected. *Lefemine* struck a clause that gave the seller the option of keeping the deposit or suing for actual damages, because the option showed the deposit was not a genuine pre-estimate.
- **Lost profits for a new business.** Permitted if proven with reasonable certainty. *W.W. Gay Mechanical Contractor, Inc. v. Wharfside Two, Ltd.*, 545 So. 2d 1348 (Fla. 1989).
- **Prejudgment interest** on contract damages runs from the date the loss was incurred, as a matter of right, once the amount is fixed. *Argonaut Insurance Co. v. May Plumbing Co.*, 474 So. 2d 212 (Fla. 1985). The statutory rate is set quarterly by the Chief Financial Officer under § 55.03 (verify).
- **Attorney fees.** Florida follows the American rule. Section 57.105(7) makes a contractual fee provision that favors one party apply equally to the other. If your vendor's form says the vendor gets fees if it wins, you get fees if you win. Fee clauses are enforced as written and the "prevailing party" is the one who prevailed on the significant issues, *Moritz v. Hoyt Enterprises, Inc.*, 604 So. 2d 807 (Fla. 1992).
- **Limitation of liability clauses** are enforceable in Florida between commercial parties and are read according to their plain terms. They do not shield intentional torts or fraud.
- **Specific performance** is available for land and unique goods. Florida will not specifically enforce personal services contracts but will enjoin breach of a valid non-compete under § 542.335.
- **Limitation periods.** Five years (verify) for a written contract, four (verify) for oral, running from breach. § 95.11.

## Angles checklist

- What would the injured party have gained had the contract been performed? Can that be proven with documents?
- What did it spend in reliance? What did it save by not performing?
- What did the breaching party know at contracting about the consequences of breach?
- What steps could the injured party have taken to limit the loss? Did it take them?
- Is there a liquidated damages clause, a cap, a consequential damages waiver, or a fee clause? Does § 57.105(7) make the fee clause reciprocal?
- Is the subject unique enough for specific performance?
- Is there an independent tort that opens punitive damages?
- Flip it: can the other side show the loss was speculative, unforeseeable, or avoidable?
- Procedure: amount in controversy decides county or circuit court; proposals for settlement can shift fees.

## Drill

An Orlando caterer contracts to supply a tech company's product launch for $25,000 (verify). The contract says the caterer is liable for "no more than the amount paid under this agreement" and excludes consequential damages. The caterer no-shows. The company scrambles, pays a replacement $38,000, and the launch runs two hours late. The company claims the $13,000 difference, $20,000 in lost press coverage, and attorney fees under the contract's clause saying "caterer shall recover its fees in any action to collect amounts due." Analyze.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Cover damages.** The $13,000 difference is a direct expectation loss: the cost to obtain substitute performance minus the contract price. It arises naturally from the breach and is foreseeable under *Hadley*.
- **Lost press coverage.** This is consequential. Was it foreseeable at contracting that a no-show would cost press value? Probably in general terms, since the caterer knew it was a launch. But the contract excludes consequential damages and Florida enforces such exclusions between businesses. The $20,000 is also hard to prove with reasonable certainty. Likely not recoverable.
- **The cap.** "No more than the amount paid" caps liability at what the company paid, which may be the deposit or nothing if payment was due after the event. If the cap reduces recovery to zero, the company will argue the limited remedy fails of its essential purpose, or that the cap is unconscionable. Florida courts generally enforce caps as written, so the drafting matters.
- **Attorney fees.** The clause favors the caterer only. Section 57.105(7) makes it reciprocal, so the company can recover fees if it prevails, but only in an action within the clause's scope, here "to collect amounts due." Courts read reciprocity to cover the same kind of action. Expect a fight over scope.
- **Likely outcome.** The company recovers the $13,000 difference, subject to the cap, plus prejudgment interest and possibly fees. No consequential damages. No punitive damages.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the contract had no cap or exclusion and the caterer knew specific media were attending, the lost coverage claim would turn on proof of amount. A deliberate no-show is still only a breach, so the cap likely holds.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A commercial tenant abandons with three years left on the lease. Must the landlord try to relet?
2. A contract for a custom-built restaurant hood sets liquidated damages of $500 per day of late delivery. Enforceable?
3. Can a buyer get specific performance of a contract to buy a warehouse?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Generally yes. Florida case law requires a landlord who retakes possession to make reasonable efforts to relet, with the result depending on which remedy the landlord elects under § 83.595 (verify). A landlord who leaves the space dark and sues for all remaining rent risks a mitigation defense.
2. Likely yes under *Lefemine*, if daily losses from a late hood were hard to estimate at contracting and $500 is a reasonable forecast of lost restaurant revenue. It becomes a penalty if $500 bears no relation to likely loss or if the clause also lets the buyer sue for actual damages.
3. Yes. Real property is presumed unique, so damages are inadequate and specific performance is the normal remedy for a seller's breach of a land contract, provided the buyer was ready, willing, and able to close.

</details>
