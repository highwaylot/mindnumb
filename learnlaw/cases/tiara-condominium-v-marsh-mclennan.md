# Tiara Condominium Ass'n v. Marsh & McLennan Cos., 110 So. 3d 399 (Fla. 2013)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · torts · economic loss rule · contract versus tort · independent tort doctrine
**Used in:** [`04-torts/06-economic-loss-rule.md`](../04-torts/06-economic-loss-rule.md), [`04-torts/01-negligence.md`](../04-torts/01-negligence.md), [`04-torts/03-fraud-and-misrepresentation.md`](../04-torts/03-fraud-and-misrepresentation.md), [`03-contracts/07-remedies.md`](../03-contracts/07-remedies.md)

## One-line takeaway

In Florida the economic loss rule applies only in products liability cases; it no longer bars tort claims between parties in contractual privity, but a tort claim must still be independent of the breach of contract.

## Facts

Tiara Condominium Association hired Marsh & McLennan as its insurance broker to obtain property coverage for its high-rise. Marsh obtained a policy from Citizens Property Insurance with a limit of roughly $50 million. In 2004 Hurricanes Frances and Jeanne hit the building. Tiara began remediation. Marsh told Tiara the policy limit was per occurrence, so two storms meant about $100 million of coverage. Tiara spent more than $100 million on repairs. Citizens took the position that the limit was aggregate, and after litigation Tiara settled with Citizens for about $89 million.

## Procedural posture

Tiara sued Marsh in the Southern District of Florida for breach of contract, negligent misrepresentation, breach of the implied covenant of good faith, negligence, and breach of fiduciary duty. The district court granted summary judgment to Marsh on every claim. It held that the negligence and fiduciary duty claims were barred by the economic loss rule because the parties were in privity and Tiara sought only economic damages. On appeal, the Eleventh Circuit affirmed on the contract-based claims but certified a question to the Florida Supreme Court on whether the economic loss rule barred the negligence and fiduciary duty claims.

## Issue

Does Florida's economic loss rule bar a tort claim for purely economic loss between parties in contractual privity, outside the products liability context?

## Holding

No. The court held that the economic loss rule applies only in the products liability context. It receded from its prior decisions to the extent they had extended the rule to cases involving contractual privity.

## Reasoning

Justice Labarga traced the rule's history. It began as a products liability doctrine: when a defective product damages only itself, the buyer's remedy is in contract and warranty, not tort. *East River Steamship Corp. v. Transamerica Delaval*, 476 U.S. 858 (1986). Florida adopted that version in *Florida Power & Light Co. v. Westinghouse Electric Corp.*, 510 So. 2d 899 (Fla. 1987). *AFM Corp. v. Southern Bell*, 515 So. 2d 180 (Fla. 1987), then applied it to a service contract.

The court then described its own retreat. *Moransais v. Heathman*, 744 So. 2d 973 (Fla. 1999), held the rule did not bar a professional negligence claim against an engineer and warned that the rule had been expanded beyond its principled basis. *Indemnity Insurance Co. v. American Aviation, Inc.*, 891 So. 2d 532 (Fla. 2004), tried to limit the rule to two situations, products liability and contractual privity, with exceptions for professional malpractice, fraudulent inducement, and negligent misrepresentation.

The court concluded that the rule had never been needed in the privity context. The simplest course was to return the rule to its origin: products liability only.

## Dissent or counterpoints

Justice Pariente concurred, joined by Justice Labarga and Justice Perry, to explain what the decision did not do. Florida's common law has always required that a tort claim between contracting parties be independent of the breach of contract. *Lewis v. Guthartz*, 428 So. 2d 222 (Fla. 1982). A plaintiff cannot recast a breach of contract as negligence. The concurrence said that principle survives, so the decision does not open the door to tort claims that merely repeat the contract claim.

Justice Canady dissented, joined by Justice Polston. He argued that the majority had dismantled a sound rule that kept contract disputes in contract, that the question could have been answered within the existing professional services exception, and that the decision would expand tort liability and generate confusion.

Courts after *Tiara* follow the concurrence. Florida trial courts and the Eleventh Circuit routinely dismiss negligence, fiduciary duty, and interference claims that depend entirely on the duties the contract created, calling it the independent tort doctrine. *Peebles v. Puig*, 223 So. 3d 1065 (Fla. 3d DCA 2017) (verify citation).

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Two practical consequences. First, if you provide services under a contract, a dissatisfied customer may now plead negligence, negligent misrepresentation, and breach of fiduciary duty alongside breach of contract. The economic loss rule will not get those claims dismissed. The independent tort doctrine might, if the tort claim does nothing more than restate the contract claim. Second, tort claims carry remedies contract claims do not: punitive damages, and in fraud cases, rescission. A clause disclaiming reliance on statements outside the contract is a key defense against negligent misrepresentation claims.

## Florida connection

This is Florida law. *Tiara* binds every Florida court on the scope of the economic loss rule. The rule still applies in products cases: a product that damages only itself gives rise to contract and warranty claims, not tort. Outside products, the controlling questions are whether the plaintiff pleaded a tort that is independent of the contract and whether the damages sought are separate from contract damages. The four-year limitation period for negligence became two years for claims accruing after March 24, 2023 (verify), which is shorter than the five years for a written contract, so the choice of theory also changes the deadline.
