# Dania Jai-Alai Palace, Inc. v. Sykes, 450 So. 2d 1114 (Florida Supreme Court 1984)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · limited liability · veil piercing · parent and subsidiary · improper conduct
**Used in:** [02-business-entities/05-limited-liability-and-veil-piercing.md](../02-business-entities/05-limited-liability-and-veil-piercing.md), [02-business-entities/02-forming-a-florida-llc.md](../02-business-entities/02-forming-a-florida-llc.md), [08-disputes-procedure/07-asset-protection-basics.md](../08-disputes-procedure/07-asset-protection-basics.md)

## One-line takeaway

In Florida the corporate veil cannot be pierced, even between a parent and a subsidiary run as one operation, without a showing that the corporate form was used for an improper purpose.

## Facts

Dania Jai-Alai Palace operated a jai-alai fronton in Dania, Florida. Valet parking at the fronton was run by Carrousel Concessions, Inc. Both companies were wholly owned subsidiaries of the same parent, Saturday Corporation. The companies shared officers and were managed as parts of one operation. Sykes, a patron, was injured in the parking lot when a Carrousel valet attendant drove a car into her. There was no evidence that the corporate structure was set up or used to defraud anyone or to work an injustice on her.

## Procedural posture

Sykes sued Dania and Carrousel for negligence. The jury returned a verdict against both. Dania argued that the attendant was Carrousel's employee, not its own, so it was not liable. The Fourth District Court of Appeal affirmed, reasoning that because the corporations were operated with so little separateness, the corporate entities could be disregarded and Dania held liable for Carrousel's employee. The Florida Supreme Court accepted jurisdiction because the DCA's decision conflicted with earlier Florida Supreme Court decisions on veil piercing.

## Issue

May a Florida court disregard the separateness of affiliated corporations, and hold one liable for the other's torts, based on common ownership and intertwined operations without any showing that the corporate form was used improperly?

## Holding

No. The Court reaffirmed its earlier decisions, *Advertects, Inc. v. Sawyer Industries, Inc.*, 84 So. 2d 21 (Fla. 1955), and *Roberts' Fish Farm v. Spencer*, 153 So. 2d 718 (Fla. 1963): the corporate veil may not be pierced absent a showing of improper conduct. The decision of the Fourth DCA was quashed (verify the precise disposition on remand as to each defendant).

## Reasoning

The Court treated the question as settled by its own precedent. *Advertects* had held that a court will not disregard the corporate entity unless it is shown that the corporation was organized or used to mislead creditors or to perpetrate a fraud. *Roberts' Fish Farm* restated the point. The DCA had instead applied a "mere instrumentality" approach drawn from other jurisdictions, under which domination and lack of separateness alone could justify piercing. The Court rejected that approach as inconsistent with Florida law.

The reasoning rests on the purpose of limited liability. The legislature permits incorporation precisely so that owners can limit their exposure. Common ownership, shared management, and shared facilities are normal features of corporate groups. If those features were enough to pierce, limited liability would be an illusion for any business with more than one entity. The plaintiff must show something more: that the corporate form was used to accomplish a fraud, an injustice, or some other improper end, and that the injury flowed from that misuse.

The Court also noted that the plaintiff had other routes. She could have shown Dania's own negligence, or an agency relationship under which Dania was responsible for the valet operation, without piercing anything.

## Dissent or counterpoints

The strongest counterpoint is the Fourth DCA's reasoning. Where two corporations are run as one, with the same people and no real accounting between them, the public reasonably treats them as one. Requiring an injured patron to prove the owners' motive for the structure puts the burden on the party with the least information. Florida does not (verify whether any justice dissented).

## Why it matters for a Florida business

This is the single most important Florida case for an owner who formed an LLC to protect personal assets. The shield is strong. A creditor who wants to reach you personally must prove not only that you dominated the company but that you used it for an improper purpose and that the misuse caused the harm. Sloppy formalities are not enough on their own. Commingling funds, moving assets out of the company when a claim is coming, misrepresenting who the contracting party is, or forming the entity to carry out a fraud are the kinds of facts that satisfy the test.

For a business with several entities, the case also confirms that running them as a group is permitted. It does not protect you from direct liability for your own negligence or from agency theories. Keep contracts, insurance, employees, and accounts attributed to the right entity.

## Florida connection

This is the controlling Florida case. Later Florida decisions have distilled the rule into a three-part test: (1) the shareholder dominated and controlled the corporation to such an extent that the corporation's independent existence was in fact nonexistent and the shareholder was its alter ego; (2) the corporate form was used fraudulently or for an improper purpose; and (3) the fraudulent or improper use of the corporate form caused injury to the claimant. See *Gasparini v. Pordomingo*, 972 So. 2d 1053 (Fla. 3d DCA 2008) (verify citation). The same test applies to LLCs. Section 605.0304(2), Fla. Stat., adds that failure to observe formalities relating to the exercise of powers or the management of the LLC is not a ground for imposing liability on members or managers. For corporations, § 607.0622 addresses the limits of shareholder liability (verify section).
