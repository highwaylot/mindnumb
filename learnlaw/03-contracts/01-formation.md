# Formation: offer, acceptance, and the objective theory

**Module:** 03 · **Lesson:** 1 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Most business contracts form in email threads, texts, quotes, and purchase orders, not in a conference room. "Do we have a deal?" is the first question in almost every contract dispute, and the answer turns on what a reasonable person would have understood, not on what either side secretly meant. Knowing the formation rules tells you when you are bound and how to keep negotiations from becoming contracts by accident.

## The rule

A contract forms when there is an offer, an acceptance of that offer, and consideration. This lesson covers the first two. Consideration is lesson 2.

**Offer.** A manifestation of willingness to enter a bargain, made so that the other person is justified in understanding that assent will close the deal. The test is objective. Courts ask what a reasonable person in the recipient's position would have understood, not what the speaker intended. An offer must be definite enough that a court can tell what was promised. Price, subject matter, quantity, and the parties are the usual essential terms. Advertisements, price lists, and "we are interested" letters are usually invitations to deal, not offers, because they are not addressed to a specific person with definite terms.

**Acceptance.** A manifestation of assent to the offer's terms, in the manner the offer requires or permits. The offeror is master of the offer and can specify how it must be accepted. If the offer is silent, any reasonable means works. Silence is not acceptance unless the parties' prior dealings make it so.

**Termination of the offer.** An offer ends by rejection, counteroffer, lapse of a stated or reasonable time, revocation before acceptance, or death of the offeror. A counteroffer kills the original offer. Asking a question about it does not.

**Timing.** Under the mailbox rule, an acceptance is effective when sent, if sent by a means the offer permits. A revocation or rejection is effective only when received.

**The mirror image rule.** At common law, an acceptance must match the offer. A reply that adds or changes terms is a counteroffer. For the sale of goods, the UCC replaces this with § 672.207, Fla. Stat. A definite expression of acceptance forms a contract even if it states additional or different terms. Between merchants, additional terms become part of the contract unless the offer limits acceptance to its terms, the terms materially alter the deal, or the offeror objects within a reasonable time.

**Unilateral contracts.** An offer can invite acceptance by performance rather than a return promise. A reward poster is the classic example. Acceptance happens when the act is done.

**Burden.** The party claiming a contract exists must prove it by a preponderance of the evidence, including agreement on the essential terms.

## How courts reason about it

The objective theory is the organizing idea. In *Lucy v. Zehmer*, Zehmer said he was joking when he wrote out a farm sale on a restaurant receipt. The court held him to it because forty minutes of negotiation and a rewritten document signed by both spouses would lead a reasonable person to believe he meant it. The flip side is *Leonard v. Pepsico*, 88 F. Supp. 2d 116 (S.D.N.Y. 1999), where a commercial offering a fighter jet for points was obviously a joke. Same test, opposite result, because the facts differed.

Courts fight most over three things.

**Is it definite enough?** An "agreement to agree" is not a contract. Courts will fill gaps on minor terms with reasonable defaults, and the UCC fills many gaps by statute, including price (§ 672.305) and delivery (§ 672.308). But if the parties left an essential term open and showed they intended to negotiate it later, there is no contract. Letters of intent and term sheets live on this line. A document that says "subject to definitive agreement" usually signals no contract yet. One that sets every material term and says "we agree" usually is one, whatever its title.

**Did negotiations cross into contract?** Courts read the whole email chain and ask when, if ever, both sides stopped proposing and started agreeing. Words like "confirmed" and "we accept" matter. So does conduct. If one side starts performing and the other accepts the performance, a court will usually find a contract even if the paperwork was never finished.

**Which terms govern when forms conflict?** The battle of the forms under § 672.207 generates endless litigation. A buyer sends a purchase order with its terms. The seller responds with an acknowledgment carrying its own terms. Goods ship. There is a contract. The question is whose terms. The section's answer: terms the forms agree on, plus UCC gap-fillers, with conflicting terms often cancelling out under the "knockout" approach most courts use. Arbitration clauses and warranty disclaimers appearing only in one form are the usual casualties, because they materially alter the deal.

Policy drives the doctrine. The objective theory exists so people can rely on what they see and hear. The definiteness requirement exists so courts enforce bargains the parties made, not ones the court invents. The UCC drops the mirror image rule because commerce needs contracts to form even when the paperwork is sloppy.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on every point above. A few Florida touchstones:

- Florida requires a meeting of the minds on all essential terms, judged objectively. *St. Joe Corp. v. McIver*, 875 So. 2d 375 (Fla. 2004) (verify citation), applied this to an oral agreement. Oral contracts are enforceable if proven, subject to the statute of frauds.
- Florida adopted the mailbox rule in *Morrison v. Thoelke*, 155 So. 2d 889 (Fla. 2d DCA 1963). An acceptance mailed before a revocation was received formed the contract even though the acceptor tried to call it back.
- The Eleventh Circuit applied Florida's objective theory in *Kolodziej v. Mason*, 774 F.3d 736 (11th Cir. 2014). A lawyer's televised challenge, offering a million dollars to anyone who could make a certain trip in a certain time, was not an offer. A reasonable viewer would hear hyperbole, not a bargain.
- UCC Article 2 is Florida chapter 672. The battle of the forms is § 672.207. Firm offers by merchants are § 672.205: a signed written offer that says it will stay open is irrevocable for the stated time, up to 3 months (verify), without consideration.
- Florida enforces agreements formed by electronic means under § 668.50, the Uniform Electronic Transactions Act. See lesson 9.

- [`cases/carlill-v-carbolic-smoke-ball.md`](../cases/carlill-v-carbolic-smoke-ball.md): an advertisement can be an offer to the public, accepted by performance, when its terms are definite and it shows intent to be bound.

## Angles checklist

- Who said what, in what words, in what medium, and when? Pull the full thread.
- Would a reasonable recipient read the message as an offer or as an invitation to negotiate?
- Were the essential terms fixed? Which ones were left open and did the parties intend to come back to them?
- Was there a clean acceptance, or a counteroffer that killed the offer?
- Did anyone revoke, and did the revocation arrive before acceptance was sent?
- Is this a sale of goods? If so, § 672.207 applies and the mirror image rule does not.
- Did either side perform or accept performance? Conduct can form the contract the paper did not.
- Flip it: if no contract formed, is there a promissory estoppel or unjust enrichment claim for work already done?

## Drill

A Tampa printing company emails a prospective customer: "We can do 10,000 brochures at $0.42 each, delivery in two weeks. Let us know." The customer replies: "Great, go ahead, but we need them in ten days." The printer does not respond and does not start work. Four days later the customer emails: "Where are we on the brochures?" The printer says it never agreed to ten days and has since taken a bigger job. Spot the formation issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Was the first email an offer?** Probably yes. It is addressed to a specific customer and states quantity, price, and delivery. "Let us know" invites acceptance. A court applying the objective test would likely call it an offer.
- **Was the reply an acceptance?** This is a sale of goods, so § 672.207 governs, not the mirror image rule. "Great, go ahead" is a definite expression of acceptance. The ten-day term is an additional or different term. If both parties are merchants, the ten-day term becomes part of the contract unless it materially alters the deal or the printer objected within a reasonable time. Cutting delivery time by nearly a third is a plausible material alteration, so the contract likely formed on the printer's two-week term, with the ten-day request dropping out. If the customer is not a merchant, the additional term is a proposal that the printer never accepted, and again the two-week term governs.
- **Likely outcome.** A contract formed for 10,000 brochures at $0.42 with two-week delivery. The printer's refusal to perform is an anticipatory repudiation (lesson 5). The customer's remedy is cover: buy elsewhere and sue for the difference (lesson 8).
- **Facts that would change it.** If the printer's email said "quote valid only on signed order form," the reply would not be an acceptance. If the customer had replied "only if you can do ten days," that is a counteroffer and no contract formed.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A supplier posts a price list on its website. A customer emails "I accept, send 500 units." Contract?
2. A buyer mails an acceptance on Monday. The seller's revocation, mailed Sunday, arrives Tuesday. Contract?
3. Two companies exchange a purchase order and an acknowledgment with conflicting warranty terms, and goods ship. Is there a contract, and what warranty terms apply?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Usually no. A price list is an invitation to deal, not an offer, because it is not addressed to anyone in particular and the seller has not committed to any quantity. The customer's email is the offer. The supplier accepts by shipping or confirming.
2. Yes. Under the mailbox rule the acceptance was effective Monday when sent. The revocation was effective only when received on Tuesday, after the contract formed.
3. Yes, under § 672.207(1), because the acknowledgment was a definite expression of acceptance and the parties performed. The conflicting warranty terms knock each other out under the approach most courts follow, and the UCC's implied warranties fill the gap. Neither side gets its own warranty language.

</details>
