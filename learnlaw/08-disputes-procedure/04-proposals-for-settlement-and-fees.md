# Proposals for settlement and attorney fees

**Module:** 08 · **Lesson:** 4 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[FL]` `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

"Who pays the lawyers" often matters more than the merits. A $40,000 dispute can cost each side $60,000 to try. Under the American rule each side eats its own fees. Florida has three ways to shift them, and one, the proposal for settlement, is a weapon either side can fire at a chosen moment to put the other side's fees at risk.

## The rule

**American rule.** Each party pays its own attorney fees unless a contract, a statute, or a rule provides otherwise. Costs go to the prevailing party as of right under § 57.041.

**Contract clauses.** A "prevailing party" fee clause is enforced as written. Under § 57.105(7), a clause that gives fees to only one party is read as reciprocal: if the contract lets the landlord recover fees, the tenant can too when the tenant wins.

**Statutes.** Many Florida statutes shift fees. Some are one-way to the claimant (minimum wage, § 448.110; FLSA). Some are two-way (FDUTPA, § 501.2105; construction lien, § 713.29). Section 57.105(1) awards fees against a party and its lawyer for claims or defenses with no basis in fact or law, after the 21-day safe harbor.

**Proposals for settlement, § 768.79 and Rule 1.442.** The offer-of-judgment statute, applicable to any civil action for damages.

- **Who and when.** A defendant may serve a proposal no earlier than 90 days (verify) after service of process on it. A plaintiff may serve one no earlier than 90 days (verify) after the action commenced. Neither may serve one later than 45 days (verify) before trial. The offeree has 30 days (verify) to accept in writing; otherwise it is deemed rejected.
- **Thresholds.** If a plaintiff rejects a defendant's proposal and the judgment obtained is at least 25% (verify) less than the offer, the defendant recovers its reasonable fees and costs from the date of the offer. If a defendant rejects a plaintiff's proposal and the judgment is at least 25% (verify) more than the offer, the plaintiff recovers fees and costs from the offer date. A defense verdict is a judgment of zero, which is always 25% less than any defendant's offer.
- **Content (Rule 1.442(c)).** Must be in writing, state it is made under § 768.79 and Rule 1.442, name the party making it and the party to whom it is made, identify the claims it resolves, state all relevant conditions, state the total amount, state any nonmonetary terms, state whether punitive damages are included, and state whether attorney fees are part of the claim and included. Joint proposals must state the amount attributable to each party (Rule 1.442(c)(3)).
- **Judgment obtained.** The net judgment plus taxable costs and prejudgment interest accrued before the offer, and attorney fees if they were part of the claim.
- **Good faith.** The court may disallow fees if the proposal was not made in good faith (§ 768.79(7)(a)). A nominal offer, such as $500, is in good faith if the offeror had a reasonable basis to believe its exposure was nominal.

**Federal court.** Fed. R. Civ. P. 68 lets a defendant make an offer of judgment; if the plaintiff recovers less, the plaintiff pays post-offer costs, which include attorney fees only where the underlying statute defines fees as costs (*Marek v. Chesny*, 473 U.S. 1 (1985)). In diversity cases applying Florida law, federal courts apply § 768.79 as substantive (verify).

**Prejudgment interest.** On liquidated damages, interest runs from the date of loss as a matter of right (*Argonaut Insurance Co. v. May Plumbing Co.*, 474 So. 2d 212 (Fla. 1985)). The rate is set quarterly by the Chief Financial Officer under § 55.03. Unliquidated tort damages generally do not carry prejudgment interest.

**Amount of fees.** Florida uses the lodestar: reasonable hours times a reasonable rate, with possible adjustment (*Florida Patient's Compensation Fund v. Rowe*, 472 So. 2d 1145 (Fla. 1985)).

**Burden.** The party seeking fees proves entitlement and amount. The party resisting a proposal-based award proves lack of good faith or a defect in the proposal.

## How courts reason about it

- **Strict construction.** Because § 768.79 is in derogation of the American rule, courts construe it and Rule 1.442 strictly against the offeror. A proposal that fails to state whether fees are included, that is ambiguous about what claims it resolves, or that attaches a release with undefined terms, is unenforceable. Drafting is everything.
- **Joint proposals.** A single undifferentiated offer to two defendants, or from two plaintiffs, is invalid because each offeree cannot evaluate its own exposure (*Attorneys' Title Insurance Fund, Inc. v. Gorka*, 36 So. 3d 646 (Fla. 2010)) (verify). The exception is where one party's liability is purely vicarious.
- **Mixed relief.** Section 768.79 applies to actions "for damages." A case seeking both damages and nonmonetary relief such as an injunction or declaration generally falls outside it (*Diamond Aircraft Industries, Inc. v. Horowitch*, 107 So. 3d 362 (Fla. 2013)) (verify). Courts look at whether the real claim is for money.
- **Nominal offers.** Courts uphold $100 or $1,000 proposals when the defendant had a reasonable belief in a defense verdict at the time, judged by what it knew then.
- **Conditions and releases.** A proposal conditioned on a release or confidentiality must state those terms with enough particularity to be evaluated. Courts have voided proposals over vague release language.
- **Prevailing party.** Under a contract clause, the prevailing party is the one who won on the significant issues, judged by the net result, not count by count (*Moritz v. Hoyt Enterprises, Inc.*, 604 So. 2d 807 (Fla. 1992)) (verify). A court may also find neither party prevailed.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the American rule like every other state, and follows the majority in enforcing contract fee clauses. It departs sharply in § 768.79, which shifts fees, not merely costs, in both directions.
- Section 57.105(7) reciprocity is a Florida statute; many states have no equivalent and enforce one-way clauses as written.
- FDUTPA's § 501.2105 fee provision is discretionary and two-way, which makes a weak FDUTPA count a liability for the plaintiff.
- The quarterly interest rate under § 55.03 has been in the 8 to 10 percent range (verify) recently, which makes prejudgment interest significant on old invoices.

## Angles checklist

- Is there a fee clause? Is it one-way, and therefore reciprocal under § 57.105(7)?
- Does any statute in the case shift fees, and in which direction?
- Timing: is the 90-day window open? Is trial within 45 days?
- Drafting: every element of Rule 1.442(c), each party separately, fees and punitive damages addressed, release terms spelled out.
- Valuation: what judgment would miss the 25% threshold? What is the realistic range?
- Mixed relief: does the complaint seek an injunction or declaration that takes the case outside § 768.79?
- Good faith: what did the offeror know at the time?
- Interest: liquidated or not? From what date? Current rate?
- Flip it: has the other side served a proposal? What does the fee exposure look like from today forward?

## Drill

Coral Gables Catering LLC sues a wedding venue for $90,000 under a written contract with a prevailing-party fee clause. Ninety-one days after service, the venue serves a proposal for settlement of $20,000 "to resolve all claims, conditioned on a mutual general release and confidentiality." Coral Gables rejects it. At trial, the jury awards Coral Gables $14,000. Both sides move for fees. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Contract clause.** Who prevailed on the significant issues? Coral Gables won a judgment, but $14,000 on a $90,000 claim may lead a court to find the venue prevailed on the net result, or that neither did.
- **Proposal threshold.** $14,000 is more than 25% below $20,000 (the threshold is $15,000). If the proposal is valid, the venue recovers its fees and costs from day 91 forward, which likely exceed the $14,000 judgment, leaving Coral Gables a net debtor.
- **Validity.** The proposal was timely. But it conditions acceptance on a "mutual general release and confidentiality" without stating the release terms or the confidentiality terms. Under strict construction, that ambiguity may void it. Did it state whether fees were included and address punitive damages?
- **Interest and costs.** The judgment obtained includes prejudgment interest accrued before the offer and taxable costs. If the contract damages were liquidated, interest from the breach date at the § 55.03 rate could push the judgment obtained above $15,000 and defeat the proposal.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the complaint also sought a declaration about the contract, § 768.79 may not apply at all. If the proposal attached the actual release, the ambiguity argument disappears.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A contract says "tenant shall pay landlord's attorney fees in any action to enforce this lease." Tenant wins. Who pays?
2. A defendant serves a $1 proposal for settlement on day 95 and wins a defense verdict. Does it recover fees?
3. Why does a plaintiff's FDUTPA count create fee risk for the plaintiff?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Landlord pays tenant's fees. Section 57.105(7) makes the one-way clause reciprocal.
2. Probably, if it had a reasonable basis at the time to believe its exposure was nominal and the proposal met every Rule 1.442 requirement. A defense verdict is zero, which is 25% less than $1. Good faith is judged by what the defendant knew on day 95.
3. Because § 501.2105 awards fees to the prevailing party on that claim, which can be the defendant. A FDUTPA count that fails exposes the plaintiff to the defendant's fees for defending it even if the plaintiff wins other counts.

</details>
