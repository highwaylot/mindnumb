# The life of a lawsuit

**Module:** 08 · **Lesson:** 1 · **Time:** ~60 min
**Tags:** `[FL]` `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

A lawsuit is a sequence of deadlines, each with a consequence for missing it. Knowing the sequence tells you what a lawyer is doing with your money, where the leverage points are, and how long and how expensive the road is. Most cases settle at predictable moments: after the motion to dismiss, after depositions, at mediation, and on the courthouse steps. This is the Florida circuit court version, with federal differences noted.

## The rule

**1. Pre-suit.** Demand letter, any statutory notice, litigation hold on documents (lesson 3). Check § 95.11 and any contractual notice, forum, or arbitration clause.

**2. Complaint.** Filed with the clerk, with a civil cover sheet and the filing fee, about $400 (verify) in circuit court. Florida requires fact pleading: ultimate facts supporting each element of each count (Fla. R. Civ. P. 1.110(b)). A jury demand must be made in a pleading or within 10 days (verify) after the last pleading, or it is waived (Rule 1.430).

**3. Service.** The plaintiff must serve it within 120 days (verify) (Rule 1.070(j)). An LLC is served on its registered agent, or on a member or manager if the agent cannot be found (§ 48.062).

**4. Response, 20 days.** The defendant must serve an answer or a motion within 20 days (verify) of service (Rule 1.140(a)). A motion to dismiss for failure to state a cause of action, lack of jurisdiction, improper venue, or insufficient service tolls the answer deadline. Defenses of personal jurisdiction, venue, and service are waived if not raised in the first response (Rule 1.140(h)). Do nothing and the clerk enters a default (Rule 1.500).

**5. Answer, affirmative defenses, counterclaims.** The answer admits or denies each paragraph. Affirmative defenses such as limitations, release, waiver, estoppel, payment, and fraud must be pleaded or they are waived (Rule 1.110(d)). A counterclaim arising from the same transaction is compulsory and is lost if not brought (Rule 1.170(a)). Third-party claims against outsiders (Rule 1.180) come here too.

**6. Case management.** Since January 1, 2025 (verify), Rule 1.200 requires the court to assign each case to a track (streamlined, general, or complex) and enter a case management order with firm deadlines for discovery, motions, and trial. Continuances are harder to get (Rule 1.460).

**7. Discovery.** Also since 2025, Florida requires **initial disclosures** within 60 days (verify) of service of the complaint: witnesses, documents, damages computation, and insurance (Rule 1.280(a)), with a continuing duty to supplement. The scope is now governed by **proportionality**, imported from federal Rule 26: relevance weighed against the needs of the case, the amount in controversy, the parties' resources, and burden (Rule 1.280(c)) (verify subsection). Tools: interrogatories, limited to 30 (verify) including subparts (Rule 1.340); requests for production (Rule 1.350); requests for admission (Rule 1.370); depositions (Rule 1.310); subpoenas to non-parties (Rule 1.410). Disputes go by motion to compel, with fee sanctions available (Rule 1.380).

**8. Motions.** Summary judgment under Rule 1.510, which since May 1, 2021, uses the federal standard: judgment is granted if there is no genuine dispute of material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law, and a party who bears the burden at trial must come forward with evidence on which a reasonable jury could find for it. The motion must be served at least 40 days (verify) before the hearing, and the response is due 40 days (verify) after service of the motion under the 2025 timing amendment (verify).

**9. Mediation.** Almost every Florida civil case is ordered to mediation before trial (ch. 44, Rules 1.700 to 1.730). A party representative with full settlement authority must attend. Lesson 5.

**10. Trial.** Jury or bench. Business trials run one to five days and end in a final judgment.

**11. Post-trial and appeal.** Motion for new trial or rehearing within 15 days (verify) (Rules 1.530, 1.480). Notice of appeal to the District Court of Appeal within 30 days (verify) of rendition of the final judgment (Fla. R. App. P. 9.110(b)). The deadline is jurisdictional; miss it and the appeal is gone.

**12. Judgment and collection.** Lesson 6.

**Federal differences.** Notice pleading under the *Twombly* plausibility standard; 21 days (verify) to answer under Fed. R. Civ. P. 12; initial disclosures under Rule 26(a); 25 interrogatories; magistrate judges handle discovery; appeal to the Eleventh Circuit within 30 days (verify).

**Timeline and cost.** A contested commercial case in Florida circuit court typically takes 12 to 24 months (verify) to trial on the general track, longer if complex. Discovery is the largest cost. A case through trial commonly costs each side six figures.

## How courts reason about it

- **The pleading stage tests the story, not the evidence.** On a motion to dismiss, the court takes the complaint's facts as true and asks only whether they state a claim. Florida's fact pleading is stricter than federal notice pleading, so vague complaints get dismissed with leave to amend.
- **Summary judgment tests the evidence.** Since 2021, Florida judges apply *Celotex* and *Anderson v. Liberty Lobby*: the question is whether the evidence would let a reasonable jury find for the non-movant. A "mere scintilla" or a self-serving affidavit contradicting the party's own deposition is not enough.
- **Discovery is bounded by proportionality now.** Judges deny requests that are expensive relative to the stakes. A $60,000 contract case does not justify forensic imaging of every laptop.
- **Case management orders are enforced.** The 2025 rules were written to end the practice of agreeing to endless continuances. Courts strike late-disclosed witnesses and deny extensions without good cause.
- **Waiver is everywhere.** Jurisdiction and venue defenses not raised at the first opportunity. Jury trial not demanded. Affirmative defenses not pleaded. Compulsory counterclaims not filed. Appellate courts rarely rescue a party from its own procedural omission.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the majority of states in its general civil procedure structure, borrowing heavily from the federal rules, and moved closer in 2021 (summary judgment) and 2025 (disclosures, proportionality, case management).
- It departs in requiring fact pleading, in the 20-day response period, in mandatory mediation as a near-universal practice, and in the proposal for settlement fee-shifting rule.
- County court handles claims up to $50,000 (verify) under the same rules, with small claims under $8,000 (verify) using the simpler Small Claims Rules (lesson 2).
- An LLC or corporation must appear through a licensed attorney in circuit and county court outside small claims.

## Angles checklist

- Limitation period and any contractual pre-suit steps?
- Forum: county or circuit by amount; state or federal by diversity or federal question; arbitration clause?
- Pleading: can every element be stated as a fact? Fraud with particularity?
- Service on the right agent, within 120 days?
- Day 20: motion or answer? Which defenses are waived if not raised now?
- Counterclaims: what does the defendant have against the plaintiff from the same transaction?
- Disclosures: what must be turned over in 60 days?
- Proportionality: what discovery is worth the cost at these stakes?
- Summary judgment: can the claim or defense survive on evidence, not allegations?
- Fees: who pays under contract, statute, or proposal for settlement?
- Appeal: 30 days, bond to stay collection.

## Drill

Harbor Logistics LLC sues a former customer, Delray Foods, in Palm Beach circuit court for $180,000 in unpaid freight invoices under a written agreement. Delray is served through its registered agent on March 1. Delray believes Harbor damaged $70,000 of refrigerated cargo last year and also thinks the venue clause in the agreement requires suit in Broward. Delray's owner plans to "deal with it next month." Spot the procedural issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Day 20.** Delray must respond by March 21 or face a clerk's default. "Next month" is a default judgment for $180,000 plus interest and possibly fees. Setting aside a default requires excusable neglect, a meritorious defense, and diligence.
- **Venue.** Improper venue must be raised by motion before or with the answer or it is waived (Rule 1.140). Delray should move to transfer now.
- **Compulsory counterclaim.** The cargo damage arises from the same agreement and course of dealing. It is compulsory under Rule 1.170(a); if Delray answers without it, the $70,000 claim is gone.
- **Counsel.** Delray is an entity and must appear through a lawyer.
- **Fees.** A written freight agreement likely has a prevailing-party fee clause, reciprocal under § 57.105(7). Either side can serve a proposal for settlement after the 90-day window (lesson 4).
- **Summary judgment.** Unpaid invoices under a written contract are a common summary judgment case for the plaintiff; Delray's setoff defense and counterclaim are what keep it from judgment.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the agreement has an arbitration clause, Delray should move to compel arbitration immediately rather than answer. Removal to federal court requires complete diversity and a defendant that is not a Florida citizen.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A defendant files a motion to dismiss on day 18. Does it still have to file an answer on day 20?
2. What changed about Florida summary judgment in 2021?
3. Why does Florida require ultimate facts in a complaint when federal court requires only a plausible claim?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. A timely motion under Rule 1.140(b) tolls the time to answer until the court rules. If the motion is denied, the answer is due 10 days (verify) after.
2. Florida adopted the federal standard: no genuine dispute of material fact judged by whether a reasonable jury could find for the non-movant, abandoning the old rule that the slightest doubt defeated the motion.
3. Historical choice. Florida's rules were drafted around fact pleading and the Florida Supreme Court has kept it, reasoning that it gives defendants fair notice and screens weak claims early. The practical effect is more motions to dismiss and amended complaints in state court.

</details>
