# Charging orders and transfers

**Module:** 02 · **Lesson:** 6 · **Time:** ~45 min
**Tags:** `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Lesson 5 was about the company's creditors reaching the owner. This lesson is the reverse: the owner's personal creditors reaching the company. If a member loses a lawsuit over a car accident, a divorce, or a personal guaranty, what can the judgment creditor take from the LLC? The answer depends on whether the LLC has one member or more than one, and it is the single most misunderstood point in Florida LLC planning. The same rules govern what a member can sell, give away, or leave in a will.

## The rule

**What a member owns.** A member's interest in an LLC has two parts. The **transferable interest** is the right to receive distributions, § 605.0102(67) (verify numbering). It is personal property, § 605.0501. Everything else, the right to vote, manage, inspect records, and be a member, is not transferable on its own.

**Transfers.** A member may transfer the transferable interest in whole or in part, § 605.0502(1). The transfer is permissible, does not by itself dissociate the member or dissolve the LLC, and does not entitle the transferee to participate in management, to inspect records, or to become a member. The transferee gets only the distributions the transferor would have received and, on dissolution, the net amount the transferor would have received, § 605.0502(2) and (3). The transferor keeps all rights and duties of a member other than the right to distributions transferred, § 605.0502(5) (verify). A transfer in violation of a restriction in the operating agreement is ineffective as to a person with notice of the restriction, § 605.0502(7) (verify). To become a member, the transferee needs the consent of all members or whatever the operating agreement requires, § 605.0401(3).

**Charging orders.** A judgment creditor of a member or transferee may apply for a **charging order** against the debtor's transferable interest, § 605.0503(1). The order is a lien on the interest and requires the LLC to pay the creditor any distribution that would otherwise go to the debtor, until the judgment is paid. The creditor does not become a member, cannot vote, cannot force a distribution, and cannot reach the LLC's assets.

For an LLC with **more than one member**, the charging order is the **sole and exclusive remedy** by which a judgment creditor may satisfy a judgment from the debtor's interest, § 605.0503(3). No foreclosure, no forced sale, no other legal or equitable remedy against the interest.

For an LLC with **one member**, the rule is different. If the creditor shows that distributions under a charging order will not satisfy the judgment within a reasonable time, the court may order foreclosure and sale of the interest, § 605.0503(4). The buyer at the sale takes the member's entire interest, becomes the member, and the debtor ceases to be one, § 605.0503(5) (verify). The LLC's assets are then in the hands of the buyer.

The creditor bears the burden of showing the foreclosure condition. The rules apply whether the member is a person or an entity, and to transferees as well as members.

## How courts reason about it

The charging order is a compromise written into the uniform acts to protect the other members. If a creditor could seize a partner's or member's interest and become an owner, the remaining owners would be in business with a stranger they never chose. The charging order gives the creditor the money without the membership. The policy is sound for a multi-member company. For a single-member LLC there are no other members to protect, and that is where *Olmstead v. FTC* came in.

**Olmstead.** The FTC obtained a judgment against individuals who ran a credit card scam and sought to reach their single-member Florida LLCs. The pre-2011 statute, § 608.433, said a charging order was available but did not say it was exclusive. The Florida Supreme Court, answering a certified question from the Eleventh Circuit, held that the statute did not bar a court from ordering the debtor to surrender all right, title, and interest in a single-member LLC to satisfy the judgment. The reasoning: the charging order remedy exists to protect non-debtor members, and a single-member LLC has none. Moreover, in a single-member LLC the sole member's full interest can be transferred without anyone's consent, so the creditor can take it all.

**The legislative response.** The 2011 amendment, carried into § 605.0503 when the Revised Act was adopted, codified a split result. For multi-member LLCs the Legislature made the charging order explicitly exclusive, overruling any reading of *Olmstead* that would have allowed broader remedies against them. For single-member LLCs it allowed foreclosure, but only on a showing that distributions would not satisfy the judgment within a reasonable time. The practical lesson: a single-member Florida LLC gives the member almost no protection against the member's own creditors. A multi-member LLC gives strong protection, so long as the other members are real.

**What counts as a real second member.** Courts have not fully developed the question in Florida (verify). The risk is a member added only to defeat creditors, with no capital, no role, and no economic stake. A creditor can attack that as a fraudulent transfer under ch. 726, or argue the LLC is in substance single-member. The facts that make a second member credible: a genuine contribution, a share of profits actually paid, a signed operating agreement, and participation that predates the debt.

**Distributions and discretion.** A charging order reaches only distributions. If the LLC never distributes, the creditor waits. Courts recognize this and have limited tools: they can enforce the order against distributions disguised as salary or loans, and they can look at whether the withholding is itself a fraudulent transfer. The other members, who may be the debtor's family, often prefer to leave money in the company. A creditor with a charging order against a multi-member Florida LLC may hold it for years.

**Transfers and the operating agreement.** Courts enforce transfer restrictions in operating agreements against transferees with notice. A member who assigns an interest to a spouse in a divorce, or whose interest passes to an estate, produces a transferee, not a member. The estate or ex-spouse can collect distributions but cannot vote or demand information beyond what § 605.0502 allows. Many fights about "my late husband's share" are transferee cases, and the answer is that the share is an economic interest only unless the agreement says otherwise.

## Florida specifics

- Florida follows the majority rule that the charging order is the exclusive remedy against a multi-member LLC interest. Its single-member foreclosure rule is an explicit statutory departure from states that give single-member LLCs the same protection.
- The *Olmstead* history is the reason Florida planners treat single-member LLCs as liability shields but not as asset protection from the member's own creditors.
- Florida's homestead exemption and tenancy by the entireties, module 08, are the main personal asset protection tools. They do not depend on the LLC. *Beal Bank, SSB v. Almand & Associates*, 780 So. 2d 45 (Fla. 2001), covers entireties.
- An LLC interest owned by spouses as tenants by the entireties is a planning technique whose treatment under § 605.0503 is unsettled (verify).
- Transfers on death: a deceased member's personal representative may exercise the member's rights for purposes of settling the estate, § 605.0504 (verify), but the heirs are transferees unless admitted.
- A transfer restriction binds a transferee with notice; a buy-sell clause triggered by a charging order or bankruptcy must be drafted carefully because federal bankruptcy law limits some restrictions.

## Angles checklist

- How many members, really? Is each one genuine, with capital and a role?
- Is the judgment against the LLC or against a member personally?
- Has a charging order been entered? Are distributions being made, or withheld?
- For a single-member LLC, can the creditor show distributions will not pay the judgment in a reasonable time?
- Was any member added or interest transferred after the debt arose?
- Does the operating agreement restrict transfers, and did the transferee have notice?
- Is a transferee claiming management or information rights it does not have?
- Is a spouse, ex-spouse, or estate holding a transferable interest only?

## Drill

Dana is the sole member of a Naples LLC that owns a rental fourplex worth $800,000 with no mortgage. She loses a personal injury suit from a boating accident and owes $450,000. The month after the verdict she gives her brother a 10% interest for no money and adds him on Sunbiz as a manager. The LLC has never made a distribution; Dana takes a "management fee" of $6,000 a month. The judgment creditor comes after the LLC. List the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Charging order.** Available against Dana's interest regardless. It attaches to distributions. The management fee is a candidate for recharacterization as a distribution in disguise; a court can order it paid to the creditor.
- **Single or multi-member.** If the brother's interest is genuine, § 605.0503(3) makes the charging order exclusive and foreclosure is off the table. If it is not, the LLC is single-member in substance and the creditor can seek foreclosure under § 605.0503(4) by showing that no distributions will ever satisfy $450,000.
- **The brother's interest.** Transferred after the judgment, for no value, to a family member, while Dana was insolvent as to this debt. That is a textbook fraudulent transfer under ch. 726. The creditor can seek to void it, restoring single-member status. The brother's lack of capital and role makes the transfer hard to defend.
- **The fourplex.** It belongs to the LLC, not Dana. The creditor cannot levy on it directly. The only routes are the charging order, foreclosure of the interest, or a fraudulent transfer claim about the LLC's formation if the property was moved into it to hinder creditors.
- **Timing.** When was the LLC formed and the fourplex contributed? Before the accident, it is legitimate planning. After the suit, it is another transfer to attack.
- **Open questions.** Did the brother contribute anything? Is there a signed operating agreement? What does the LLC do with its rental income?

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A judgment creditor of one member of a three-member LLC wants to force the LLC to sell its building. Can it?
2. A member assigns his entire interest to his daughter. Can she vote at the next members' meeting?
3. Why does a single-member LLC in Florida provide a liability shield but weak asset protection?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. The charging order is the exclusive remedy under § 605.0503(3). The creditor gets the member's share of distributions, if any, and cannot reach the LLC's assets or compel a sale.
2. No, unless the other members consent to admit her under § 605.0401(3) or the operating agreement provides otherwise. She holds a transferable interest: distributions only.
3. The shield under § 605.0304 protects the member from the LLC's debts and does not depend on the number of members. Asset protection runs the other way, and § 605.0503(4) lets the member's creditor foreclose on a single-member interest, taking the whole company, once distributions are shown to be inadequate.

</details>
