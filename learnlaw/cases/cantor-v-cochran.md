# Cantor v. Cochran, 184 So. 2d 173 (Fla. 1966)

**Tags:** `[FL]` · employment · agency · employee versus independent contractor · right to control · workers' compensation
**Used in:** [`05-agency-employment/04-employee-vs-contractor.md`](../05-agency-employment/04-employee-vs-contractor.md), [`05-agency-employment/02-vicarious-liability.md`](../05-agency-employment/02-vicarious-liability.md), [`05-agency-employment/05-wage-and-hour.md`](../05-agency-employment/05-wage-and-hour.md)

## One-line takeaway

Whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor turns primarily on the right to control the manner in which the work is done, judged by the Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220 factors, not by the label the parties use.

## Facts

Cochran was injured while doing work for Cantor and sought workers' compensation benefits. The arrangement had features of both employment and independent contracting. Cantor had hired Cochran to perform physical work on Cantor's property, paid him for the work, and had the practical ability to direct what was done and how, but had not set him up as a regular employee (verify the specific nature of the work and the payment arrangement). Cantor contended Cochran was an independent contractor and so outside the workers' compensation system.

## Procedural posture

Cochran filed a claim under Florida's Workmen's Compensation Law, now ch. 440, Fla. Stat. The deputy commissioner and the Florida Industrial Commission reached different conclusions on whether Cochran was an employee (verify which found employee status). The losing party petitioned the Florida Supreme Court for certiorari, which at that time reviewed Industrial Commission orders directly.

## Issue

What test determines whether a worker is an employee or an independent contractor under Florida law?

## Holding

The Florida Supreme Court adopted the test of Restatement (Second) of Agency § 220. The primary factor is the right to control the details of the work. The other listed factors inform that inquiry. Applying the test, the court held Cochran was an employee entitled to compensation (verify).

## Reasoning

The court set out the Restatement factors: the extent of control the employer may exercise over the details of the work; whether the worker is engaged in a distinct occupation or business; whether the kind of work is usually done under the direction of an employer or by a specialist without supervision; the skill required; whether the employer or the worker supplies the instrumentalities, tools, and place of work; the length of time of the engagement; whether payment is by the time or by the job; whether the work is part of the regular business of the employer; whether the parties believe they are creating an employment relationship; and whether the principal is in business.

The court made the right of control the center of the test. The question is not whether the employer actually directed every detail but whether it had the right to. A worker who is told what result to achieve and left free to decide how is more likely a contractor. A worker who can be told how, when, and with what to do the job is more likely an employee. The other factors are evidence of that right. Payment by the hour, tools supplied by the employer, work that is part of the employer's ordinary business, and an open-ended engagement all point toward employment. A separate business, specialized skill, the worker's own tools, and payment by the job point the other way.

The court also emphasized that the parties' own characterization is one factor among many and does not control. A worker called a contractor who is treated like an employee is an employee.

## Dissent or counterpoints

No dissent is reported (verify). The argument on the other side is the one employers always make: the parties agreed on contractor status, the worker accepted the terms, and courts should honor the bargain. Florida's answer, repeated in later cases, is that status is a legal conclusion drawn from how the relationship actually operated, because workers' compensation, wage, tax, and vicarious liability rules exist to protect third parties and the public and cannot be waived by private agreement.

## Why it matters for a Florida business

Misclassification is one of the most expensive mistakes a small business makes. If a worker you call a contractor is really an employee, you may owe workers' compensation coverage and penalties, reemployment tax, overtime under the FLSA, and you may be vicariously liable for the worker's torts. The *Cantor* factors are the checklist. The practical signs that point to employee status are hourly pay, set hours, your tools and premises, your supervision of method, and work that is your core business. A written contractor agreement helps only if the day-to-day relationship matches it.

## Florida connection

*Cantor* remains the common-law test in Florida for vicarious liability and agency questions. For workers' compensation, the Legislature has added a statutory definition. Section 440.02(15)(d), Fla. Stat., lists criteria for independent contractor status, including maintaining a separate business, holding a federal employer identification number, receiving compensation for work performed by the job rather than salary, and the right to control the means of work (verify current text). The Florida Supreme Court has held that where a written agreement expresses the parties' intent, courts look first to it, but actual practice controls when it contradicts the agreement. *Keith v. News & Sun Sentinel Co.*, 667 So. 2d 167 (Fla. 1995). Federal wage law uses the FLSA "economic reality" test, which is broader, and the IRS uses its own common-law factors. A worker can be a contractor under one test and an employee under another, so each statute must be checked separately.
