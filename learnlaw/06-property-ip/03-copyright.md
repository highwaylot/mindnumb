# Copyright

**Module:** 06 · **Lesson:** 3 · **Time:** ~55 min
**Tags:** `[US]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Every business makes copyrighted works daily: website text, product photos, marketing copy, software, training videos, social posts. Every business also uses other people's: stock photos, fonts, music in videos, images pulled from a search. Copyright is the law that decides who owns all of that and what using it without permission costs. The costs are statutory, need no proof of loss, and come with attorney fees, which is why one photograph on a website generates demand letters for thousands of dollars.

## The rule

Copyright is federal, under the Copyright Act, 17 U.S.C. § 101 et seq. State law is preempted for works within its scope (§ 301). Florida has no separate copyright law for fixed works.

**What is protected.** "Original works of authorship fixed in any tangible medium of expression" (§ 102(a)). Two requirements:

1. **Originality**: independently created with a minimal degree of creativity. The bar is low but real. *Feist Publications, Inc. v. Rural Telephone Service Co.*, 499 U.S. 340 (1991), held that an alphabetical phone directory was not original; effort ("sweat of the brow") does not count.
2. **Fixation**: recorded in some form that can be perceived or reproduced. A speech that is not recorded is not protected; the notes for it are.

Copyright does not protect ideas, procedures, processes, systems, methods of operation, concepts, principles, or discoveries, only the expression of them (§ 102(b)). Facts, short phrases, titles, and names are trademark territory, not copyright. Works generated entirely by a machine without human authorship are not protected, according to the Copyright Office and the courts that have addressed it (verify current state).

**Protection is automatic.** Copyright exists from the moment of fixation. No notice, registration, or filing is required.

**Exclusive rights** (§ 106). The owner alone may reproduce the work, prepare derivative works, distribute copies, perform it publicly, and display it publicly. Anyone who does one of those without permission infringes, whether or not they knew or intended. Infringement is proven by ownership of a valid copyright plus copying of protected expression, usually shown by access plus substantial similarity.

**Term.** Life of the author plus 70 years. For works made for hire and anonymous works, 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever is shorter (§ 302).

**Registration.** Not required for the right to exist, but required for two things:

- **To sue.** A U.S. work must be registered, meaning the Copyright Office has acted on the application, before an infringement suit is filed (§ 411(a); *Fourth Estate Public Benefit Corp. v. Wall-Street.com, LLC*, 586 U.S. 296 (2019)).
- **Statutory damages and fees.** Available only if the work was registered before the infringement began, or within three months of first publication (§ 412). Statutory damages range from $750 to $30,000 per work, up to $150,000 for willful infringement (§ 504(c)) (verify). Without timely registration, the plaintiff gets only actual damages and the infringer's profits, which for a website photo may be nearly nothing.

The online filing fee is modest, in the range of $45 to $65 per application (verify current fee).

**Fair use** (§ 107). A defense weighed on four factors: the purpose and character of the use, including whether it is commercial and whether it is transformative; the nature of the copyrighted work; the amount and substantiality taken; and the effect on the market for the original. *Andy Warhol Foundation for the Visual Arts, Inc. v. Goldsmith*, 598 U.S. 508 (2023), narrowed "transformative" to focus on whether the specific use shares the original's purpose. The defendant bears the burden.

**The DMCA** (§ 512). A service provider that hosts user content gets a safe harbor from monetary liability for user infringement if it designates an agent with the Copyright Office, has and enforces a repeat-infringer policy, and responds to proper takedown notices by removing the material and honoring counter-notices. The agent designation costs $6 and must be renewed every three years (verify). A person who knowingly sends a false takedown notice is liable under § 512(f).

**Limitation period.** Three years from when the claim accrued (§ 507(b)). Courts apply a discovery rule, and *Warner Chappell Music, Inc. v. Nealy*, 601 U.S. 366 (2024), allowed damages for infringements older than three years if the claim was timely under that rule (verify citation).

## How courts reason about it

**Idea versus expression.** The hardest line in copyright. A recipe's list of ingredients is unprotected; the prose describing it may be. Software is protected as a literary work, but functional elements and elements dictated by efficiency or external constraints are filtered out before comparing.

**Substantial similarity.** After filtering unprotected elements, courts ask whether an ordinary observer would find the works substantially similar in protected expression.

**Fair use after Warhol.** Courts now ask what the defendant's specific use is for and whether it substitutes for the original's licensed uses. Using a photographer's image on a commercial website to illustrate the same subject the photographer would license it for is the weakest case. Commentary, criticism, and parody remain strong. The amount factor looks at whether the taking was reasonable in light of the purpose.

**Statutory damages as a lever.** Because statutory damages do not require proof of loss, a registered photographer with one infringed image has a claim worth up to $30,000 in theory, plus fees, against a business whose actual benefit was a few dollars. Courts have discretion within the range and consider the infringer's state of mind, the license fee it would have paid, and deterrence. Innocent infringement can reduce the floor to $200.

**The Copyright Claims Board.** Since 2022 a small-claims tribunal in the Copyright Office hears claims up to $30,000 (verify). A respondent can opt out within 60 days.

**Implied licenses.** When a business pays a contractor for a work delivered for a known use, courts find an implied nonexclusive license for that use. It does not transfer ownership and may not cover new uses. Lesson 6 covers the fix.

## Florida specifics

Copyright is entirely federal. Federal courts have exclusive jurisdiction over infringement claims (28 U.S.C. § 1338). Florida courts hear related state claims that survive preemption, such as breach of a license agreement.

Practical points for Florida businesses:

- Register the works that matter, especially photographs and software, within three months of publication to preserve statutory damages.
- Keep every stock license and its terms. A website license often does not cover social media or print.
- Designate a DMCA agent if the site accepts user content.
- Photographs of your products taken by a photographer belong to the photographer unless assigned in writing.

## Case anchors

- [`cases/procd-v-zeidenberg.md`](../cases/procd-v-zeidenberg.md): a license can impose contract terms on a copyrighted work that copyright itself would not, and the terms are enforceable.
- [`cases/nguyen-v-barnes-and-noble.md`](../cases/nguyen-v-barnes-and-noble.md): website terms, including license and IP terms, bind users only with adequate notice.

## Angles checklist

- What exactly is the work, and is it original and fixed? Is any of it facts, ideas, or functional elements?
- Who created it, under what arrangement? Employee, contractor, founder? Any written assignment?
- Is it registered, and when relative to the infringement and to publication?
- How was the copy made: reproduction, derivative work, public display? Any license, and what does it cover?
- Fair use: purpose, nature, amount, market effect, with *Warhol* in mind.
- DMCA: is the defendant a host of user content with a designated agent and a policy?
- Flip it: implied license, independent creation, de minimis use, statute of limitations.
- Exposure: actual damages and profits, or statutory damages plus fees if timely registered.

## Drill

Bayside Kitchens, LLC redesigns its website. Its marketing contractor pulls a kitchen photo from an image search, writes new product descriptions, and embeds a licensed stock music track in a promo video, which Bayside later reposts to Instagram. A former employee's photos of Bayside's showroom, taken on her own camera for the old site, are reused on the new one. Eight months later Bayside receives a $9,000 demand from the kitchen photo's owner, who registered it last year. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **The kitchen photo.** Reproduction and public display without a license. The photo is registered, apparently before the infringement, so statutory damages and fees are on the table. Fair use is weak: commercial, same purpose as the photographer's licensing market, the whole work taken. Likely liability; the question is amount. The contractor's conduct is Bayside's problem because Bayside displayed the photo; indemnity against the contractor depends on the contract.
- **Product descriptions.** Written by the contractor, so the contractor owns them absent a written assignment, with Bayside holding an implied license for the website. A risk if the relationship sours.
- **Music track.** The stock license governs. Many web licenses exclude social media or require a separate license; the Instagram repost may exceed scope. Read the license.
- **Former employee's showroom photos.** Taken on her own camera, but if taking photos was within the scope of her employment, they are works made for hire and Bayside owns them. If she took them as a favor outside her job, she owns them and Bayside has at most an implied license for the old site. Facts needed: her job duties and any direction from Bayside.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A competitor copies your website's layout, color scheme, and the general idea of a "build your own package" tool. Infringement?
2. You find a photo on a site with no copyright notice. Does the absence of notice make it free to use?
3. A photographer's image was infringed in January; she registered it in June. What can she recover?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Probably not for the idea or the general look. Ideas and functional layouts are unprotected. Copying your actual text, your specific images, or your code would be infringement, and a distinctive overall look might be trade dress under trademark law.
2. No. Notice has been optional since 1989. Lack of notice at most supports an innocent infringement argument that can reduce statutory damages to $200.
3. Actual damages and the infringer's profits, but not statutory damages or fees, because registration came after the infringement began and more than three months after publication (assuming the photo was published earlier). She can still get an injunction.

</details>
