# Trademarks

**Module:** 06 · **Lesson:** 2 · **Time:** ~55 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Your name is the asset customers remember. Trademark law decides whether you can stop someone else from using it, whether someone else can stop you, and whether the name you chose is worth protecting at all. The common mistakes are cheap to avoid and expensive to fix: picking a descriptive name, skipping a clearance search, and assuming a Sunbiz or fictitious name filing gave you rights. It did not.

## The rule

A **trademark** is a word, name, symbol, design, or combination used in commerce to identify the source of goods or services and distinguish them from others. A service mark does the same for services. Trade dress covers product packaging and design.

**Rights come from use, not registration.** In the United States, the first to use a distinctive mark in commerce on particular goods or services in a particular area owns it there. Registration adds benefits but does not create the right. This is the majority rule everywhere in the country because it is federal.

**Distinctiveness.** A mark is protectable only if it identifies source. Courts sort marks along a spectrum from *Abercrombie & Fitch Co. v. Hunting World, Inc.*, 537 F.2d 4 (2d Cir. 1976):

1. **Generic**: the name of the thing itself ("Bakery" for a bakery). Never protectable.
2. **Descriptive**: describes a quality or feature ("Fresh Baked," "Tampa Tile"). Protectable only after acquiring **secondary meaning**, meaning consumers have come to associate it with one source. Surnames and geographic terms fall here.
3. **Suggestive**: requires imagination to connect to the goods ("Coppertone"). Protectable immediately.
4. **Arbitrary**: a real word unrelated to the goods ("Apple" for computers). Protectable immediately.
5. **Fanciful**: an invented word ("Kodak"). Strongest.

**Infringement.** The owner of a valid mark can stop another's use of a mark that is likely to cause confusion about source, sponsorship, or affiliation. The Eleventh Circuit weighs seven factors: the strength of the plaintiff's mark; similarity of the marks; similarity of the goods or services; similarity of sales channels and customers; similarity of advertising; the defendant's intent; and actual confusion. *Frehling Enterprises, Inc. v. International Select Group, Inc.*, 192 F.3d 1330 (11th Cir. 1999) (verify citation). The plaintiff bears the burden. Actual confusion is not required but is the best evidence.

**Unfair competition.** Section 43(a) of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1125(a), gives a federal claim for infringement of unregistered marks, false designation of origin, and false advertising. Florida's common law and FDUTPA add parallel claims.

**Federal registration** on the Principal Register (15 U.S.C. § 1051 et seq.) gives: nationwide constructive use and notice as of the filing date; a presumption of validity and ownership; the ® symbol; the ability to record with Customs; and, after five years of continuous use and a filing, **incontestability** (§ 1065), which removes most challenges to validity. Applications can be filed on actual use or on intent to use. The filing fee is per class of goods or services (verify current USPTO fee).

**Florida registration** under ch. 495, Fla. Stat., with the Department of State, is cheap and fast, lasts five years, and gives a presumption of validity within Florida and a state dilution claim (§ 495.151) (verify). It gives no rights outside Florida and no constructive nationwide notice.

**Fictitious name registration** under § 865.09 and an entity name on Sunbiz confer **no trademark rights**. The statute itself says a fictitious name filing does not reserve the name against use by others (verify subsection). They are consumer-notice filings, not ownership.

**Domain names.** Registering a domain is a contract with a registrar, not a trademark. The Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1125(d), and the UDRP arbitration process let a mark owner recover a domain registered in bad faith.

**Remedies.** Injunction, with irreparable harm presumed on a showing of likely success since the Trademark Modernization Act of 2020 (§ 1116(a)); the defendant's profits, the plaintiff's damages, and costs (§ 1117), with willfulness relevant but not required for profits, *Romag Fasteners, Inc. v. Fossil, Inc.*, 590 U.S. 212 (2020); attorney fees in exceptional cases; statutory damages for counterfeiting.

## How courts reason about it

**Where on the spectrum.** The first fight in most cases is classification, because it decides whether the plaintiff has anything to enforce. Courts ask whether the term describes the goods directly or requires a mental leap, and whether competitors need the term to describe their own goods. "Florida Pool Care" is descriptive and competitors need the words. A descriptive plaintiff must prove secondary meaning with evidence of advertising spend, duration of use, sales, and consumer surveys. Registration shifts that burden.

**Likelihood of confusion is a balance, not a count.** Courts weigh the factors, and two usually dominate: the strength of the mark and the similarity of the marks and goods. A strong mark gets a wide berth; a weak descriptive mark gets protection only against near-identical use on the same goods. Marks are compared as a whole, by sight, sound, and meaning, from the view of a consumer who does not see them side by side.

**Priority and territory.** Under common law, a senior user owns the mark only where it has used it or has reputation. A junior user who adopted in good faith in a remote area keeps that area. Federal registration changes this: the registrant has nationwide priority from the filing date against anyone who adopted later, though a prior good-faith local user keeps its existing territory. This is why a Florida business that plans to grow should file federally early.

**Fair use and First Amendment.** A defendant may use a descriptive term descriptively, or use the plaintiff's mark to refer to the plaintiff's goods (nominative use), for comparison or resale. Parody gets some latitude, but *Jack Daniel's Properties, Inc. v. VIP Products LLC*, 599 U.S. 140 (2023), held that a parody used as the defendant's own source identifier gets the ordinary confusion analysis.

**Abandonment.** Rights last as long as use continues. Three years of non-use is prima facie abandonment (§ 1127).

## Florida specifics

Florida follows federal law. The departures are additions, not differences: ch. 495 state registration; § 495.151 dilution for famous marks in Florida; common-law unfair competition; and FDUTPA, which reaches passing off as a deceptive practice. Florida courts and the Eleventh Circuit apply the *Frehling* factors.

Practical Florida points:

- Search Sunbiz for entity and fictitious names, the Florida trademark database, the USPTO database, and the web before adopting a name. An entity name that is "available" on Sunbiz can still infringe a senior common-law user.
- A Florida registration is useful for a purely local business that will never need federal rights, and as evidence of a use date. For anything that sells across state lines or online, federal registration is the standard.

## Angles checklist

- What is the mark, exactly: word, logo, both? For what goods or services?
- Where does it sit on the spectrum? If descriptive, is there evidence of secondary meaning?
- Who used it first, where, and is there proof: dated invoices, ads, website captures?
- Is it registered federally, in Florida, or not at all? Is the registration incontestable?
- Compare the marks by sight, sound, meaning; compare goods, channels, customers; any actual confusion?
- Flip it: descriptive fair use, nominative use, prior use in a territory, abandonment, laches.
- Remedies: injunction, profits, damages, fees in exceptional cases; four-year limitation under Florida law for the state claims and laches for the federal (verify).

## Drill

Two years ago Ana opened "Sunshine Pilates" in Orlando, registered the fictitious name, and built a local following. She never searched or registered. A Jacksonville studio called "Sunshine Pilates Studio" opened three years ago and filed a federal application last year, now registered. Ana just received a cease-and-desist letter. A franchisor also offers her a deal to open "Sunshine Pilates" locations statewide. Spot the issues.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Distinctiveness.** "Sunshine" is suggestive-to-descriptive for a Florida fitness studio, and "Pilates" is generic. A weak mark, but probably protectable.
- **Priority.** Jacksonville used first, three years ago, and now holds a federal registration with nationwide constructive priority from its filing date. Ana's use began before that filing date, so she is a prior good-faith user with a defense limited to her existing Orlando territory under § 1115(b)(5), and she can likely keep operating there. She cannot expand under the name.
- **Likelihood of confusion.** Nearly identical marks, identical services, same state, same online channels. Confusion is likely, so outside Orlando Ana is an infringer.
- **The franchise deal.** Statewide expansion under "Sunshine Pilates" would infringe. The franchise idea needs a new name or a license from Jacksonville.
- **Fictitious name.** Confers nothing. A red herring as to rights.
- **Response to the letter.** Document first-use evidence in Orlando; consider a coexistence agreement defining territories; evaluate a rebrand against the cost of litigation in federal court.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. Does registering "Gulf Coast Plumbing, LLC" on Sunbiz give the LLC trademark rights in "Gulf Coast Plumbing"?
2. A competitor opens under a confusingly similar name in a county where you have never done business or advertised. Can you stop them with only common-law rights?
3. Why file a federal application before you are sure the name will work?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. No. Sunbiz checks only that the exact entity name is not already taken. Rights come from use of a distinctive mark, and "Gulf Coast Plumbing" is descriptive, so even use creates rights only with secondary meaning.
2. Probably not. Common-law rights extend only to the area of use and reputation. A federal registration would give nationwide priority. Without it, the competitor may keep that territory.
3. Because an intent-to-use application reserves nationwide priority as of the filing date, before anyone else can file, and the examination tells you early whether the USPTO sees a conflict or a descriptiveness problem.

</details>
