# Negligence and Florida's duty analysis

**Module:** 04 · **Lesson:** 1 · **Time:** ~50 min
**Tags:** `[US]` `[FL]`

## Why this matters to a business owner

Negligence is the tort you are most likely to face: a customer hurt on your floor, a delivery driver in a crash, a job done carelessly that damages property. The four elements look simple. The work is in duty and proximate cause, where Florida courts decide whether a careless business is on the hook for harm it did not foresee. Since 2023 the rules on comparative fault and the deadline to sue have changed, and both cut against plaintiffs.

## The rule

A plaintiff must prove four elements by a preponderance of the evidence.

1. **Duty.** The defendant owed the plaintiff a legal duty to conform to a standard of care. The general standard is reasonable care under the circumstances. Duty is a question of law for the court.
2. **Breach.** The defendant's conduct fell below that standard. A question of fact.
3. **Causation.** The breach was both the cause in fact of the injury, meaning the harm would not have occurred but for it or it was a substantial factor, and the proximate cause, meaning the harm was a foreseeable result and not too remote. Usually a question of fact.
4. **Damages.** Actual injury to person or property. Negligence without harm is not actionable.

**Where duty comes from.** Florida's Supreme Court lists four sources in *McCain v. Florida Power Corp.*, 593 So. 2d 500 (Fla. 1992): legislative enactments and administrative regulations; judicial interpretations of those; other judicial precedent; and the general facts of the case. The last category is the broad one. Under *McCain*, a defendant whose conduct creates a **foreseeable zone of risk** owes a duty to lessen the risk or protect those within the zone.

**Negligence per se.** Violation of a statute designed to protect a class of persons from a type of harm, where the plaintiff is in the class and suffered that harm, establishes breach without further proof. Violation of a traffic regulation is only evidence of negligence in Florida.

**Res ipsa loquitur.** Where the instrument of harm was in the defendant's exclusive control and the accident is of a kind that ordinarily does not happen without negligence, the jury may infer breach.

**Defenses.** Comparative fault, § 768.81, Fla. Stat., reduces recovery by the plaintiff's share and, for claims accruing on or after March 24, 2023 (verify), bars recovery entirely if the plaintiff is more than 50% (verify) at fault. Assumption of risk is merged into comparative fault except for express written waivers. The limitation period is 2 years (verify) for negligence claims accruing after that date, 4 years (verify) before. § 95.11.

## How courts reason about it

The duty element is where courts control the reach of negligence law. Two New York cases set the framework every first-year lawyer learns.

*MacPherson v. Buick* abolished the privity requirement. A wheel collapsed; the buyer had bought from a dealer, not from Buick. Cardozo held a manufacturer owes a duty of care to foreseeable users of a product that will be dangerous if negligently made, regardless of contract. It opened the door to modern products liability.

*Palsgraf v. Long Island Railroad* drew a limit. A guard pushed a passenger onto a train; a package of fireworks fell and exploded; scales at the far end of the platform fell on Mrs. Palsgraf. Cardozo held the railroad owed her no duty because she was outside the zone of foreseeable danger. Negligence is a wrong to someone, not a wrong in the air. Judge Andrews dissented: everyone owes a duty to the world, and how far liability extends is a proximate cause question decided on practical grounds.

Florida resolved the Cardozo-Andrews tension in *McCain* by splitting the work. Foreseeability enters twice. At the duty stage, the court asks whether the defendant's conduct created a general zone of risk that encompasses people like the plaintiff. This is a legal question, decided broadly, and it is usually satisfied. At the proximate cause stage, the jury asks whether this specific injury was within the scope of danger created, a fact question decided narrowly. In *McCain*, a power company marked the location of an underground cable incorrectly; a trencher operator was electrocuted. The court held the duty question was easy, since a mismarked live cable creates an obvious zone of risk, and the proximate cause question belonged to the jury.

What pushes courts:

- **Affirmative acts versus omissions.** There is no general duty to rescue or to control third parties. Duty to protect others from third-party crime arises from special relationships, such as business and invitee, and from a defendant's own conduct creating the risk.
- **Undertaking.** One who voluntarily undertakes a service that it should recognize is necessary for another's protection assumes a duty to perform it with care. *Clay Electric Cooperative, Inc. v. Johnson*, 873 So. 2d 1182 (Fla. 2003), applied this to a utility that undertook to maintain streetlights.
- **Intervening causes.** A later act, including a third party's crime, breaks the causal chain only if it was not foreseeable. If the risk of the intervening act was part of what made the defendant's conduct negligent, it does not break the chain.

## Florida specifics

Florida follows the majority rule on the elements, negligence per se, and res ipsa loquitur. Its distinct features:

- **Duty analysis** follows *McCain*'s foreseeable zone of risk framework, which tends to find a duty and leave the hard work to proximate cause.
- **Comparative fault.** Florida adopted pure comparative negligence in *Hoffman v. Jones*, 280 So. 2d 431 (Fla. 1973), and moved to a modified system in 2023 under § 768.81(6). The greater-than-50% bar does not apply to medical negligence claims. Lesson 7.
- **No joint and several liability.** Since 2006, § 768.81(3) makes each defendant liable only for its own percentage of fault. Fault can be apportioned to non-parties under *Fabre v. Marin*, 623 So. 2d 1182 (Fla. 1993), if pleaded and proven.
- **Punitive damages** require a reasonable showing before they can be pleaded, and clear and convincing evidence of intentional misconduct or gross negligence. § 768.72. An employer is liable for punitive damages for an employee's conduct only if the employer actively participated, knowingly condoned, or was itself grossly negligent. § 768.72(3).
- **Limitation period.** Two years (verify) for claims accruing on or after March 24, 2023. § 95.11(4)(a) (verify subsection).
- **Medical damages evidence.** Section 768.0427 limits evidence of medical expenses to amounts actually paid or owed under specified benchmarks, not billed amounts. Lesson 7.
- **Vicarious liability** is covered in module 05, lesson 2. Florida's dangerous instrumentality doctrine makes a vehicle owner vicariously liable for a permissive driver's negligence, with statutory caps for some owners under § 324.021(9) (verify).

## Angles checklist

- What did the defendant do or fail to do? Act or omission?
- Did that conduct create a foreseeable zone of risk that included the plaintiff?
- Is there a statute or regulation setting the standard? Negligence per se?
- What is the evidence of breach: industry custom, internal policies, prior incidents?
- Would the harm have happened anyway? Did anything intervene?
- What is the plaintiff's own share of fault? Over 50%?
- Who else shares fault, including non-parties?
- Is the employer liable vicariously, or directly for negligent hiring or supervision?
- When did the claim accrue, and which limitation period applies?
- Flip it: is this really a contract or warranty claim dressed as negligence?

## Drill

A Lakeland landscaping company's employee, driving a company truck to a job, runs a red light and hits a cyclist, who was riding at night without lights and against traffic. The cyclist has $80,000 (verify) in medical bills, of which the insurer paid $30,000. The accident happened in June 2024. Analyze the claims and defenses.

<details>
<summary>Model answer</summary>

- **Duty and breach.** The driver owed every road user a duty of reasonable care. Running a red light is a breach; the traffic violation is evidence of negligence in Florida, not negligence per se, but it is strong evidence.
- **Causation.** But for the red light, no collision. Proximate cause is clear; a cyclist in the intersection is within the zone of risk.
- **Vicarious liability.** The employee was driving a company truck to a job, within the scope of employment. The company is liable under respondeat superior and, as owner of the vehicle, under the dangerous instrumentality doctrine. Module 05.
- **Comparative fault.** The cyclist rode at night without lights and against traffic, both statutory violations. A jury will apportion fault. If the cyclist is found more than 50% at fault, the claim is barred under § 768.81(6), because it accrued after March 24, 2023. If 40%, recovery is reduced by 40%.
- **Damages.** Under § 768.0427, evidence of medical expenses is limited to amounts paid and owed under the statutory benchmarks, not the $80,000 billed. The recoverable medical damages are closer to $30,000 plus any outstanding amounts measured under the statute.
- **Limitation period.** Two years from June 2024. Suit must be filed by June 2026, which has passed as of October 2026 unless suit was filed or the period was tolled.
- **Likely outcome.** Liability on the company is likely. The outcome turns on the fault split and the evidence limits. If the cyclist's fault exceeds 50%, nothing.
- **Facts that would change it.** If the driver was on a personal errand, scope of employment fails, though owner liability for the truck remains. If the accident were before March 24, 2023, pure comparative negligence and a four-year period would apply.

</details>

## Check yourself

1. A store sees a customer collapse and employees do nothing for ten minutes. Negligence?
2. A contractor's work on a roof is careless; two years later, during a hurricane, the roof fails and damages a neighbor's car. Proximate cause?
3. A plaintiff is found 50% at fault for a 2025 accident. Does she recover?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Possibly. There is no general duty to rescue, but a business owes its invitees a duty to render reasonable aid once it knows they are injured or ill on the premises. The duty comes from the relationship, not from causing the harm.
2. Likely yes. Hurricanes are foreseeable in Florida, and a careless roof installation creates a zone of risk that includes neighbors and their property during a storm. The passage of time matters only if an intervening cause, such as a later contractor's work, broke the chain.
3. Yes. The bar applies only when the plaintiff's fault is greater than 50%. At exactly 50%, she recovers 50% of her damages.

</details>
